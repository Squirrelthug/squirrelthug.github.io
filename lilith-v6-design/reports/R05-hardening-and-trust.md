# Report 05 — Hardening and Trust (CH-09, CH-10)

*Covers Chapter CH-09 (Phase 2: Foundation Hardening & Telemetry — §16, §06, §07) and Chapter CH-10 (Phase 3: Security & Key Management Expansion — §02, §03, §14, §08). The experiments are behind us; this is the stretch where Lilith earns the right to be trusted with more of the user's life — by proving how she behaves when things go wrong.*

---

## Where we are in the story

The walking skeleton is validated and in daily use. The natural next move — the one every previous era of this project would have made — is to start adding the fun capabilities: contacts, calendar, local models. The build sequence deliberately refuses, and the reasoning deserves plain statement: **the next chapters after these will hand Lilith real personal data at scale, and she has not yet demonstrated she deserves it.** What happens when she crashes mid-write? Where does telemetry actually live? Can a log file leak what the network boundary protects? What happens to a background task when the vault locks around it? Until those answers are *demonstrated* — not designed, demonstrated — connecting a Google account would be an act of faith. These two chapters convert faith into evidence.

This is also the stretch where the project's oldest philosophical thread becomes physical. Universal Conversation Logging — the idea from the June 2025 blog post that every event in the system is a turn in one auditable conversation — has been living in a humble dev-log file since Milestone 1. CH-09 gives it its real home.

## What we're building, in plain English

**CH-09 builds the nervous system and the crash discipline.** The telemetry database (`ucl_db`) mounts — encrypted like everything else — and every event the system has been dutifully emitting moves from a text log into a queryable, permanent record. A logging sanitizer goes in front of the plaintext logs, enforcing a strict boundary: what's protected at the network boundary must be equally protected from leaking into a debug file. The process lifecycle grows up: a lockfile state table, real crash detection, and a quarantine protocol — a corrupted database is moved aside and the user is asked about restoring from backup; nothing is ever silently deleted. And the CI pipeline arrives: every merge now builds and tests on Linux, macOS, and Windows in parallel, making cross-platform a continuously-verified fact instead of an aspiration.

**CH-10 completes the security promises.** Passphrase rotation — the ability to change the master passphrase and re-key every database without losing a byte — with its relocking wrappers. The remaining half of the PII lifecycle that CH-03 deliberately deferred: artifact tasks now carry their own snapshot of the tag mappings, and if the vault re-locks while a task is in flight, the task is cancelled *fail-closed* — its late response discarded, never completed with identity mappings held past the lock. Fabricated-tag events wire into telemetry. And a merge-blocking CI gate arrives for the PII detector itself: a frozen recall corpus that the detector must score ≥95% on names and 100% on emails and phones, or the merge simply does not happen.

## What we'll be thinking about

**Telemetry is Lilith's self, under construction.** The vision from the introduction report — that Lilith can be introspective and honest because nothing in her system is a blind spot — stops being a vision in CH-09 and starts being a database. Every code path that does something emits an event; the events accumulate into the raw material of self-knowledge. The enhancement layers that will eventually process this record into first-person narrative — *"yesterday I handled forty questions, missed one tool call, and my average answer took 2.1 seconds"* — come later, but they can only ever be as good as the substrate laid down now. So the thinking in CH-09 is archival: name events well (the taxonomy is law), capture the fields a future narrator would need, and treat a gap in coverage as a bug. The standing rule — *every new write path ships with its telemetry event, enforced at review* — is the no-blind-spots principle expressed as a merge criterion.

**The sanitizer closes a hypocrisy.** Milestone 1 built an elaborate border crossing for the network and then wrote freely into a local dev log. That was an accepted, temporary hypocrisy — and CH-09 ends it. The plaintext-logs boundary is the same promise as the PII gate, aimed inward: debugging output, crash dumps, and telemetry payloads must be safe to read over someone's shoulder, safe to attach to a bug report, safe to sync in a backup folder. The engineering mindset is that *every* sink — network, log file, database, screen — has a defined sensitivity contract, and data flows are checked against the contract of their destination.

**Crash behavior is character.** Anyone can be trustworthy on the happy path. The quarantine protocol is where Lilith's character under failure gets defined: detect the crash honestly (and log it as an event), protect what can be protected, move what's damaged aside rather than deleting it, and *ask the user* rather than deciding for them. The clean-VM validation for CH-09 — trigger an artificial crash, watch the recovery behave exactly this way — is a rehearsal of the worst day the user will ever have with Lilith, run deliberately, until the worst day is boring. This is what it means for an assistant to deserve the word *reliable*: not that nothing goes wrong, but that what goes wrong is handled with the user's interests at its core — the founding conviction, applied to failure modes.

**Fail-closed is a value judgment, encoded.** The re-lock cancellation rule in CH-10 embodies a priority ordering that will recur for the rest of the project: *when security and task completion conflict, security wins, always, automatically.* An artifact task holding PII mappings does not get to finish its work after the vault locks — the lock means *locked*, and the task dies with its mappings rather than outliving them. Getting this reflex right matters beyond the specific feature, because the artifact layer is about to grow enormously (overnight local tasks, external data jobs), and every future task inherits the lifecycle semantics we prove here.

**A merge-blocking gate is the 80% principle applied to ourselves.** The PII recall corpus in CI is interesting philosophically: it takes a property we claim ("the detector catches personal data") and converts it into a mechanical, non-negotiable check that runs on every merge forever. No human vigilance required; no gradual erosion possible. This is the same service Lilith renders her user — take a thing that requires ongoing attention and make it automatic — rendered to the codebase itself. Expect this pattern to become a habit: whenever a promise matters, look for the way to make CI hold it.

**CI is the hivemind's passport.** Three platforms building in parallel isn't just packaging hygiene. The Mac-node portal experiments of the v5 era and the multi-endpoint future in the parked list both depend on the codebase being genuinely portable, and the cheapest time to guarantee portability is continuously, from now on, by machine. A red Windows build on a Tuesday is a gift; the same discovery during a release is a crisis.

## Engineering at a high level

CH-09's natural order: `ucl_db` schema and the event writer first (everything else wants to log to it), then migrate the existing dev-log emissions, then the sanitizer (with tests that actively try to leak PII into logs), then lifecycle state and quarantine (validated in a clean VM, per the law), then CI. The CI job will surface a wave of small platform sins — path assumptions, dependency quirks, audio libraries behaving differently in runners — and the sprint should budget patience for that wave rather than treating it as surprise.

CH-10's order: rotation and relocking first (self-contained, testable against populated databases), then the artifact-lifecycle PII surface (snapshot semantics, cancellation wiring, the discard path for late responses), then the recall-corpus gate. The corpus itself is a small authoring project: realistic names, emails, phones embedded in realistic utterances, frozen with the same hash discipline as the CH-08 set. The adversarial tests from CH-03 graduate into permanent CI citizens here.

What we defer without guilt: the enhancement layers that narrate telemetry (they arrive with the app suite's L1/L2 machinery), any user-facing telemetry views, and packaging polish beyond what CI needs.

## What done feels like

The demonstrations that close this stretch are quiet and absolute. A VM crashes mid-write and comes back up apologizing correctly: damaged file quarantined, backup offered, nothing lost silently. A grep of every log file for every name and number in the test corpus comes back empty. A passphrase changes and every database re-keys without a byte lost. A task caught mid-flight by a vault lock dies cleanly, taking its secrets with it. A deliberately-weakened detector tries to merge and CI refuses. Three platforms build green on every push.

None of it demos well. All of it is the difference between an app and an institution. Lilith now fails safely, remembers everything she does, and keeps her promises under pressure — which is exactly the standing required for the next stretch, where she gains local independence and is introduced to the people in your life.
