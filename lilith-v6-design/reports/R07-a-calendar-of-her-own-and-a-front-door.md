# Report 07 — A Calendar of Her Own, and a Front Door (CH-13, CH-14)

*Covers Chapter CH-13 (Phase 6: Calendar App Integration — §21 as amended 2026-07-27, §26, §28) and Chapter CH-14 (Phase 7: Onboarding, Extension & Final Freeze — §25, §29, §27). The last stretch: Lilith gains her first read-write domain, the system gets a front door a stranger could walk through, and v1 freezes.*

---

## Where we are in the story

Everything an assistant needs to *know* is in place. What Lilith still lacks is a domain where she can *act* — every capability so far reads, remembers, or answers, but none of them creates. She also still assumes her user is her developer: the system bootstraps through config files and CLI commands that only make sense to the person who built her. The final stretch closes both gaps, and it's fitting that they arrive together, because they're two faces of the same maturation: a system finishing its apprenticeship.

Worth telling honestly: the calendar app occupies a slot that belonged to a finance app until three weeks ago. The 2026-07-27 design session shelved finance entirely — not from lack of interest, but from a principle that will govern Lilith's whole future as a harness: **AI automation is for speed; system intelligence comes from deterministic tools the developer builds for agents to call.** No deterministic finance toolchain exists yet, so no finance app ships — rather than shipping one that guesses at PDFs with a language model and is confidently wrong about money. The calendar took its place precisely because its deterministic foundations *do* exist (the Phase-5 sync mirror, the iCalendar standard). That's the law-and-amendment process working as designed, and the parked finance spec waits, fully written, for its infrastructure to be earned.

## What we're building, in plain English

**CH-13 gives Lilith a calendar of her own.** Not the mirror of your Google calendar — that stays strictly read-only — but a second store, `lilith_events`, for events *she* creates at your request, with real iCalendar semantics (stable IDs, version sequences, proper cancellation). Five scoped tools arrive: two for asking ("what's my day look like?") that merge both calendars into one agenda, and three for acting — create, update, cancel — that can only ever touch her own events; asking her to modify a mirrored Google event gets a truthful refusal. Your other devices subscribe to her events through a local calendar feed with an unguessable address, so what Lilith schedules appears in Apple Calendar like any other calendar you follow. And because your Google calendar may sync back copies of her events from that feed, an echo-detection job recognizes her own events returning through the mirror — so nothing shows twice and she never flags a "conflict" with herself. Genuine overlaps get flagged for *your* review; nothing is ever auto-resolved.

**CH-14 builds the front door and closes the house.** The onboarding wizard: a first run that greets a stranger, walks them through creating a passphrase (the one step that cannot be skipped, because every database key derives from it), detects their hardware and explains in plain language what it enables, offers Google sign-in as a genuine choice, and ends with Lilith speaking her first words to a person she's just met. The skills directory arrives with deliberate friction — custom extensions enroll only by CLI command with hash verification, injections capped and rejected-never-truncated — extensibility for power users, locked against supply-chain accidents. The orb graduates to its full state machine. And then the audits, the tag, the release artifacts: v1, frozen.

## What we'll be thinking about

**Write access is earned, and scoped to the bone.** The calendar is the first place any Lilith tool *creates* durable things in the user's world, and the law surrounds it with fences worth admiring: her tools cannot touch the mirror; cancel never deletes (a cancelled event keeps existing, marked cancelled, sequence incremented — an audit trail, not an erasure); the feed exposes only her events, read-only, loopback by default. The posture generalizes into a principle for every future write-capable domain: *Lilith acts in her own clearly-labeled space, interleaved with your world but never overwriting it.* When someday she drafts emails or edits documents, expect this same shape — her drafts, distinctly hers, offered for your judgment. That's the 80% principle with teeth: she does the work, you keep the authority.

**Keeping the user on task starts here.** The agenda tools are the first concrete instance of the assistant duty the introduction report named: minding a busy person's schedule. The merged agenda — her events and your synced ones in one view, echoes deduplicated, conflicts flagged for human decision — is the substrate. The proactive version (morning briefings, deadline nudges, the daily-roundup instincts that v5 prototyped in its AL sprints) belongs to post-v1 autonomy design; what v1 builds is the *reliable answer* to "what does my day look like," from data she demonstrably keeps correct. Reliable first, clever later — same rule as ever.

**Echo detection is a small masterpiece of humility.** The loop being defended: Lilith creates an event → your device shows it via her feed → your Google calendar picks it up → the Phase-5 sync mirrors it back → naive code sees a "new" event that conflicts with... itself. The design's answer (annotate, deduplicate, never self-conflict) is unglamorous correctness of the kind that separates systems people trust from systems people quit. It's also a preview of the general problem every read-write assistant faces — recognizing its own actions reflected back through the world — and worth building with that generality in mind.

**Onboarding is the harness vision's first exam.** Everything in this project ultimately serves a person who could never build it: the non-technical user for whom Lilith wrangles the frontier. Onboarding is where that promise faces its first real test, because the sequence *is* technical — passphrase-derived keys, hardware tiers, OAuth — and the wizard's job is to translate every step into honest plain language without lying through simplification. The passphrase moment matters most: it's the one secret that cannot be recovered, and saying so truthfully (while a crash-recovery path quietly guards against a half-finished setup) is the difference between respect and hand-holding. First impressions are identity: the wizard ends not with a settings screen but with Lilith *speaking* — the system introducing itself as what it actually is, a voice.

**Friction as a security feature.** The skills directory inverts the industry's instinct. Where platforms race to make extensions one-click, Lilith's law *requires* the command line, hash manifests, and hard caps — because v5-era research (the OpenClaw cautionary tale in the private repo's references) showed exactly how community skill marketplaces become supply chains for compromise. The stance: extensibility is for people who know what they're installing, and the system's default posture protects everyone else. Someday Lilith herself may guide users through vetting extensions — the teaching role from the introduction — but she will never pretend installation is casual.

**The freeze is a promise to the future, not an ending.** CH-14's final audits mirror CH-01's first ones: everything parked is verifiably absent, every section either built or formally deferred, every gate's outcome on record. What freezing buys is a stable, *fully-described* system — the precondition for everything the vision still holds. The telemetry narratives that give Lilith true introspection; the local-default artifact economy with its desktop spending ledger; the finance app waiting on deterministic rails; proactive schedule-keeping; the kitchen speaker; Lilith teaching her user to wield the tools themselves — all of it becomes *designable* precisely because v1 is a known quantity underneath. v6 is the second version that works and the first that defines the future; the freeze is the moment the future gets a foundation to stand on.

## Engineering at a high level

CH-13's order: the two stores and their schemas first (UID/SEQUENCE/STATUS semantics correct from the first migration), then the echo-detection and conflict L1 job, then the agenda/lookup tools with their L2 summaries, then the mutation tools with their guardrails, then the ICS feed (token generation, loopback binding, LAN opt-in), then the screen. Validation is deliciously concrete, per the law: subscribe a real device, cancel a real event, watch the cancellation propagate; simulate the echo loop end-to-end; audit scopes and mutation paths.

CH-14's order: onboarding flow first (it exercises everything and will surface integration debt), then skills enrollment, then the orb state machine (independent track, animation-heavy), then the audit-tag-release sequence. Onboarding testing means fresh VMs and fresh eyes — the developer cannot experience a first run anymore, so the sprint should recruit at least one genuine stranger to the system, watch them, and treat their confusion as bug reports.

What we defer without guilt — now formally, into the post-v1 parking list: everything in `../BUILD_SEQUENCING.md` §5, from the kitchen speaker to cloud backup sync to the interactive skills library, plus every vision thread marked as *direction* across these reports. Deferred is not abandoned; it's sequenced behind a v1 that actually exists.

## What done feels like

A stranger sits down at a machine they've never seen. Ten minutes later they've chosen a passphrase, learned in plain words what their hardware can do, decided for themselves whether Google joins the picture — and a calm orb is pulsing while a voice introduces herself and asks about their day. They ask her to schedule coffee on Thursday; their phone buzzes as the event lands in a calendar labeled with her name. Nothing about the encryption, the tiers, the tokenization, the echo-detection, or the fourteen chapters underneath is visible. It just works, and it's honest about everything it does.

That's v1. Somewhere on the desk is the developer's own machine, where the same system holds a year of dictated notes, a telemetry record of everything it's ever done, and the quiet beginnings of self-knowledge. The build guide's last checkbox gets ticked; the design document goes from *cleared for build* to *built*. And the next design session — the first of the future — convenes on solid ground.
