# Report 06 — Independence and First Real Data (CH-11, CH-12)

*Covers Chapter CH-11 (Phase 4: Local Intelligence & Fallbacks — §12, §05) and Chapter CH-12 (Phase 5: The Custodian App Suite — §19, §20, §28). Two thresholds get crossed in this stretch: Lilith gains a mind of her own that runs entirely on your hardware, and she meets the actual people and events of your life.*

---

## Where we are in the story

Lilith is hardened, telemetered, and trustworthy under failure — and still, in two important senses, dependent and ignorant. Dependent, because every thought she has runs through a cloud API key; unplug the internet or empty the account and she goes quiet. Ignorant, because for all her memory of your conversations, she knows nothing of your world — no contacts, no calendar, nothing that a real assistant would consider the basic furniture of the job. This stretch fixes both, in the order that respects the founding convictions: independence first, then intimacy. Prove she can think privately before handing her the address book.

## What we're building, in plain English

**CH-11 gives her a local mind.** An Ollama backend joins the Anthropic adapter behind the same provider interface: on capable hardware (the tiers detected way back in CH-02), Lilith can run a local model — installed automatically, GPU-detected, warmed up before use — and answer without a single byte leaving the machine. A system-tray indicator tells the truth about which mind is active at any moment. The validation is blunt and wonderful: take a machine with *no API key at all*, and watch Lilith set up her own local intelligence and answer questions with it.

**CH-12 introduces her to your world.** Google sign-in through the proper front door — the system browser, OAuth, tokens straight into the OS keychain — with *read-only* scopes: Lilith looks, never touches. Contacts sync into their own encrypted database (with CSV and vCard import for everything that isn't Google), and a mirror of your calendar syncs alongside. Two new tools join `recall_memory` in the catalog — `people_search` and `person_detail` — and the context manager learns the entity trick: say a friend's name in passing and their profile quietly enters Lilith's awareness for that turn. The chat screen learns deep-linking, so a spoken answer can navigate the UI to the thing it's talking about and point at it.

## What we'll be thinking about

**Local-first is a philosophy arriving on schedule.** The introduction report laid out the direction: local execution as the default posture, cloud as something you deliberately choose, with costs always visible. CH-11 builds the load-bearing half of that vision — the *capability* for local thought, behind the same interface as cloud thought, selected honestly by hardware tier. It matters to be precise about what's law now versus direction for later: in v1's law, local models are the fallback and Anthropic BYOK is primary; the fuller posture — long artifacts defaulting to patient local processing (overnight is fine, and most artifacts will likely live here), cloud reserved for what you explicitly push to it, and a running spending ledger in a plain document on your desktop so proposed cloud work always shows its dollar cost before you approve it — is post-v1 design-session material. But *everything* that future needs is being laid down in this stretch: the provider abstraction that makes local and cloud interchangeable, the tier detection that knows what your hardware can bear, the telemetry that already records every cloud call and could price them, and the artifact lane built for work that takes as long as it takes. When that design session happens, it will find the machinery waiting.

**The tray indicator is a tiny feature carrying a large principle.** Which mind is thinking right now — the one in your house or the one in a datacenter — is exactly the kind of fact most products blur and Lilith must not. It's the honesty thread again: the orb shows what she's doing; the tray shows *where she's thinking*; and the future spending document shows what it costs. A user who always knows these three things is a user in control, which was the founding point of the entire project.

**Warmup is a user-experience problem wearing an infrastructure costume.** Local models have a cold-start penalty measured in tens of seconds, and the design's answer is choreography, not denial: warm the model deliberately, never route a conversational turn to a cold model, and let the narrative layer cover the gaps honestly. The deeper pattern being established: the conversational lane's feel is sacred, and every new capability — local inference now, sync jobs in the same stretch, heavy artifacts later — must find a way to exist without ever making the user wait on it unknowingly.

**Read-only is a promise you can audit.** The decision that v1 Lilith does not write back to Google is written into the law, and CH-12's definition of done includes auditing that no write scope is even *requested*. Think about what that buys: the user's calendar and contacts cannot be corrupted by any bug in this entire codebase, categorically, because the credentials themselves lack the power. This is the security posture of CH-10 extended to the outside world — fail-closed by construction — and it's also good assistant manners: a new hire earns write access; they don't start with it.

**Sync is an introduction, not an interrogation.** The custodian pattern — Lilith as caretaker of mirrors, with clear provenance for every record — matters more than the plumbing. Everything synced lands in its own encrypted database, tagged by source; imports (CSV, vCard) go through the same shaped pipeline; incremental sync tokens and etags keep it polite and resumable; and the L1/L2 enhancement machinery arriving with this stretch turns raw rows into the narrative profiles that actually enter Lilith's context. The rule inherited from the design — *the LLM reads only enhanced summaries, never raw rows* — is both a privacy measure and a quality measure: what enters her mind about a person is a composed, current profile, not a database dump.

**Entity context is the 80% principle becoming ambient.** The quiet magic of this stretch: you mention "Sarah" while asking something else entirely, and Lilith already knows which Sarah, when you last spoke, what's coming up — because the context manager detected the name and injected the profile before the model ever saw the sentence. No lookup command, no interface, no asking. That's the assistant ideal from the introduction — do the legwork before the client knows there was legwork — implemented as a context-assembly rule. It's also the pattern that scales: every future data domain (calendar next chapter, whatever comes after) plugs into the same detect-and-inject shape.

**Two tools become four, and the catalog becomes a pattern.** `people_search` and `person_detail` follow the YAML-plus-function shape that `recall_memory` established, with their own narratives and telemetry. This is the moment the tool system stops being "the thing that runs recall" and becomes a genuine catalog — and the moment to hold the line on artifact-lane discipline: data tools are `artifact_only`, they run in the background lane with acknowledge and handoff, and the conversational lane stays light. The deep-link work (§28) closes the loop by letting handoffs *point*: "here's Sarah's profile" can navigate the screen there and highlight it.

## Engineering at a high level

CH-11 is adapter work plus operational choreography: implement the Ollama provider against the existing interface (streaming included), then the installer/detector/warmup path, then tray status, then tier-based selection logic. The seams to watch: model download failures on bad networks, GPU detection lying on edge hardware, and the handoff between providers mid-session. Validation needs a genuinely keyless machine, per the law.

CH-12 has a strict internal order: OAuth and token storage first (everything depends on it), then contacts sync and its schema, then importers, then the L1/L2 profile pipeline, then the tools, then entity injection, then calendar mirror sync, then deep-linking. Sync engineering rewards pessimism — interrupted syncs, revoked tokens, rate limits, weird vCards — and the CH-09 telemetry makes every sync job legible (`sync.*` events) so dogfooding can see what the plumbing is doing. The privacy surface grows here too: synced names and numbers are exactly what the PII detector protects at the network boundary, so the recall corpus from CH-10 should grow test cases drawn from realistic contact-shaped data.

What we defer without guilt: any Lilith-owned calendar events (next stretch), any write-back ambitions (post-v1 at the earliest), manual contact entry UI (settings-screen scope, later), and the spending ledger (its design session comes when the artifact economy exists to meter).

## What done feels like

Two scenes close this stretch. First: a laptop with no API key anywhere on it asks Lilith a question, and she answers — having noticed her hardware, fetched her own model, and warmed it up — with the tray icon truthfully reporting that this thought happened at home. Second: you mention a friend by name in a passing question, and the answer comes back already knowing who they are — and when you ask to see them, the screen glides to their profile by itself.

Independence and intimacy, in that order, both under the user's control and both fully audited. What's left is Lilith's own domain — a calendar she keeps rather than mirrors — and the front door that welcomes a stranger into all of this. That's the final report.
