1. The answer in one paragraph

The 2024-26 landscape tells Lilith three things.

(a) Everyone is building whole-life, memory-bearing assistants, but the winners are platform owners (Apple, Amazon, OpenAI, Meta). The standalone memory and gadget startups were bought or died within about 12-18 months (Humane, Limitless/Rewind, Bee), and the largest platform (Apple) has repeatedly slipped on its own "personal context Siri". The hard part is reliable use of personal data, not the model.

(b) The decisive risk in 2025-26 is not a database breach in the classic sense. It is that an agent that holds private data, reads untrusted content, and can act or send data out is structurally exploitable (prompt injection, memory poisoning, malicious skills), and the vendor record on this (OpenClaw, ChatGPT memory, Recall) is poor. A single encrypted local database is a defensible store but concentrates value, and encryption at rest does not protect against the assistant itself being steered.

(c) Regulation is mostly manageable for a local-first single-user tool. GDPR's household exemption plausibly covers the user's own contacts, and obligations start with the developer's own processing. But finance, health data and the "tokenise, then send to the cloud" pattern each carry legal subtleties that are unresolved, jurisdiction-dependent and, for US financial data, in active flux. Local-first is a real differentiator, but it is limited by local model capability, by hardware, and by integration access, which is now gated by platform OAuth and security-review regimes and, for banks, by an undecided rights framework.

2. Findings

2.1 Products and outcomes

F1. Humane AI Pin: dead less than a year after launch. HP bought Humane's assets (CosmOS, 300+ patents, most staff; not the Pin business) for $116M. Pin servers stopped on 28 Feb 2025; refunds were offered only within the 90-day window; the price had been cut from $699 to $499. Reviewers were harsh (a widely cited review called it the "worst product I've ever reviewed"). [evidence from multiple press reports; no formal post-mortem found; confidence high on facts, medium on causes] Lesson: a cloud-dependent device bricks when the company does; users who paid for it lost their data path. (E1) paraphrase: not found in the cited source

F2. Rabbit R1. Press and a YouTube investigation (Coffeezilla) alleged the "Large Action Model" was largely scripted web automation (Playwright) that breaks when sites change. The Rabbitude group reported on 16 May 2024 hardcoded API keys (ElevenLabs, Azure STT, Yelp, SendGrid) in the codebase that could expose user responses; Rabbit revoked or rotated keys, and one revocation briefly bricked devices. [practice; press only; the company's account blaming a former employee is disputed; confidence medium] Lesson: brittle per-site automation sold as an "agent", and secrets in client code. (E2)

F3. Limitless (formerly Rewind) bought by Meta, 5 Dec 2025. Rewind recording features were disabled on 19 Dec 2025; pendant sales to new customers ended; export and delete tools were promised; one source says service ended in the EU and UK (single source, unverified). Amazon bought Bee (a wearable memory device) in July 2025. [practice; press; medium-high] Lesson: "memory of your life" startups are acquisition targets, and the user's life record then falls under a different company's policy. (E3)

F4. Microsoft Recall. The 2024 preview stored OCR text in an unencrypted SQLite database (Kevin Beaumont; a "TotalRecall" extraction tool by Alex Hagenah). Microsoft then made it opt-in, with TPM-bound encryption, Windows Hello gating, VBS enclave isolation, session time-outs and rate-limiting, and default filtering of passwords, ID and card numbers. Microsoft's own page states: "Snapshots and any associated information in the vector database are always encrypted." and "Recall snapshots are available only after you authenticate using Windows Hello credentials." [evidence: vendor page read (E4) plus press (E5); no independent audit of the final design found; confidence high on what Microsoft claims, low on how well it holds] This is the closest public precedent for "one local database of someone's whole digital life", and it shows how fast such a store becomes a headline and a target. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

F5. Apple's personalised Siri slipped repeatedly. Promised for March 2025, then delayed. Apple and Google announced a Gemini partnership on 12 Jan 2026. Bloomberg reported in Feb 2026 that the personal-data features would slip again (from iOS 26.4 to 26.5 or 27); Apple disputed the delay; after 26.4 shipped, the features were absent. Status as of Nov 2026 could not be confirmed. [practice, press only; confidence medium] Lesson: even with on-device models, a private cloud and full OS integration, "uses your personal context across apps" proved hard to ship reliably. (E6) paraphrase: not found in the cited source

F6. Alexa+. Generally available in the US on 4 Feb 2026 after a soft launch. Reviewers praised the conversation but reported latency (up to 15 s in one summary), smart-home regressions, hallucination and forced upgrades. [practice; aggregator quality varies; low-medium] Wave 1 already documents the voice-assistant expectation gap (Luger and Sellen 2016); the 2026 pattern repeats it: generative fluency raises expectations while action reliability lags. (E7)

F7. ChatGPT memory. User complaints in OpenAI's own forum and on Reddit: wrong or garbled stored memories (names, jobs), invented "remembered" context, memory wipes (a Feb 2025 wipe reported only by a blog; a 6-7 Nov 2025 wipe said to be acknowledged on the status page), and no pruning of stale entries. [practice, anecdotal forum evidence; low-medium] A search summary cites OpenAI's April 2025 sycophancy post-mortem and a "2026 MIT study" linking memory to more agreement with wrong users; the study could not be verified and is treated as unconfirmed. (E8) Wave 1 06-pitfalls.md covers wrong-memory harms in more depth.

F8. Open-source local-first voice and agents. Mycroft AI ceased in early 2023 (sources disagree on why: a patent suit or funding). OpenVoiceOS and Neon continue as community successors, with a foundation formalised in 2025. Home Assistant runs a deliberately local-first pipeline: it handles known commands with a deterministic parser first and sends only unparseable requests to an LLM. It concedes "local AI models can be slow", that recent local models with 8GB+ VRAM are "nearly keeping up", and it tested on an RTX 3090. Community reports say CPU-only boxes hang with local LLMs and small models make more mistakes; reviewers called the voice hardware unsuitable for non-technical users. [practice/evidence mix; medium] (E9, E10)

F9. OpenClaw (open-source, self-hosted personal agent), 2026: the biggest personal-agent security story of the year. From secondary reports: 21,639 exposed instances (Censys, 31 Jan 2026), 30,000+ (SecurityScorecard) and an unverified 135,000; CVE-2026-25253 (reported CVSS 8.8, fixed in 2026.2.26); CVE-2026-22708 (web content not sanitised before reaching the model); Snyk reporting 283 marketplace skills leaking API keys; a third-party backend exposing 1.5M API tokens; and a June 2026 report of compromise through message injection and social engineering. Counts vary widely and come from vendor blogs, not primary disclosures. [practice; confidence high that serious exposure occurred, low on any specific number] Lesson: "self-hosted, local" does not mean safe. Misconfiguration, open ports, skill marketplaces and untrusted inputs are the real failure modes. (E11)

2.2 Security risk of a concentrated store

F10. The lethal trifecta. Willison (2025): the dangerous combination is "Access to your private data", "Exposure to untrusted content", and "The ability to externally communicate". His advice: users should "avoid that lethal trifecta combination entirely"; developers must ensure that "once an LLM agent has ingested untrusted input, it must be constrained so that it is impossible for that input to trigger any consequential actions." He adds: "we still don't know how to 100% reliably prevent this from happening." [practice, authoritative practitioner; read; confidence high that this framing is the field's working model] (E12) Lilith has the first leg by definition (the whole-life database). Email, web, contact-card and message content supply the second. Any outbound tool (email, a cloud model call, a web request) supplies the third.

F11. Memory poisoning has been demonstrated. Rehberger (2024): ChatGPT's memory could be written through indirect prompt injection (a Google Doc, an image, browsing), producing fake memories, biased answers, or deletion of all memories. OpenAI closed the report as a "Model Safety Issue"; mitigations were partial. Later follow-ups (Atlas CSRF "Tainted Memories"; Jan 2026 "ZombieAgent") were seen in search only. [evidence for the primary post (read); follow-ups search only; confidence high for the principle] (E13) For Lilith, her own notes and memories are an attack surface: anything the assistant may write to the database from untrusted content (a forwarded message, a calendar invite from a stranger) can persist and steer later behaviour.

F12. Single point of failure, honestly. No study was found that measures the risk of "one database holding finance, relationships and schedules" for personal assistants specifically. [hypothesis, confidence medium] - The concentration risk is real but distinct from per-app breach risk. With separate apps, one compromise leaks one domain. With a unified store, one key or one steerable agent exposes everything, and also enables cross-domain inference (location pattern + salary + relationships). - Mitigations: per-domain scoping (separate keys or tables, unlock per domain); minimum necessary context to the model; no autonomous outbound channel from sessions that touched untrusted content. - Recall's redesign (hardware-bound keys, authentication per access, time-outs, rate limits, enclave isolation) is the best public template, but it is OS-level. A cross-platform app (Linux, macOS, Windows) cannot assume a TPM and enclave on every machine. [hypothesis]

F13. The local compromise model. Encryption at rest (SQLCipher) protects a stolen disk or backup. It does not protect against malware running as the user, a decrypted session, or the assistant itself. Recall's plaintext episode was exactly "database readable by any process running as the user". [evidence from the Recall record; the reasoning is the track author's]

F14. Vendor cloud designs: Apple Private Cloud Compute states five requirements: stateless processing ("no trace in the PCC system"), enforceable guarantees, no privileged runtime access (no remote shell), non-targetability, and verifiable transparency (published builds). [evidence: vendor page read via condensed fetch; the claims are the vendor's; independent verification not checked; medium] (E14) It is the main example of a cloud model path with hardware-attested statelessness. A small developer cannot replicate it, so Lilith's cloud path will rely on contract and policy plus minimisation (tokenisation) rather than attestation. [hypothesis]

F15. Tokenise-before-cloud (Lilith's plan) is minimisation, not anonymisation. EDPB Opinion 28/2024 (secondary summaries only; the PDF would not open) treats the anonymity of AI models as a case-by-case question, not presumed, and allows legitimate interest for AI development and deployment only after a three-step test (interest, necessity, balancing). That opinion is about models trained on personal data, not about prompts, so it touches Lilith only indirectly. What matters for her: pseudonymised data is still personal data under GDPR, and context in a prompt (a rare event, a specific amount, a distinctive relationship description) can re-identify someone even with names replaced. [practice/legal commentary; confidence medium; hypothesis for the re-identification point] (E15)

2.3 Regulation, consent and third-party data

F16. The GDPR household exemption. Article 2(2)(c): the Regulation does not apply to processing "by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity" (read at gdpr-info.eu; the recital 18 text did not load). - Commentary (search only) says the CJEU reads the exemption narrowly (Lindqvist: publishing colleagues' data on a website; Rynes: home CCTV covering public space) and that it protects the individual user, not the platform provider. - The closest analogue to Lilith's contacts is the Belgian DPA's Twoo decision (contact-sync invitations), where the service, not just the user, was treated as controller for the processing it performed on synced contacts (search summary only; decision not opened). - [practice; confidence medium] Conclusion: a user keeping their own address book in their own local assistant is very likely personal or household processing. The developer becomes a controller if Lilith's operator receives, matches, shares or telemetry-logs contact data. - The CLAUDE.md dev-time telemetry, "readable only by her and the developer", needs checking: if contact content reaches the developer's telemetry, that processing is not household. [hypothesis; flag for the design session] (E16)

F17. GDPR special categories. Health data, and data revealing for example sexual life, religious belief or union membership, get stricter rules. A life database will include these as free-text notes about oneself and others (a friend's diagnosis, a relative's care needs). Under the household exemption the user's own notes remain exempt; any handling by the developer, a vendor or a cloud processor raises the bar. Article 9 was not fetched; this is general knowledge. [practice; medium; cite the regulation text in the design session]

F18. Health data outside HIPAA (US). The FTC's 2024 amendments to the Health Breach Notification Rule extend it to health apps and connected devices outside HIPAA, and define a breach to include unauthorised disclosures, not only intrusions; effective July 2024 (search summaries of the Federal Register notice; the notice itself would not open; one source gave a different effective date). [evidence for existence, medium on details] (E17) Implication: if Lilith stores health details (medication, appointments, symptoms) and a cloud model call or telemetry sends them out, that could count as a breach disclosure for a covered vendor. Whether a purely local tool by an individual developer is a "vendor of personal health records" is untested. [hypothesis]

F19. US financial data rights are unsettled. The CFPB's Section 1033 rule: in May 2025 the agency told a court it considered the rule unlawful; in July 2025 it began reconsideration; an ANPR followed on 22 Aug 2025; on 29 Oct 2025 an E.D. Ky. court enjoined enforcement pending reconsideration. Later sources (to mid-2026) show it still enjoined and under reconsideration, with fees for aggregators under review. JPMorgan began charging aggregators, and Plaid agreed to pay in Sept 2025. Status after mid-2026 could not be confirmed. [evidence from law-firm summaries and a tracker; search only; medium] (E18) Implication: bank-feed access for a hobbyist or solo developer is a negotiated commercial relationship through aggregators (Plaid pricing is unpublished and negotiated), not a user right to rely on. In the EU and UK, PSD2 and open-banking access requires regulated status or a regulated partner (general knowledge; not sourced here; confirm).

F20. Platform integrations are gated. Google states that restricted scopes "can't be used in production apps without review", require verification before use in an authorisation request, and "might include third-party security assessments" (read). Gmail's scopes are in the restricted class (general knowledge; the page did not name Gmail). [evidence for gating; the Gmail point is from memory, confirm] (E19) Implication: a local app that reads a user's Gmail or Calendar can run in test mode for the developer's own use, but distributing it widely means verification and possibly a paid security assessment. Alternatives are IMAP or CalDAV with app passwords, local files and ICS export, and OS APIs (EventKit, Windows/Outlook), each with its own limits. [hypothesis; options]

F21. EU AI Act. Not researched directly; thin ground. A general-purpose personal assistant is unlikely to be "high-risk" unless used in listed areas (employment, credit scoring, essential services). Transparency duties (telling people they are talking to an AI) and GPAI obligations fall mainly on model providers. Credit scoring is the finance trap: if a harness evaluates other people's creditworthiness, it changes class. [hypothesis from general knowledge; low confidence; needs a proper read]

2.4 Local-first and on-device

F22. Ink & Switch's local-first essay lists seven ideals (fast, multi-device, offline, collaboration, longevity, security and privacy, user control) and states its own costs: collaboration is "one of the biggest challenges", and "With data ownership comes responsibility: maintaining backups or other preventative measures against data loss." [evidence/essay, read; high] (E20) The last point matters: with a single local encrypted database the user, not a vendor, is responsible for backups, and losing the key or the disk loses the whole life record.

F23. On-device models are small. Apple's Foundation Models framework (WWDC June 2025) exposes a roughly 3B-parameter on-device model with tool calling and structured output, free to developers; commenters questioned its capability (TechCrunch and MacRumors via search; Apple docs not opened) (E22). Home Assistant's data (F8) shows local LLMs need about 8GB+ VRAM for decent quality and are slower. [practice; medium] Implication: "local model where the machine allows" implies a capability tier by hardware. Plumbers' laptops and old PCs will often be below the line, so the cloud-with-tokenisation path is the default for most real users, not the exception. [hypothesis, medium-high]

2.5 What users say (thin)

F24. Pew (Feb 2026 survey of 5,119 US adults, via news summaries only; the report itself not opened): about 49% had used AI chatbots, up from roughly a third in 2024; 71% expect AI to make personal information less secure. [evidence via secondary; medium] (E21) Usage and distrust coexist, so privacy is a selling point but not a gate on adoption: people use tools they do not trust. [hypothesis]

F25. No good 2025-26 survey was found on which integrations users of whole-life assistants actually need. The evidence is indirect: complaints about Alexa+ concentrate on smart-home reliability; wave 1 points to reminders, timers and messages as the persistent voice uses; and Apple's delayed features are "personal data + in-app actions". Thin ground. Treat integration priority as a hypothesis to test with real users: calendar, contacts, email, reminders and tasks, notes, bank statements (by CSV or PDF import before live feeds), and files. [hypothesis; low-medium]

3. What the developer did not ask

  1. Is the user's own contact data GDPR-regulated for me? Probably not for the user's own household use (Art. 2(2)(c)), but the exemption does not stretch to the operator. Any contact data that reaches a developer-run server, telemetry, a crash report or a cloud processor makes the operator a controller or processor for it (F16; Belgian DPA Twoo precedent; low-medium confidence; take legal advice before shipping to others). Also, tokenising names does not make the data non-personal.
  2. What happens when someone else's data is in a prompt to the cloud model? The user's disclosure of a friend's health or finances to a model provider can become a non-household disclosure through the provider's processing. Treat third-party special-category data as "never leaves the device unless the user explicitly chooses" (design option; F17).
  3. Does encryption at rest solve the single-point-of-failure problem? No. It covers disk theft. The live risks are malware running as the user, the assistant being steered by untrusted content (F10, F11), and key loss (F22). Recall shows what happens when "local" means "readable by anything on the machine" (F4).
  4. Is local-first safe against agent attacks? No. OpenClaw was self-hosted and local, and tens of thousands of instances were exposed (F9). Local-first changes who holds the data, not whether the agent can be tricked.
  5. What if Lilith (or I) disappear? Humane bricked, Rewind was shut down, Mycroft folded (F1, F3, F8). A local database with an open export format and no required cloud service is the only structural answer. An encrypted database readable only by one app is itself a lock-in the user should be able to leave (export). [hypothesis from the record]
  6. Who is the developer, legally, when he ships this to a plumber? A software supplier that processes nothing is one thing. A supplier with telemetry, update servers, a cloud relay or a model-provider account is a controller or processor and may owe breach notification (F16, F18). The CLAUDE.md telemetry the developer reads is dev-time; end-user builds must not send content-bearing telemetry without consent.
  7. Can the bank or finance integration be had at all? Not as a right in the US at present (F19); in practice only through aggregators with commercial terms, or through user-provided statements and CSV files. A "budget" capability should be designed to work with manual import first. [hypothesis]
  8. Does per-domain consent make sense to users? Unstudied here. Consent fatigue is real; per-domain scoping is cheap to build and expensive to explain. [hypothesis] See DQ3.
  9. What happens to the data of a user who dies or loses capacity? Whole-life stores make this a live issue (estate access, power of attorney). No source in the search addressed it, which is itself a finding: thin ground, and an unmet need page 01 may inform.
  10. Does a harness change the security surface? Yes. Third-party harness and skill marketplaces were the vector in OpenClaw (F9). A "nexus" of downloadable harnesses brings supply-chain risk (signing, review, permission manifests). Options in section 4.

4. Design-session questions

DQ1. How do untrusted inputs touch the database? Options: (a) read-only for content of untrusted origin, with writes needing user confirmation; (b) writes allowed but tagged by provenance and quarantined from instructions; (c) free writes. Trade-off: (a) is safest but taxes the conversational flow and conflicts mildly with proactive value; (c) conflicts with the privacy constraint in practice (F11).

DQ2. Break the trifecta by construction? Options: no outbound action in any turn that has ingested untrusted content; outbound only through a user-confirmed, narrowly typed tool; or accept the risk with logging. Trade-off: constrains the convenience of "send this message for me". Matches the narrow-tools constraint well.

DQ3. Per-domain scoping. Options: one key with per-domain permissions in software; per-domain keys unlocked when first needed; separate tables never joined without user opt-in. Trade-off: stronger separation against the cross-domain synthesis that is the product thesis. A possible middle path: synthesis happens locally, and only the minimal derived fact goes to a cloud model. Flag: tension between the thesis and scoping.

DQ4. Cloud path policy. Options: tokenise and send (current); tokenise and also drop high-sensitivity classes (health, third-party special-category data) from cloud prompts entirely; or require a local model for those classes. Trade-off: re-identification risk remains with tokenisation (F15); local-only classes mean degraded answers on low-end hardware (F23). Flag: a legal-basis review for cloud processors is needed.

DQ5. Hardware tiers. Option: define tiers (cloud-only; small local model for routing and extraction; larger local model for everything) and tell the user honestly which tier they have. Trade-off: complexity against an honest "can't do that here" (aligned with the honesty constraint). Home Assistant's "deterministic parse first, LLM only if needed" is a useful precedent (F8).

DQ6. Key and backup model. Options: user passphrase only; OS keystore; hardware-bound where available. Trade-off: usability for users with low digital literacy (the plumber) against recoverability; a lost key loses everything (F22). Also: an encrypted backup export and a documented format.

DQ7. Integration order. Options: file, CSV and ICS import and OS APIs first; then IMAP and CalDAV; then OAuth providers (verification burden, F20); then bank feeds via an aggregator (cost and legal flux, F19). Trade-off: reach against effort and compliance.

DQ8. Harness supply chain. Options: first-party only; signed community harnesses with a permission manifest; unreviewed. Trade-off: ecosystem growth against the OpenClaw outcome (F9). The narrow-tools constraint favours manifests that declare which domains a harness may read and write.

DQ9. Developer telemetry in end-user builds. Options: metadata only; opt-in content; none. Trade-off: debugging ability (the CLAUDE.md protocol relies on the UCL) against becoming a controller of contacts and finance data (F16). Flag: the current rule "the answer is in her telemetry" conflicts with privacy if shipped to others unchanged.

DQ10. Scope of legal review. Before any external user: jurisdiction list (EU, UK, US states); role under GDPR; health and finance positioning ("not financial advice", not a credit tool); EU AI Act classification (F21, thin).

5. Weak spots (the three claims least sure of)

  1. F16, third-party contact data under GDPR. The household-exemption conclusion rests on a statute line that was read, plus law-firm commentary and a Belgian DPA decision seen only in summary. No CJEU judgments or EDPB guidance were read. Medium-low confidence; needs a lawyer.
  2. F9 and F19, numbers and status. OpenClaw exposure counts and CVE details come from vendor blogs, and the CFPB 1033 status was last confirmed from mid-2026 material; both may be stale or inflated.
  3. F12 and F15, the risk reasoning. That a unified store has a worse risk profile, and that tokenisation leaves re-identification risk, are hypotheses; no study was found that measures either for personal assistants. F21 (EU AI Act) is essentially unresearched.
