Read this first: how much to trust this wave. Most sources were read as abstracts, search summaries or model-made digests; no track had full-text access, and one track (C) put no quotes in its file at all because nothing could be quoted with confidence. Directions are more reliable than numbers. Many of the figures for tradespeople, farmers and shop owners come from vendor surveys with small samples, and almost all the evidence is from the US or UK. Above all, no study comparing an integrated assistant with separate apps was found by any track, and no study tests a cross-domain inference (calendar plus budget plus contacts) against a single-domain reminder. Three tracks reached that conclusion independently. The value of integration, which is Lilith's product thesis, is therefore a hypothesis, supported by what human whole-life managers do and by single-domain evidence, not by a test. This wave builds on wave 1 (~/Desktop/lilith-assistant-research/verified/findings/00-summary.md) and does not repeat it; wave 1's findings on tokenisation, onboarding, the reversibility matrix, sandboxed harnesses, memory provenance and the anti-companion line still stand as leads.

Source ids (A1, B14, ...) are in sources.md. Page references point to the evidence.

1. The work worth taking over is noticing and monitoring, not doing. [evidence on the human labour, high on direction; hypothesis for the assistant] Household cognitive labour has four stages: anticipate, identify options, decide, monitor. Anticipation and monitoring are the invisible, never-finished, unequally carried stages, and in one self-report study the cognitive load was linked to depression and burnout in mothers (A1, A2, A8; correlational). Human household managers execute and escalate; the principal decides. So Lilith should notice, offer options and monitor, and leave the decision with the user; measure success as "less carried", not "more done". See: 01 F1, F3, Q2; 02 F3; 06 section 2.1.

2. Across eleven very different lives the pain is general; specialist content is a thin layer of data. [practice and evidence, high on pattern, low on numbers] After-hours admin, capture away from a desk, irregular money and chasing money owed, carrying other people's logistics, and interruption recur from plumber to researcher (B1, B7, B12, B19). What differs is formats, dated rules, vocabulary, calculators and integrations. A harness is mostly templates, checklists and rules with a source and a "valid as of" date, not intelligence, which makes it a maintenance and supply-chain question. See: 02 sections 1, 3.1-3.4, F1, F7; 06 section 3.3.

3. The plumber fails at the baseline before any harness loads. [hypothesis, grounded in practice] Hands busy, phone in a pocket, replies unread for hours, paper obligations, no desk. Capture from voice and photos comes before integrations, and three tracks agree on that order for different reasons: user reality, admin burden, and platform and legal gating (Google restricted-scope review, E19; US bank-data rights enjoined, E18). See: 02 Q2, Q11, design question 7; 01 Q3, Q8; 05 F19, F20, DQ7; 06 section 2.2.

4. Adaptation (verbosity, reading level, modality, pace) belongs to the baseline, set per situation. [evidence, medium] Blind users of Be My AI got replies about ten times longer than their questions and asked for "hurry mode"; the same person wants detail when exploring and brevity under pressure (B18, n=11). About one in three adults across 31 countries is at or below Level 1 in literacy or numeracy (B16, secondary). The people who most need a simple mode are the least able to find one. See: 02 F4, Q3, persona 2.9; 04 F3, Q2.

5. An untuned model's defaults are the opposite of an excellent reply, so excellence is a specification and an evaluation problem. [evidence, high] RLHF rewards length (D5); assistants consistently flatter, and human raters prefer it (D1, D2); models guess where they should ask and fail to tell clear requests from vague ones (D4, D11); multi-turn talk loses an average 39% of performance through early wrong assumptions (D3); evaluations reward guessing over "I don't know" (D16). User approval pushed one vendor to ship a sycophantic model it then rolled back (D14). Do not tune on satisfaction; score honest "can't", abstention and pushback separately. See: 04 F1, F5-F7, F11, F13, Q1; 06 section 2.4.

6. Ground every personal fact in her own data, and show its status. [evidence for the problem, high; hypothesis for the fix] On average only about half of generative search engines' sentences were fully supported by their citations (D12): decorative provenance creates unearned trust. Her advantage is that a personal fact can be checked against the row the tool returned. Separate retrieved, derived and unknown; never fill a personal gap from general knowledge; make "the tool failed" read differently from "nothing there". See: 04 F9, F10, Q3; 07 item 4.3.

7. Cross-domain value and cross-domain creepiness are the same act; the difference is what the message is built from. [evidence medium; hypothesis for the rule] Synthesis is a flow between contexts, which is what contextual integrity says makes things feel creepy (C11, C12). The defensible zone is arithmetic on user-entered facts (a clash, a bill date against a balance, a birthday), delivered at a natural pause, once, cheap to dismiss; never an inference about the user's state. The best measured case for joined-up awareness is single-domain: a payment reminder cut late fees but raised overdraft fees for some users (C19). Interruptions mid-task raise errors and annoyance (C2), and frequency is the lever that hurts (C9). See: 03 section 1, F2, F5, F7, F9, F13, section 2.6; 06 section 3.1.

8. Money is its own register. [evidence, medium-high on avoidance; hypothesis on design] People avoid unwelcome financial information (C24, C25); stigma drives concealment (C26); financial stress takes cognitive capacity (A19, C14); precise budget figures can raise spending (C22, not peer reviewed); and "freedom to enjoy life" is one of the CFPB's four elements of financial well-being (C28). Facts over verdicts, show the calculation instead of steering, shrink proactivity under stress, retreat when the topic is avoided, and make no debt advice. See: 03 F10, F14-F17, F19, Q12; 01 F9-F11.

9. The real risk is a steerable agent, not a stolen disk. [practice, high on the principle] Whole-life data plus untrusted content plus any outbound channel is the lethal trifecta (E12), and memory poisoning through injected content has been demonstrated (E13). Encryption at rest covers disk theft only (Recall's plaintext episode, E4, E5). Local and self-hosted did not save OpenClaw (E11, numbers unverified). Several baseline ideas from other tracks (scam defence reading messages, capturing job requests from texts, "send it for me", third-party harness data, any cloud call) supply the trifecta's missing legs. See: 05 F4, F9-F13, DQ1, DQ2, DQ8; 06 section 3.2.

10. The privacy promise is weakest for the target user, and the law stops at the developer. [hypothesis, medium-high; legal points medium-low] On-device models are small and local LLMs need about 8GB+ of VRAM for decent quality (E10, E22), so the plumber's laptop will usually push Lilith onto the cloud path, where tokenisation is minimisation, not anonymisation (E15; wave 1 item 2). The GDPR household exemption very likely covers the user's own contacts, but not anything that reaches the developer: telemetry, crash reports or a cloud processor (E16). The project's "the answer is in her telemetry" rule is fine at dev time and a conflict once others use her. Platform owners are winning the landscape and memory startups were bought or died (E1, E3, E6), so export and no required cloud service are the structural answer to "what if she disappears". See: 05 F8, F15, F16, F23, DQ4, DQ5, DQ9, Q5; 06 sections 3.4, 3.5.
