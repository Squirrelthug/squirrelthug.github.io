Who runs a whole life for someone, how they do it, and what integrated knowledge of schedule, money, people and obligations makes possible that separate apps cannot.

1. The answer in one paragraph

Whole lives have always been run by someone: a spouse (usually unpaid, usually a woman), a household manager or "chief of staff" in wealthy households, a family-office team, or an adult child coordinating a parent's care. In every case the work is mostly cognitive, not physical: noticing what is coming, deciding what to do, remembering to do it, and checking that it happened. That is exactly the layer a database plus a language model can carry. It is also the layer separate apps do not carry, because each app holds one domain and none holds the obligations that span domains (a trip, a tight month, a friend's birthday). The value of integration is therefore real but narrow. It lies in anticipation, deadline and dependency tracking, and "what am I forgetting" queries, far more than in dashboards. The cost is trust. A single store of schedule, money, relationships and obligations is what a good human household manager is trusted with, and it is what makes that person dangerous when trust fails. Human practice answers this with discretion, scoped authority, a named principal and graduated delegation; those four things have to be designed in, not assumed.

The developer's framing ("Jarvis for everyone") is incomplete in three ways: 1. The household is rarely one person: there is shared data, there are co-decision makers, and there are dependents. 2. The cognitive-labour literature says the unit of value is anticipation and monitoring, not task execution. 3. Some users' lives are dominated by obligations to others (caregiving). For them the assistant's main product is coordinating third parties, not self-management.

2. Findings

2.1 Who runs a whole life, and what they actually do

F1. The private-household or family-office "chief of staff" role is a bundle of duties, with confidentiality listed as essential. [practice] Confidence: medium. The source is recruiter marketing, so it describes what employers ask for, not what staff do day to day. - Eden Private Staff's candidate page lists, among others, "Organisation and coordination", budgeting and bill payments, "Calendar and travel", "Crisis management" and "Personal assistance", and states "confidentiality and discretion is absolutely vital" (A6, fetched digest). - Job postings add contracts, NDAs and personal finance for some roles (A5, search summary only). - Eden separates the household version (closest to the principal's personal life) from the family-office version (more strategic and governance-led). Another source notes the title is used loosely and often means an estate manager (A5, A6, search summaries). - The full bundle: staff and rota management, budgeting and bill payment, family diaries and appointments, travel, events, crisis handling, errands and special projects.

Implication [hypothesis]: the scope list of an expensive human is a usable checklist of what "whole-life" means. Note what is missing from it: there is no duty like "decide for the family". The role executes and escalates; the principal decides. This matches wave 1's authority and escalation principles (02-principles.md).

F2. Wealthy households buy this role because the unpaid version is unevenly distributed and costly. [evidence] Confidence: medium. The support is F3 to F5.

2.2 The mental-load literature

F3. Housework has a cognitive dimension distinct from the physical one, and the cognitive part is both more unequally distributed and more harmful. [evidence] Confidence: high on direction, medium on magnitude. - Daminger (2019), American Sociological Review 84(4): 609-633; 70 interviews with 35 couples. Splits cognitive labour into four stages: anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding, monitoring. In secondary summaries, anticipation and monitoring fell disproportionately to women, while decision-making was more shared or weighted toward men (A8; summary only, article not accessed). Figures such as "26 of 32 couples" come from JSTOR Daily coverage and should be checked. - Saxbe and Aviv (2024), Archives of Women's Mental Health; 322 mothers of young children, 30 tasks each split into cognitive and physical parts. Mothers reported about 73% of cognitive labour against 27% for partners, and 64% against 36% of physical labour. The coverage defines cognitive labour as "the cognitive effort that goes into anticipating needs, planning, organizing and delegating household tasks." The authors report that it was "the cognitive labor that had a more profound impact on women's psychological well-being", with more depression, stress, relationship dissatisfaction and burnout (A1, A2, fetched digests). Stated limits: self-report, mothers only, cohabiting heterosexual couples, no long-term data. Correlational. - Emens (2015 law review article "Admin"; 2019 book Life Admin) names the office-type work needed to run a life (paperwork, scheduling, medical and benefits matters) and notes that it is uncredited and unevenly distributed (A9; search summary only; book not read).

Implication [hypothesis]: an assistant should aim at the anticipate and monitor stages, because they are invisible and never finished. "Identify options" is where it can help most cheaply. "Deciding" should usually stay with the human. The data supports this aim but not a causal claim that offloading these stages improves wellbeing. No study found tests an assistant.

F4. Anticipation and monitoring are the stages the app ecosystem serves least. [hypothesis, grounded in F3 and F6] A to-do app records a decision once it has been made; no app notices that the passport expires before the trip.

2.3 Personal information management (PIM)

F5. The core PIM findings are fragmentation, the difference between finding and re-finding, and the fact that people curate rather than just retrieve. [evidence] Confidence: medium (read at the level of abstracts and book summaries). - Jones and Teevan (2011, Personal Information Management, UW Press) frame the problem as information scattered across documents, email, paper, photos, messages and tools, partly because each tool is bound to one environment. They distinguish finding new information from re-finding information the person has already seen (A7, search summary). - Whittaker (2011, ARIST 45) argues that the "consumption" model fails to explain most PIM, and that curation has three processes: keeping, managing and exploitation (re-finding) (A10, search summary; full text not retrieved). - Bergman and Whittaker (2016, The Science of Managing Our Digital Stuff, MIT Press) report from comparative studies that search, tags and similar public-information methods work less well for personal information than people's own hierarchical folders; the person is both organiser and retriever (A11, publisher and library abstracts only). - Boardman and Sasse (2004, CHI) is a cross-tool study of how people organise email, files and bookmarks. Only the abstract was seen; it concludes that tools and organisations could encourage more reflection on PIM (A12, abstract only).

Implication for Lilith [hypothesis]: integration pays most for people whose life is already fragmented across tools and who re-find the same facts again and again (insurance details, a child's school dates, a tradesperson's quote). Bergman and Whittaker's result is a warning: a retrieval-only assistant that does not match the user's own organising logic may do worse than a folder tree. Lilith should treat the user's own labels and structure as first-class and not impose a taxonomy. This is a lead, not a finding, because the folder result concerns files, not obligations.

F6. The brief's "personal information ecosystem" framing is real, but the exact phrase was not confirmed. A search for it in the PIM literature returned fragmentation and group dimensions but not the phrase itself (A7). Jones and Teevan do argue that PIM cannot be studied for an individual in isolation from their groups (search summary), which supports the "household, not individual" point in section 3. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

2.4 Caregiving, the sandwich generation, chronic conditions

F7. A very large minority of adults already does unpaid coordination work for another person, at financial cost. [evidence] Confidence: high for the headline numbers (AARP is the primary publisher), medium on the definitions. - AARP and NAC, Caregiving in the U.S. 2025 (A3, press release, fetched digest): - "63 million Americans—nearly 1 in 4 adults—provided ongoing care" - "Nearly 1 in 4 caregivers report providing 40+ hours of care per week" - "Nearly half of caregivers experienced at least one major financial impact"; about "$7,200 a year out-of-pocket in caregiving expenses" - "Just over 20% of caregivers have received formal training on medical and nursing tasks", while over half manage "complex medical and nursing tasks like injections, wound care, or medication management" - "Nearly 1 in 4 caregivers report feeling socially isolated" - A secondary summary reports a lower figure (36%) for narrower definitions of strain; the primary report was not examined (A4). - Pew (survey 28 Nov to 5 Dec 2012; published 2013): "Nearly half (47%) of adults in their 40s and 50s" have a parent aged 65 or older and are raising or supporting a child; about 15% support both financially; 21% supported a parent financially in the past year (A13, fetched digest). These figures are old. Newer figures (about one in four adults "sandwiched") come only from secondary press and are not confirmed (A14). - Caregiver apps. A 2018 JMIR review found only 8 of 44 caregiver apps had features in three or more categories. A later scoping review found no study of apps for coordinating care for people with multiple chronic conditions (A15, A16, search summaries). A CHI 2024 paper (Hsu and Chung) argues for attention to the wider care ecosystem and the "invisible work" of maintaining it (A16, search summary only).

Implication [hypothesis]: caregiving is a major whole-life use case that cuts across every other domain (money, appointments, other people's medical data). It also puts a third party's health data into the user's database, which raises the stakes of every concern on page 05.

F8. With ADHD and executive-function difficulties, prospective memory and time-based tasks are the main failure point, and event-based cues beat time-based ones. [evidence for the deficit; practice for the aids] Confidence: medium. - Via search summary: Fuermaier et al. (2013, PLoS ONE; 45 adults with ADHD and 45 controls) found the largest deficit in planning and initiating action. A 2018 study (ADHD Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorders; 29 with ADHD, 24 controls) linked poorer everyday prospective memory to procrastination, and its authors report that every study of time-based prospective memory found poor performance (A17, search summary; the Springer page would not open). - External aids and event-based cues are recommended by a patient-advocacy source, CHADD (A18, search listing only; not opened). No controlled trial of aids improving money or time outcomes was found. - The "ADHD tax" is a descriptive term, not a study finding.

Implication [hypothesis]: an assistant that reminds is not the same as one that helps the user start. The deficit found was in initiation and planning, not only in remembering. Evidence on what assistants add beyond a calendar is thin.

2.5 Money, stress and behaviour (covered in depth on page 03)

F9. Financial stress uses up cognitive capacity, so an assistant that makes the user feel the shortfall may degrade their other functioning. [evidence] Confidence: medium. Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir and Zhao (2013, Science 341: 976-980) induced thoughts about finances and found reduced cognitive performance in poor but not well-off participants, and found that Indian sugarcane farmers performed worse before harvest than after (A19, search summary; paper not opened). Replication and effect-size debates exist and were not checked. The press framing of "13 IQ points" is a secondary claim.

F10. People avoid unwelcome financial information. [evidence, with disagreement] Karlsson, Loewenstein and Seppi (2009, Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 38(2): 95-115) found that investor account logins fell in flat or falling markets. Gherzi et al. (2014) found that monitoring rose after both positive and daily negative returns, which cuts against the simple version (A20, search summary only).

Implication [hypothesis]: integration that surfaces "your month is tight" must expect avoidance, and unrequested bad news is a design risk. Page 03 takes this further.

F11. The financial-wellbeing literature offers a measuring stick, not a causal map. [evidence] The CFPB's 0-100 scale found that savings and cushions best separate people, that some debt and credit experiences are strongly negatively associated, and that scores are stable over the short term (A21, search summary). No evidence was found on whether assistance changes scores.

2.6 What integration enables, and its cost in trust

F12. What integration enables that separate apps cannot. [hypothesis, labelled as such because no study tests an integrated assistant against separate apps] 1. Cross-domain constraint detection (trip date x low balance x gift obligation). 2. A single "what is coming that I have not dealt with" view, built on anticipation and monitoring (F3). 3. Dependency and deadline tracking (renewals, tax dates, vaccinations, warranties). 4. Context for drafting (writing to a person with the right relationship, history and pending items). 5. Carrying state for third parties (a parent's appointments, a child's school dates).

Fieldwork on the human equivalents supports (1), (2) and (5) as the substance of the role (F1, F3, F7). Items (3) and (4) are inference.

F13. The trust costs have precedent in how human staff are managed. [practice] Recruiters list confidentiality first (F1). The matching digital risks are: a single data store is the object of attack (page 05); a shared household is a coercion surface; and tools with legitimate sharing purposes get repurposed for surveillance. Freed et al. (2018, CHI Best Paper, "A Stalker's Paradise") and follow-on work (Bellini 2023 on technology-facilitated financial abuse; 158 survivors and 16 advocates) document intimate-partner abusers exploiting shared family plans, shared cloud accounts and "dual-use" apps. Only search-summary descriptions were seen, not the papers (A22).

[hypothesis] A whole-life assistant for a couple or household is a dual-use tool of exactly this kind, and its designers must decide who can see what.

3. What the developer did not ask

  1. Whose life is it? The household problem. Most whole lives are shared (partner, children, parents). Cognitive-labour research is about couples; caregiving is about another person. A one-user, one-database design hides this. Answer: decide early whether Lilith is strictly single-principal, and if so, what she does when the user asks her to remember the partner's commitments or a parent's medication. Evidence: F3, F7, F13. Confidence is high that this will come up; there is no evidence on the right answer.
  2. Is the target "more done" or "less carried"? The research says the wellbeing harm comes from carrying anticipation and monitoring, not from doing chores (F3). An assistant that raises task output but still needs the user to notice everything will not relieve the load. Answer: measure "things the user no longer has to remember", not "tasks completed". [hypothesis]
  3. Whole-life management can create a worse mental load if setup is a chore. Maintaining the database is itself admin (Emens). Answer: ingestion cost is a first-order risk; if the user must enter everything, the assistant is one more app. Evidence: Emens (summary), F5. Wave 1 03-trust-and-onboarding.md already argues against upfront interviews.
  4. Who is accountable when she is wrong about an obligation? A human manager can be fired and sued; a missed renewal costs real money. Answer: unlike a human, she has no duty of care in law. The product must say what she monitors, what she does not, and when she last checked. [hypothesis]
  5. Does she serve the user when the user's wellbeing and wishes diverge (avoidance, F10)? An integrated assistant knows the bad number. Answer: do not volunteer bad news without a rule; give the user the choice of when, how and whether. Deferred to page 03. Evidence: F9, F10.
  6. Third-party data in contacts and caregiving. The user's database holds other people's birthdays, health information and debts. Answer: legally this is the user's responsibility in many places, but the product should still minimise (page 05 on GDPR). Caregiver data about a parent is health data about a person who never consented to the tool.
  7. People in crisis or transition (bereavement, divorce, job loss, diagnosis). Whole-life data suddenly includes things the user wants gone or hidden. Answer: provide a way to scope, pause and forget a domain or a person. Wave 1 04-digital-assistants-evidence.md cites Sas and Whittaker (2013) on selective forgetting.
  8. Not everyone's obligations are digital. Cash jobs, paper bills, a parent who only phones. Answer: capture has to start from photos, voice notes and typed one-liners, not integrations. [hypothesis] See page 02 for occupations.
  9. Abusive or controlling co-users. If someone else has the device or password, an assistant holding the whole life is a surveillance tool (F13). Answer: assume this case exists; design recovery, lock and hidden-domain behaviour; do not assume trust between household members. The evidence is real, but only summaries of the studies were seen.
  10. What does the user already use? Whole lives live in a calendar, email, a bank app and a spouse's memory. Answer: integrating with existing stores may matter more than replacing them (page 05).

4. Design-session questions

# Question Options and trade-offs Conflict with hard constraints
1 Single principal or household? (a) Strict single principal, with explicit "other people" records: simplest and safest on privacy, but ignores the shared load. (b) Shared household space with per-person scoping: matches how lives work, but creates coercion and consent risk and a harder permissions model. (b) strains the "encrypted local database" and per-domain consent unless carefully scoped.
2 How does data get in? (a) Manual and conversational capture: private, slow. (b) Import from calendar, email, bank: fast, richer synthesis, but a wider attack surface and consent issues for third-party data. (c) Photo and voice capture of paper life. (b) touches local-first and tokenisation.
3 Which cognitive stage does she own? Anticipate and monitor only (she drafts options, the human decides), or decide-and-act on low-stakes items. Trade-off: trust and error cost against real relief. None, but ties to wave 1's authority principles.
4 Is the integrated view the product, or a by-product of conversation? A "what's coming" digest, or answering when asked. A digest risks avoidance and nagging (F10); on-demand risks never being used. None.
5 Per-domain scoping Let the user wall off finance, health and relationships with separate unlock, or keep one store. Walls reduce synthesis (the thesis) but also reduce blast radius. Tension between the thesis and privacy; flag explicitly.
6 Third-party health data (caregiving) Allow, restrict, or require a separate harness with extra protections. Tied to tokenisation and to local-model-only handling of health data.
7 Stating the limits of monitoring A visible "what I watch and when I last checked", or silent best effort. Honesty favours the first. Matches "honest I can't".

5. Weak spots (the three claims least sure of)

  1. Integration value. The claim that cross-domain anticipation measurably helps (F4, F12) is inference. No study was found comparing integrated assistants with separate apps; all the evidence is about the human labour, not the tool.
  2. Daminger's detailed figures. The 2019 article could not be opened. The four-stage model is well attested, but the "26 of 32 couples" detail comes from secondary coverage.
  3. ADHD and financial-stress effects. F8 and F9 rest on search summaries, not read papers. No trials of external aids on outcomes were found, and the Mani et al. effect size and replication status were not checked.
