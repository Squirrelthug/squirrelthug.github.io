1. The answer in one paragraph

Connecting domains pays off when the connection is a time-bound, checkable fact the user already owns (a payment due, a trip date, a birthday, a clash between two commitments), and when it is delivered at a natural pause, once, in a form that is cheap to dismiss. It turns creepy or harmful in three situations: 1. when it surfaces an inference the user never stated (health, money trouble, relationship state), which breaks the norms of the context the data came from; 2. when it hits money shame or avoidance with a judgemental or high-salience message, which can make people look away from their finances; 3. when it fires often or at a bad moment, so that people learn to ignore it.

The strongest measured evidence for "joined-up" value is thin and mostly from finance: well-timed reminders and salience changes measurably reduce late and overdraft fees, but with side effects (a payment reminder cut late fees and raised overdraft fees for some users). Evidence that a cross-domain inference (calendar plus budget plus contacts) beats single-domain reminders is, as far as the track could find, absent. That is a finding, not a gap in search effort: nobody seems to have run the test. The practical stance: Lilith should be reactive by default across domains, proactive only inside a narrow whitelist of low-regret, user-owned facts, always show where a connection came from, and make "not now / never this kind" one tap.

2. Findings

2.1 When to speak: the decision problem

F1. Proactivity is an expected-utility decision under uncertainty about goals, not a feature. [evidence, medium] Horvitz (1999) frames mixed-initiative design as weighing the benefit of automated action against the cost of a wrong guess. When unsure of the user's intent, the system should engage in a short dialogue, taking into account the cost of bothering the user needlessly (C1; the search summary supports this wording, but the PDF could not be opened). Wave 1 04-digital-assistants-evidence.md makes the same case with Clippy and Lumiere: the research system had an interruption-cost model, the shipped product used fixed rules. The Clippy history relies on wave 1 and was not re-checked. Implication: "should she speak?" needs at least three inputs per candidate message: confidence that the fact is true, value if it is true, and the cost of interrupting now. The third is the one most often left out.

F2. Interrupt at task boundaries, not mid-task. [evidence, high for the direction, medium for the numbers] - In a controlled experiment (N = 50), Bailey and Konstan (2006) found that interrupting during a task rather than between tasks raised completion time by about 3% to 27%, roughly doubled errors, and raised annoyance by 31% to 106%. Deferring a notification by only a few seconds, to a natural boundary, removed much of the harm (C2, search summary). - A later study (Powers and Scerbo, 2023, via a search summary) found interruptions at coarse breakpoints looked much like no interruption, while fine breakpoints raised resumption lag and frustration (C3). - Iqbal and Bailey's attempts to detect breakpoints automatically reached only about 55% to 77% accuracy depending on the task (C4, via a survey summary).

Caveat: these are desktop lab tasks. For a voice assistant on a home machine the "task boundary" signal is weaker (is the user mid-sentence on the phone, cooking, working?). [hypothesis] Lilith has one advantage: for anything non-urgent she can simply wait until the user next talks to her. That is a free, perfect boundary.

F3. Perceived disruption depends on who and what, not just on content value. [evidence, medium] In an in-situ study of 20 users and 10,372 notifications, Mehrotra et al. (2016) found that response time and perceived disruption depended on presentation, alert type, sender-recipient relationship and the task in progress. Notifications with useful or important content could still be disruptive, and perceived disruption predicted dismissal (C5, abstract and summary). They also report personality effects. Implication: "it was relevant" does not excuse the interruption. [hypothesis] A voice assistant volunteering speech is probably more intrusive than a silent notification: it is public to anyone in the room and cannot be glanced at and ignored. There is no direct evidence for voice here; this is a gap.

F4. Receptivity and burden are first-class concepts in health "just-in-time" work, and often missing in practice. [evidence, medium] The JITAI framework (Nahum-Shani et al., 2018) separates states of vulnerability or opportunity from receptivity (the person's ability to receive, process and use the support) (C6, summary). A 2025 systematic review of mental-health JITAIs found adaptivity and receptivity were often missing and the components only partly backed by evidence (C7, summary). This is a field-wide warning that "context-aware timing" is easier to claim than to do. The paper's own definition of burden was not verified.

F5. Proactive LLM agents: early evidence says help is wanted, but frequency hurts. [evidence, low-medium] - Lu et al. (2024) built ProactiveBench (6,790 events) and report an F1 of about 66% for fine-tuned models deciding whether to offer help (C8, abstract summary). It is a benchmark on simulated human activity, so it says "this is hard and models are far from perfect", not "users like it". paraphrase: not found in the cited source - A randomised coding study (Chen et al., 2024) reports 12-18% more tasks completed and a preference for proactive assistants over a chat baseline by 80-90% of participants, but only about half preferred the variant that suggested more often, and the authors note that frequency can harm the experience (C9, secondary summaries only). The domain is programming, not life admin, so transfer is uncertain.

Net: a real signal that frequency is the control that matters, and that proactive versus reactive is not a binary choice.

F6. A product signal, not evidence: daily "briefing" proactivity is being shipped. [practice, low] Press coverage says OpenAI launched ChatGPT Pulse in late September 2025 as a once-a-day overnight digest for Pro users, drawing on memory and, per some reports, connected apps such as calendar and mail, with opt-in connections (C10, press only). The company reportedly acknowledged it may present irrelevant information. OpenAI's announcement was not read and its performance is unknown. It matters because it is a batched, once-a-day, user-scheduled shape of proactivity, which fits the bounded-interruption lesson of F2 and F5. Page 05 covers the product landscape.

2.2 When connecting domains is creepy or wrong

F7. "Creepiness" tracks contextual-integrity violations: data used in a context other than the one it came from. [evidence, medium] Nissenbaum's contextual integrity says privacy is violated when the norms of appropriateness and the norms of flow for a context are broken (C11, summary). Shklovski et al. (2014) applied this lens in two studies of European smartphone users and found people describing app behaviour as intruding on personal space in "creepy" ways, yet continuing to use the apps (C12, abstract). Tene and Polonetsky (2013/14) argue creepiness is subjective and shifts with social norms, and call for transparency, usable access to information and attention to context (C13, summary). Wave 1 06-pitfalls.md section 6.5 reaches the same position. Implication for synthesis: synthesis is, by definition, a flow of information between contexts (budget to calendar to contacts). Cross-domain value and cross-context creepiness are the same act; the difference is whether the user regards the flow as appropriate. [hypothesis] For a single-user, local-first assistant, the user did put the data in the same vault, so some flows are plainly appropriate (calendar plus calendar). Others are not (a contact's health note feeding a budget remark). Domain pairs probably need an explicit "allowed to talk to each other" matrix, not a global yes.

F8. People keep using things they find creepy, so continued use is not evidence of consent. [evidence, low-medium] Same source (C12). [hypothesis] Do not read retention as validation of a proactive behaviour; instrument dismissals, mutes and "why did you say that?" requests instead.

F9. Inference is the line; retrieval is not. [practice, medium] Wave 1 06-pitfalls.md separates "given" from "inferred" data and advises never surfacing uninvited personal inferences. The track agrees and adds an operational reading: a bill due date the user entered is given; "you seem stressed about money" is inferred. [hypothesis] A defensible rule: proactive messages may be built only from user-entered or user-confirmed facts plus arithmetic on them, never from a model's guess about the user's state.

F10. Connecting domains can reduce mental load, but money worries are cognitively expensive in themselves. [evidence, medium on direction, low on size] Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir and Zhao (2013, Science) report that financial pressure lowered cognitive test performance in a mall experiment (only the high-stakes $1,500 car-repair scenario produced a drop for lower-income participants) and in a field study of 464 sugarcane farmers before and after harvest (C14, press summaries; the "13 IQ points" equivalence is a press figure not checked against the paper). There is a replication and interpretation debate that was not researched. Implication: [hypothesis] in a tight month, extra messages cost the user more than the same messages cost a comfortable user. Proactivity should shrink, not grow, when finances are stressed, and should offer one concrete next step.

2.3 Reminders, prospective memory and the value of timing

F11. Remembering intentions is a distinct memory problem, and time-based tasks are the weak spot. [evidence, medium] Prospective memory research (McDaniel and Einstein 2007, a book seen only as catalogue entries, C15) separates event-based from time-based tasks. For adults with ADHD, studies reported through search summaries found large impairment in time-based prospective memory and no difference in event-based tasks (C16: Altgassen, Kretschmer and Kliegel 2014, n = 25; a 2013 PLOS One study). A 2018 study (n = 29) linked everyday prospective-memory performance to procrastination (C16). Implication: a "remind me at 3pm" tool serves the hardest case. [hypothesis] Cues tied to a place, an event or an arrival ("when you open the diary", "when you leave work") may be more reliable than clock alarms, and Lilith can link a reminder to a calendar event rather than a bare time. No external-aid trial for adults with ADHD was found; the intervention evidence is thin.

F12. Specific plans ("when X, I will do Y") raise goal attainment. [evidence, high on the existence of an effect, medium on size] Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 review reports 94 tests with a medium-to-large effect (d = .65) on goal attainment (C17, summary). A 2024 meta-analysis of 642 tests reportedly finds effects vary with plan format and motivation (C17, secondary only, unverified). Older secondary sites quote adherence-specific effect sizes that could not be confirmed and are not used. Implication: [hypothesis] when a user states a goal ("I need to renew the van insurance"), the highest-value cheap move is to help turn it into a when-and-where plan tied to a calendar slot, rather than just storing it.

F13. Reminders measurably change money behaviour, but the effect is narrow and has side effects. - Savings reminders. Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan and Zinman (2016) report field experiments with new bank savers (including at a Philippines bank) in which reminders increased saving. Reminders tied to a specific future expense should work best, and messages naming a goal and incentives did well (C18, summary; a working-paper summary reports about 6% more saved with monthly reminders, which may differ from the published figure). An extra late reminder added nothing (C18). - Payment reminders. Medina (2021, Review of Financial Studies) ran a field experiment in Brazil with 26,069 platform users. Credit-card payment reminders cut late fees by about 14% but raised checking-account overdraft fees by about 9%. The overdraft rise was concentrated in users with prior overdraft use, who saw a net 5% increase in total fees, while the others saved about 15% (C19, abstract and summaries). [evidence, medium] - Salience shocks. Stango and Zinman (2014) found that taking a survey that mentioned overdraft fees lowered the probability of an overdraft, with effects accumulating over repeated surveys and concentrated in people with lower education and financial literacy, working mainly through fewer debit transactions and cancelled automatic payments (C20, summary). [evidence, medium]

Net for Lilith: this is the clearest measured value of a single-domain nudge, and also the clearest warning: a nudge on one bill pushed money trouble into another pot. That is itself an argument for cross-domain awareness: a payment reminder that knew the checking balance would have been a better reminder. [hypothesis] Treat this as the best available justification for synthesis, but it is an inference from one study, not a test of synthesis.

F14. Giving people information about their finances does not reliably help, and can hurt. - Carlin, Olafsson and Pagel (2023, Review of Finance) studied the release of a mobile app by an Icelandic aggregator and found non-sufficient-funds fees fell after launch (roughly a 38% fall within 24 months per one summary; about $4.5-4.8 a year per another), with mixed results for overdraft interest and late fees. The working paper and the final paper differ (C21, summaries). [evidence, medium-low] - Pocheptsova Ghosh and Huang (2020, a technical report for the Think Forward Initiative, not peer reviewed) report that precise budget-standing information can raise spending near the end of the budget period, and that shorter windows, ranges instead of exact amounts, and carry-over reminders reduced the effect (C22, summaries). [evidence, low] - Olafsson and Pagel (2018) use the same kind of aggregator data to document spending responses to the arrival of regular and irregular income (C23, abstract). Their framing is "liquid hand-to-mouth" households spending by heuristic. [evidence, medium] No numbers were retrieved. - [hypothesis] "Show the balance" and "show the budget remaining" are not neutral. Budget framing needs testing for each design: which number, which window, what happens to an unspent remainder.

F15. People avoid unpleasant information, including about money. [evidence, medium-high] - Karlsson, Loewenstein and Seppi (2009) found investors log in less often when markets are flat or falling (C24, summary). A later large-sample study (852 million observations, 1.1 million investors) reports logins fell about 9.5% after a decline the previous day (C24, secondary summary). - Golman, Hagmann and Loewenstein (2017) review information avoidance: information itself carries utility, so people avoid useful, free information (C25, summary). - Marketing research (the journal paper "Helping Those That Hide") reports that debtors who anticipate stigma conceal, spend socially and avoid help, and that shame limited engagement with a financial education course in a field test (C26, summary). - Industry surveys report widespread financial shame (for example Coast Capital 2023: 36% of Canadians), but these are not peer reviewed (C27, summary).

Implication: [hypothesis] the proactive message about money is the one most likely to be avoided or resented. Tone and channel matter more here than in calendar help. If the user stops opening the finance topic after a firm message, that is a signal to retreat, not to repeat. This is the single most important difference between calendar proactivity and money proactivity.

F16. Define "good" with a recognised yardstick, not "spent less". [evidence, medium] The CFPB (2015) defines financial well-being through four elements: control over day-to-day and month-to-month finances; capacity to absorb a shock; being on track for financial goals; and freedom to make choices that let you enjoy life (C28, summary of the New York Fed copy; a 10-question scale was later built from them). Note that "freedom to enjoy life" is one of the four. [hypothesis] An assistant that optimises only for lower spending works against one of the four elements. Trip plus tight budget plus birthday is not a problem to be talked down; it is a trade-off the user may already have decided.

2.4 Nudges, ethics and paternalism

F17. Nudges and boosts are different design philosophies with different autonomy costs. [evidence/theory, medium] Hertwig and Grüne-Yanoff (2017) distinguish nudges (steer behaviour while preserving freedom of choice) from boosts (build the person's own competence) across seven dimensions, including reversibility and normative implications (C29, abstract). [hypothesis] For a personal assistant, a "boost" means showing the user the calculation ("you have X free after bills until payday; the trip costs Y") so they learn to see it, rather than quietly steering. not checked: source could not be opened

F18. The test for manipulation is engagement with reflective choice, and transparency lowers the ethical risk. [evidence/theory, medium-low] Sunstein (2016) defines manipulation as influence that does not sufficiently engage or appeal to a person's capacity for reflective and deliberative choice (C30, summary). A 2025 chapter argues nudges are less objectionable when their ends are legitimate and they are fully open to scrutiny, but that well-meant manipulative nudges still undermine autonomy without consent (C30, summary). No formally named "transparency test" was located; one exam-prep site claimed one and was discounted. Implication: [hypothesis] a usable internal test: "Would the user be fine with seeing the exact reason she nudged them?" That is the same as showing provenance (F20).

F19. Paternalism risk is higher when she is right. [hypothesis, no direct evidence found] When a user knowingly overspends (a funeral, a wedding, a holiday), correct arithmetic delivered unprompted reads as judgement. No studies of assistant-delivered money advice and perceived paternalism were found; thin ground and a design-session question (DQ4).

2.5 How to phrase it and how to be wrong gracefully

F20. Show the source, give the reason, make correction cheap. [practice, medium] Microsoft's guidelines for human-AI interaction (Amershi et al., CHI 2019) were distilled from over 160 candidate recommendations into 18, grouped by when they apply: initially, during interaction, when wrong, and over time. Later items include updating cautiously, encouraging granular feedback, conveying consequences, providing global controls and notifying users of changes (C31, summaries). The exact wording of the "scope services when in doubt" and "time services based on context" guidelines could not be confirmed, so they are not quoted or numbered.

F21. Set expectations about the type of error, and choose which error to risk. [evidence, low-medium] Kocielnik, Amershi and Bennett (CHI 2019) tested expectation-setting designs on a scheduling assistant that detects meeting requests in email, using two versions with equal 50% accuracy, one avoiding false positives and one avoiding false negatives. Their abstract reports the techniques significantly increased acceptance (C32, summary; the precision and recall results and numbers were not obtained). [hypothesis] For proactive cross-domain messages, a false positive (a wrong or irrelevant message) is visible and costs trust immediately; a false negative (a missing nudge) is invisible. That argues for high precision and low volume, accepting silent misses, and telling the user what she does not watch for.

F22. Alert volume itself degrades acceptance. [evidence, medium, from wave 1] Wave 1 00-summary.md finding 6 cites Ancker et al. (2017) on 1.59 million clinical alerts: each extra alert reduced acceptance, and repeat alerts were overridden at very high rates. That source was not re-opened. Different domain, but it is the best large-sample number on alert fatigue the track knows of.

F23. Memory errors and forgetting. [evidence, medium] Sas and Whittaker (2013) interviewed 24 people about digital possessions after a romantic breakup. The items were evocative and upsetting, people used different disposal strategies, and the authors argue for intentional forgetting (C33, abstract and summary). This matters because cross-domain proactivity can resurface an ex's birthday, a deceased relative's recurring appointment or an old debt. [hypothesis] Life events need an explicit "stop mentioning this" control that works across all domains at once (calendar, contacts, notes, budget), not one switch per domain.

2.6 Which cross-domain insights have measured value?

No direct trials of cross-domain synthesis were found. What the evidence does and does not support:

Insight type Supported by Strength Notes
Bill or payment due soon (calendar-like obligation) F13 (Medina; Stango and Zinman) Medium, with side effects Net benefit depends on the user's balance
Save toward a known future expense F13 (Karlan et al.) Medium Works best when the expense is named
Make a plan for a stated goal F12 (Gollwitzer and Sheeran) Medium-high on direction Generic goal pursuit, not specific to money
Trip + tight budget + gift None found None Plausible [hypothesis] via mental load (F10), scarcity and calendar conflicts; untested
Time-based reminders for people with weak time-based prospective memory F11 Medium on the deficit, low on remedy
Spending information shown back to the user F14 Mixed; can backfire Design-specific

[hypothesis] The most credible "synthesis wins" are arithmetic on user-entered facts (balance on day X against bills on day X; two overlapping events; gift cost against remaining budget), not insights that need a model to guess meaning. They are verifiable and cheap to be wrong about.

3. What the developer did not ask

Q1. Is a proactive assistant even what users want? Mixed. In the coding study most preferred a proactive assistant to chat-only, but only about half liked more frequent suggestions (F5), and voice-assistant history (wave 1 04-digital-assistants-evidence.md) shows proactive upselling failed. There is no direct evidence on whole-life assistants. Answer: build reactive excellence first; add proactivity as a narrow, opt-in, instrumented layer. [hypothesis]

Q2. What does a wrong cross-domain statement cost, compared with a wrong calendar read? A wrong calendar fact is a bug. A wrong connection ("you can't afford this") is a judgement about the user's life. A false positive spoils the message's credibility and may cause shame (F15). Answer: present connections as observations with their inputs ("balance 1,200 on the 3rd, rent 900 on the 5th, trip deposit 400 on the 4th"), so that wrongness is traceable and the user can correct inputs instead of arguing with a verdict.

Q3. Who owns the information about other people in the synthesis (contact birthdays, a friend's illness)? Contacts are third parties who did not consent to being joined to the user's budget. Page 05 covers regulation. From this track: contextual integrity (F7) suggests a friend's birthday used for a gift reminder is within norms, but a friend's reported illness used for any inference about the user is not. Treat third-party data as "use for the stated purpose only". [hypothesis]

Q4. When should she stay silent even though she knows something important? When the user is stressed, ashamed or avoiding the topic (F15, F10); when the fact is an inference (F9); when the user has muted the theme; when she cannot say how she knows. Silence has a cost too (a missed deadline). Answer: severity tiers. Anything with a hard deadline and a financial penalty may break the rule once, in plain language. Everything else waits for the user to ask. [hypothesis]

Q5. How does she tell "decided" from "unaware"? She cannot, so she should ask once. A first flag ("this brings you to X on the 4th; want me to flag this kind of thing?") turns the user's reply into a durable preference. This resembles the "dialogue to resolve uncertainty" principle in F1. Answer: the first instance gets a question, not an action.

Q6. What does "proactive" mean when the machine is off most of the day? Local-first on a desktop means Lilith may not be running when a deadline passes. There is no evidence on this; it is an architectural question for the design session. A proactive promise she cannot keep ("I'll remind you Friday" when the machine will be off) is worse than none, an instance of the reliability-first principle in wave 1 00-summary.md finding 1. [hypothesis]

Q7. Does proactivity differ by user (plumber versus nurse)? Almost certainly: a self-employed tradesperson has lumpy income and deadlines (tax, quotes, materials), a shift worker has unpredictable availability, a parent has relentless low-grade load. Page 02 covers this. From this track: the baseline proactive set should be tiny and universal (hard deadlines, conflicts, upcoming birthdays), and richer proactivity should arrive through harnesses and per-user configuration. [hypothesis]

Q8. Does a morning briefing count as proactivity, and is it safer? It is the safest shape: user-scheduled, batched, one interruption a day, bounded content (F2, F5, F6). It does not solve content risk (a briefing can still contain a shaming number). Answer: yes, it is safer on timing, and the content rules (F9, F15) still apply.

Q9. How should she handle a user repeatedly ignoring a reminder? Ignoring has several meanings (decided, overwhelmed, avoiding, forgot). The evidence that an extra late reminder adds nothing (F13) suggests repeating is not the answer. [hypothesis] After one ignored reminder, switch from "remind" to "ask": "do you want me to drop this, move it, or break it into smaller steps?" For users with ADHD, the same reminder resurfaced at a different cue (F11) may do better than a repeat at the same time.

Q10. Is there an ethical difference between helping the user keep a commitment and steering their behaviour? Yes (F17, F18). Helping with a commitment the user stated is service. Steering toward a goal the assistant inferred is a nudge, and a nudge the user cannot see is a manipulation risk. Answer: act only on user-stated goals, show the reasoning, and run no hidden engagement metrics.

Q11. Is telemetry about proactive messages itself a privacy problem? A log line saying "she warned about the budget" is sensitive data in its own right. Not researched deeply; flagged for page 05. The project's own rule that debugging reads the UCL (project CLAUDE.md) makes this concrete: the log would contain the very inferences F9 warns about.

Q12. What if the user's money situation is bad (debt, arrears)? This is where shame (F15), scarcity (F10) and harm risks peak. Wave 1 (06-pitfalls.md, scope-creep section) argues for hard scope ceilings against acting as an unlicensed financial adviser. Regulation was not researched (see page 05). Answer for now: she can organise and display facts and point to human services, and should avoid advice on what to do about debt. [hypothesis]

4. Design-session questions (options and trade-offs)

DQ1. Default stance on proactivity - (a) Reactive only; synthesis only when asked. Safest for trust, lowest risk, no measured proactive harm. Costs the "Jarvis" feel, and the Medina result suggests some users lose money for lack of joined-up warnings. - (b) Opt-in proactive whitelist (hard deadlines, conflicts, birthdays). Moderate. Needs a preference mechanism and instrumentation. - (c) Broad proactive synthesis. Highest ceiling, highest creepiness and shame risk, and no evidence for the claimed value. The track would not start here. - Constraint check: none conflict with privacy, since all are local. (c) conflicts with the spirit of "honest about what she can't do" if inferences are presented as facts.

DQ2. What may trigger a proactive message - (a) Only user-entered facts and arithmetic on them (F9). Verifiable, easy to explain; misses subtler patterns. - (b) Model-inferred patterns as well. More coverage, but this is where creepiness and wrong-memory harm live. If chosen, require a "why am I seeing this" link and a confirmation step before the first use of each pattern.

DQ3. When to deliver - (a) Only when the user next opens or speaks to her (a free boundary). No interruption; risk of missing a deadline while the user is away. - (b) A scheduled digest at a time the user picks. One interruption a day; a familiar shape (F6). - (c) Real time, with an urgency threshold reserved for penalty-bearing deadlines. Needs a defensible urgency model; start with a very short list. - Voice-specific: speaking aloud unprompted is public and unglanceable (F3, hypothesis). Option: text-first for proactive items, voice only on request.

DQ4. Money messaging policy - (a) Facts only, neutral tone, no evaluation ("balance on 5th: 300"). Least paternalistic; may feel cold. - (b) Goal-linked framing (against a goal the user set). Probably higher engagement (F13); needs a goal store. - (c) Advice ("you should cut back"). Most paternalistic and the biggest scope-creep risk; wave 1 argues against it. - Also decide whether to show exact remaining-budget figures (the F14 spending backfire) or ranges and rolling windows.

DQ5. Cross-domain permission model - (a) One global switch. Simple, blunt. - (b) A per-domain-pair matrix (budget-to-calendar yes, contacts-to-budget no). Matches contextual integrity (F7); more setup burden; could start from conservative defaults and prompt on first use. - (c) Per-message "may I connect these?". Interrupts, and F22 suggests prompts decay. - Constraint check: (b) interacts with the brief's hard constraint of per-domain scoping; consider it the natural expression of that constraint.

DQ6. How to be wrong gracefully - Standard set: show inputs; one-tap "wrong / not now / never this kind"; a persistent mute that works across domains (F23); no repeat after a dismissal (F13, F22). - Open choice: after N dismissals, does she stop that category automatically, or ask? Automatic is safer for trust; asking is more transparent.

DQ7. Measurement without surveillance - Which signals judge success (dismissal rate, mute rate, "why?" requests, acted-on rate), and how long they are kept. This trades tuning proactivity against building a log of inferences about the user (Q11). Flagged for page 05.

DQ8. Building evidence - Because there is no direct evidence on cross-domain synthesis, consider a staged experiment: ship two or three narrow synthesis types behind a flag, measure dismissals and corrections, then widen. This costs time but replaces assumptions with the first real data in this area.

5. Weak spots (the three claims least sure of)

  1. That there is no measured evidence for cross-domain synthesis specifically. This is an absence-of-evidence claim from a limited search with summary-level reading. A proper literature search in personal informatics, PIM and HCI for "context-aware reminders across calendar and finance" may find something missed.
  2. The exact figures (Bailey and Konstan percentages, the Medina fee changes, the Carlin et al. fee falls, the Chen et al. 12-18% and 80-90%). All come from abstracts or secondary summaries, and at least two (Karlan et al.'s 6%, Carlin et al.'s numbers) differ between working papers and final versions. Direction is more reliable than magnitude.
  3. That voice delivery of proactive messages is more intrusive than text, and that the "wait until she next speaks" boundary is sufficient.
