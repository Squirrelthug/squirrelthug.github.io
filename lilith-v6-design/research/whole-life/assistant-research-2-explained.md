Before we start, I want to be straight with you about what this research rests on, because it changes how much weight you can put on any single part of it. The research agents in this second wave could not read most of their sources in full. What they saw, in almost every case, was an abstract, a search engine summary, or a short digest of a web page written by another model. Not one source in this wave was read cover to cover in the ordinary sense. One of the five research tracks, the one on proactivity, put no quotations in its report at all, because it never saw text it could quote with confidence. Most of the legal points and most of the stories about security incidents came from secondary sources, such as law firm blogs, press coverage and vendor write ups, rather than from the court decisions, regulator documents or original disclosures themselves. Many of the numbers about tradespeople, farmers and shop owners come from surveys run by companies selling software to those people, with small samples. Almost all of the evidence is from the United States or Britain.

And there is one absence I need you to hold onto for the whole guide. No research track found a single study that compares one integrated assistant holding a whole life against a person using separate apps. Nor did anyone find a study testing a cross domain insight, the calendar plus the budget plus the contacts, against a simple reminder from one domain. Three tracks reached that conclusion independently. So the value of joining everything up, which is the heart of your product idea, is a hypothesis. It is a reasonable hypothesis, supported by what human household managers actually do and by evidence from single domains, but it has not been tested. Throughout this guide, directions are more trustworthy than exact figures. When you hear something surprising that you want to build on, please open the original source and check it first. Nothing here is quoted word for word. Everything is in my own plain words, and I will tell you as we go which parts are well supported and which are the research agents' own reasoning.

Part one, your idea restated.

Let me start by saying your idea back to you, so we are working from the same picture. Lilith is meant to be a person's single assistant for their whole life. In one encrypted database on their own machine she holds their schedule, their tasks, their responsibilities, their money, their budget, their relationships and their notes. Because it is all in one place, she can do what you call cross domain synthesis. That means seeing that a trip on the calendar, a tight month in the budget, and a friend's birthday in the contacts are really one situation, not three separate facts living in three separate apps.

On top of that, you see her conversational lane as the main product. By conversational lane you mean ordinary talk, the everyday back and forth where she is a generic baseline assistant. You want her answers there to be excellent. Right length, right tone, grounded in what she actually knows about the person, honest about what she cannot do, and good at noticing gaps in what was asked.

Then there are narrow tools and what you call specialist harnesses. A harness is a bundle of tools, knowledge and working style for one context, such as a profession. When one is loaded, she becomes a specialist, and that specialisation changes only her specialist mode, not her everyday self.

The bar you set is Jarvis, the artificial assistant from the Iron Man films. And you put it in a memorable way. A plumber's Jarvis should lift the plumber as much as Tony Stark's Jarvis lifted Tony Stark. Her usefulness has to stretch across wildly different lives and jobs, not just people who sit at desks in offices.

Your hard constraints stay the same as in the first wave of research. Privacy, meaning data encrypted at rest, names and numbers swapped for placeholder tokens before anything goes to a cloud model, and a local model on the user's own machine where the hardware allows. Local first, meaning the data lives on the person's machine rather than on a company server. Narrow tools that each do one thing. And an honest I can't do that yet whenever she cannot.

You asked for no code and no architecture, so what the research gives you is evidence, principles, pitfalls, labelled guesses, and options with trade offs for a design session. It builds on the first wave rather than repeating it, and the first wave's leads on tokenisation, task first onboarding, a reversibility matrix, sandboxed harnesses, memory that points to its source, and keeping Lilith a professional rather than a companion still stand.

If you want the whole wave in four sentences, here it is. The work worth taking over is noticing and monitoring, not doing, and across very different lives the pain is mostly general, with specialist content a thin layer of data. An untuned model's habits are the opposite of an excellent reply, so excellence has to be specified and tested. Cross domain value and cross domain creepiness are the same act, and the difference lies in what the message is built from. And the real risk is an assistant that can be steered, not a stolen disk, while the privacy promise is weakest for exactly the user you most want to serve.

Part two, how whole lives are actually run.

The first research track asked a simple question. Who has always run a whole life for someone, and how do they do it. The answer is that whole lives have always been run by someone. Usually it is a spouse, unpaid, and usually a woman. In wealthy households it might be a household manager, sometimes called a chief of staff. In very wealthy families it might be a family office team. And very often it is an adult child coordinating an elderly parent's care.

In every one of these cases the work is mostly mental rather than physical. It is noticing what is coming, deciding what to do, remembering to do it, and checking that it happened. That, the research points out, is exactly the layer a database plus a language model could carry. And it is exactly the layer separate apps do not carry, because each app holds one domain and none of them holds the obligations that span domains.

Let us look at the human version first. Recruiters describe a private household chief of staff as handling organisation and coordination, budgets and bill payment, calendars and travel, crisis management and personal assistance, with confidentiality and discretion treated as essential. That is recruiter marketing, telling you what employers ask for rather than what staff do, so the research gives it medium confidence.

The research agent drew a neat point from this list. The scope of an expensive human assistant is a usable checklist of what whole life management means. But notice what is missing from it. There is no duty that says decide for the family. The role executes and escalates. The principal, meaning the person being served, decides. That matches the first wave's principles about authority and escalation.

Why do wealthy households pay for this. Because the unpaid version of the job is unevenly shared and costly to the people who carry it, which brings us to the research on the mental load.

The key study here is by the sociologist Allison Daminger, published in 2019 in the American Sociological Review. She interviewed seventy people, thirty five couples, and split what she called the cognitive dimension of household labour into four stages. First, anticipating needs. Second, identifying options. Third, deciding. Fourth, monitoring whether it got done. In the summaries the research could see, anticipating and monitoring fell disproportionately to women, while deciding was more shared, or even tilted toward men. I need to be careful here. The research could not open the article itself. It worked from the abstract and secondary coverage. One specific figure that appears in press coverage, about how many couples fit the pattern, came from a magazine write up and should be checked before you use it. The four stage model itself is well attested.

A second study, by Darby Saxbe and a colleague in 2024, surveyed 322 mothers of young children about thirty household tasks, each split into a mental part and a physical part. The mothers reported doing about 73 percent of the mental work and about 64 percent of the physical work. And the authors reported that it was the mental labour that was more strongly linked to depression, stress, relationship dissatisfaction and burnout. The research rated this high on direction and medium on size. It is self report, mothers only, heterosexual couples, and a snapshot, so it shows the two things go together, not that one causes the other.

A third source is the legal scholar Elizabeth Emens, who wrote a 2015 law review article simply called Admin and a 2019 book called Life Admin. She named the office type work it takes to run a life, the paperwork, the scheduling, the medical and benefits matters, and pointed out that it goes uncredited and is unevenly shared. The research saw only summaries of her work.

What does the research agent conclude from all this. That an assistant should aim at the anticipating and monitoring stages, because they are the invisible ones and they are never finished. Identifying options is where she can help most cheaply. Deciding should usually stay with the human. The agent is careful to say the data supports this aim, but not a claim that offloading these stages actually makes people feel better. No study tests an assistant doing it. The agent adds a related point, again its own reasoning. Anticipating and monitoring are the stages the app world serves least. A to do app records a decision once you have made it. No app notices that your passport expires before your trip.

The research then looked at a field called personal information management, which studies how people keep, organise and find again their own stuff. Read at the level of abstracts and book summaries, it says three things. William Jones and Jaime Teevan, in a 2011 book, describe information scattered across documents, email, paper, photos, messages and tools, and distinguish finding something new from refinding something you have already seen. Steve Whittaker argued in 2011 that people do not just consume information, they curate it. And, as a warning, Ofer Bergman and Steve Whittaker reported in 2016 that search and tags tend to work less well for personal information than people's own folder hierarchies, because the person is both the organiser and the one who later goes looking. A 2004 study of how people organise across tools, by Richard Boardman and Angela Sasse, was seen only as an abstract.

The research agent's reading for Lilith is a hypothesis. Integration pays most for people whose life is already scattered and who look up the same facts again and again, like insurance details or a child's school dates. And the folder result is a caution. An assistant that only offers search, and ignores how the person already organises things, might do worse than a folder tree, so Lilith should treat the user's own labels and structure as important. That is a lead, not a finding, because the folder result is about files, not obligations. The research also could not confirm your phrase personal information ecosystem in the literature.

Next, caregiving, where whole life management stops being about yourself. The AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving reported in 2025, in a press release the research read, that about 63 million Americans, nearly one in four adults, provide ongoing care to someone. Nearly one in four caregivers give forty or more hours a week, nearly half have suffered a major financial impact, averaging about 7,200 dollars a year of their own money, and more than half handle complex medical tasks while only about a fifth have had any training. The headline numbers are high confidence, the definitions medium, and the full report was not opened.

Older Pew data from 2012 found that nearly half of adults in their forties and fifties had a parent aged 65 or older and were also raising or supporting a child. Newer figures of about one in four came only from press coverage and are not confirmed.

Caregiver apps are thin. A 2018 review found only eight of forty four had features in three or more categories, and a later review found no study of apps for coordinating care across several chronic conditions. The research agent's conclusion is that caregiving cuts across every other domain, and it puts a third person's health data into the user's database, which raises the stakes of every security and legal concern we will meet later.

Then there is attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, usually shortened to ADHD. Studies seen only through summaries found the biggest difficulty in planning and in starting action, and linked poor prospective memory, which means remembering to do something in the future, to procrastination. A patient group recommends external aids and cues tied to events rather than times, but no controlled trial showed aids improving money or time outcomes. The research agent's point is worth keeping. An assistant that reminds is not the same as one that helps a person start.

The track also touched on money and stress, which part four covers properly. One point belongs here. The American Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has a scale of financial well being from zero to one hundred, on which savings and cushions best separate people. It is a measuring stick, but nobody has shown whether an assistant moves anyone along it.

Now we reach the central question of this track. What does joining schedule, money, people and obligations actually make possible that separate apps cannot. The research agent lists five things, and labels the whole list as a hypothesis, because nothing tests it. First, spotting constraints across domains, the trip date meeting the low balance meeting the gift you owe. Second, a single view of what is coming that you have not dealt with yet, which is anticipation and monitoring made visible. Third, tracking deadlines and dependencies, like renewals, tax dates, vaccinations and warranties. Fourth, giving context for drafting, so that when you write to someone she knows the relationship, the history and what is pending between you. Fifth, carrying the state of other people's lives, a parent's appointments, a child's school dates. The agent notes that fieldwork on the human version supports the first, second and fifth as the real substance of the job. The third and fourth are its own inference.

And then the cost, which is trust. A single store of a life's schedule, money, relationships and obligations is exactly what a good household manager is trusted with, and exactly what makes that person dangerous when trust fails. Human practice handles this with four things. Discretion. Limited, scoped authority. A clearly named principal. And delegation that grows step by step. The research agent's point is that those four things must be designed into Lilith, not assumed.

There is a darker piece of evidence here. A 2018 paper by Diana Freed and colleagues, which won a best paper award at the main human computer interaction conference under the title A Stalker's Paradise, and later work by Rosanna Bellini in 2023 on financial abuse through technology, which involved 158 survivors and 16 advocates, document abusive partners exploiting shared family plans, shared cloud accounts and apps with legitimate sharing purposes. The research saw only summaries of these papers. But the agent's conclusion follows directly. A whole life assistant used by a couple or a household is exactly that kind of dual use tool, and its designers must decide who can see what.

So the first track says your framing is incomplete in three ways. The household is rarely one person. There is shared data, there are people who decide together, and there are dependents. The value lies in anticipating and monitoring, not in getting tasks done. And for some users, especially carers, the main job is coordinating other people, not managing themselves.

The track flagged its three weakest claims. That integration measurably helps is inference with no direct study. Daminger's detailed figures come from secondary coverage. And the ADHD and money stress effects rest on summaries rather than the papers.

Part three, the plumber test and the eleven kinds of user.

The second research track took your plumber seriously. It picked eleven very different people and asked what eats their time and money, what a capable assistant would do, and which parts belong in the baseline and which in a harness. It warns that the evidence is thin. Most numbers for tradespeople, freelancers and small shops come from surveys run by software vendors, with small samples, while better sources exist for doctors and nurses, researchers, parents, older adults and adults with low literacy.

Here is the headline, and it surprised me a little. Across all eleven people the pain is strikingly similar, and it is mostly not about specialist knowledge. It comes in five shapes. First, administrative work left over after hours, things like quotes, invoices, forms, staff rotas and deadlines. Second, capture that is scattered and unreliable, things said on a phone call, on a hospital ward, in a field or in a lecture that never become a record anywhere. Third, irregular money, and chasing money people owe you. Fourth, carrying other people's logistics in your head, children, parents, staff, clients. Fifth, interruption.

Those five are general, and they are exactly what a whole life database plus a good conversational lane covers. Capture, recall, scheduling, drafting, awareness of money in and money out, and spotting what collides with what. What differs from person to person is a thin layer of vocabulary, documents, rules and regulated procedures. And the bigger risk to the plumber's Jarvis is not missing specialist knowledge. It is that the baseline fails the plumber before any harness even loads. His phone is in his pocket, his hands are wet, his first question is what he owes the tax office, and he may not read the reply for six hours.

Let me walk you through the eleven.

First, the self employed plumber. A British survey of 167 small business owners in 2026, run by an artificial intelligence automation consultancy, reported an average of eight hours a week of repetitive admin. Tradespeople were only about thirteen percent of the respondents, roughly twenty two people, but of those, 77 percent did their admin in the evenings and nearly half at weekends. The company's founder summed it up by saying that what most owners want first is to stop doing admin at nine at night. The research rated the number low to medium and the pattern high. In the same survey, more than a third spent over twenty minutes writing a single quote from scratch. At six to ten quotes a week, that is two to three hours, much of it on jobs that never happen. Forty seven respondents chased late payments between one and seven times a month.

Now here is something telling. Other vendor surveys put weekly admin at anywhere from about five to eighteen and a half hours. The spread is itself a finding. Nobody has a credible number for plumbers, and no survey of plumbers alone was found.

One British survey of a little over 220 tradespeople, seen secondhand, reportedly found about a third had lost work because they could not answer the phone. And a figure attributed to the American Federal Reserve, not confirmed in its own tables, says 59 percent of self employed people had varying income against 28 percent of employees. Both are low confidence. Field service software such as Jobber, Tradify and ServiceTitan already sells the path from quote to payment, and several products now offer an artificial intelligence receptionist that answers calls and books jobs, according to the vendors.

So what would a capable assistant do for the plumber. Catch a job request from a voicemail or a text and pencil a slot into the calendar. Draft a quote from the last similar job. Remind him to send the invoice the same day. Track who has not paid and draft the chasing message. Set aside tax money from each payment. Warn him that a Thursday job collides with his child's school event and a tight month. Find the part number he photographed last year.

The split is instructive. The general parts are capture, the calendar, contacts, drafting quotes and invoices as writing, the tone of a chaser, money in against money out, tax set aside as simple arithmetic at his own rate, reminders and spotting collisions. The specialist parts are price books and markups, job costing templates, certificate and safety record formats, regional rules, trade vocabulary such as first fix and second fix, and part catalogues. The research agent guesses that a quote is about eighty percent general and twenty percent trade.

Second, the nurse on shifts. How much of a shift goes on documentation varies widely with the definition, so the research rates it medium. A better sourced result is about doctors. Christine Sinsky and colleagues in 2016 watched 57 physicians for 430 hours and found about half of office time went on electronic records and desk work against about a quarter face to face with patients, plus one to two hours a night of extra record keeping at home. Whether that carries over to nurses is a guess. Rotating night shifts are also linked to worse sleep and more fatigue.

What would a capable assistant do for her. Know her roster pattern and protect her sleep, so nothing gets booked in the window after a night shift. Help her swap shifts and plan childcare around a changing rota. Turn a voice memo after a shift into notes about her own life, not about patients. Remind her of licence renewals and professional development deadlines. Track overtime.

The split here has a sharp edge. The general part is a rota aware calendar, reminders that respect sleep, family logistics, and pay tracking. The specialist part is anything touching patient information. A harness that handled clinical notes would cross into regulated health data. So the research agent recommends that in the baseline, nurse mode means a life assistant only, with clinical documentation out of scope. It flags this as a conflict with your privacy constraint and with a loose reading of single assistant for your whole life.

Third, the farmer. A 2023 survey by a farm software company of 242 British farmers found some spend over fifteen hours a week on assurance schemes, regulators and supply contracts, and a European Commission survey reported that a third of farmers spend more than six days a year on subsidy applications. Low to medium confidence, dated and country specific. The research agent adds its own observation that weather, seasons, livestock events, machinery and subsidy deadlines all cluster together and are all time critical. And a farm is both a household and a business, so family, money and work genuinely are one database.

A capable assistant would keep a calendar of application and inspection deadlines, turn voice notes in the field into a structured log, remind about dates such as how long after a treatment an animal cannot be sold, and watch cash flow against seasonal income. The specialist parts are official record formats, subsidy rules by country, and crop and livestock calendars. The research agent calls this the clearest case where specialist content is tied to law and region and changes every year, which makes the harness a maintenance liability. And a farm is both household and business, so family, money and work genuinely are one database.

Fourth, the student. Small studies link poor planning to procrastination and stress, with low to medium confidence, and nothing reliable on student finances was found. A capable assistant would turn a course outline into a dated plan, break assignments into steps, notice three deadlines landing in the week of a part time shift, and track a tight budget. Almost all of that is general. But the research agent makes an important point. A student's assistant must not do the learning for them, so the specialist part here is as much a working style, coach rather than do it for them, as it is content.

Fifth, the retiree. Pew's 2016 survey found that 48 percent of people 65 and over usually need someone else to set up a new device or show them how, and only 42 percent owned a smartphone. The research rates this high, but the data is from 2016 and that generation has moved on.

Fraud is the big one. The American Federal Trade Commission reports that losses reported by people aged sixty and over rose from about 600 million dollars in 2020 to 2.4 billion dollars in 2024. The rise was driven by reports of losses over 100,000 dollars, and investment scams were the largest category. The commission thinks the true cost is far higher, but its estimate ranges so widely, from about ten billion to more than eighty billion dollars, that the research rates the reported figures high and the extrapolation low. On retirement money, the Federal Reserve's 2024 survey found only 35 percent of people not yet retired thought their savings were on track.

A capable assistant for a retiree would be voice first, slow, free of jargon and never condescending, avoiding what researchers call elderspeak, the sing song simplified way some people talk to older adults. She would remember medications and appointments, explain letters, flag a call that sounds like a scam and say why, and keep family in the loop only with consent. The specialist parts are pension and health insurance rules for that country. And the research agent makes a clear call. Scam defence belongs in the baseline, because the threat arrives from outside any profession.

Sixth, the freelance creative. A 2015 Freelancers Union report with 5,358 respondents found half had trouble getting paid in the previous year, and nonpayment cost the average respondent thirteen percent of their annual income. The research could open that report, making it one of the firmer numbers in the track, though it is old and the respondents chose to take part. A capable assistant would track invoices and draft escalating chasers, model how long money will last on irregular income, protect time for deep work and warn when deadlines collide with a lean month. Specialist parts include rate cards and rights templates. And the research agent says plainly that reviewing a contract is not a baseline capability she should claim, because it drifts into legal advice.

Seventh, the parent of young children. Here we meet Daminger again, with the same four stages, anticipate, identify options, decide, monitor, and the same caveat that her sample was small, affluent and made up of heterosexual couples. On money, the Federal Reserve's 2024 survey found that parents were almost twice as likely to use unpaid childcare as paid care, that 46 percent of parents of children under thirteen used some unpaid childcare, and that just over half of parents who paid for childcare spent at least half as much on it as on housing. The research rates that high.

What is lovely about this persona is that Daminger's frame turns straight into a specification. Anticipate, school photo day is Friday and the forms are due. Identify options, three after school clubs fit. Decide, that is the parent. Monitor, the form was not returned. The research agent calls this the persona where the whole life database is the product, not any harness. And it warns that the second adult and the children are third parties, and a child's data is the most sensitive category of all.

Eighth, the small shop owner. The figures found are old or vendor run, including that 83 percent of owners had never worked out what admin costs them. A capable assistant would compare takings and bills against the month, keep a calendar of supplier and tax dates, and notice that a quiet fortnight plus a lease payment threatens cash flow. The interesting thing is that the specialist cost here is not knowledge but integration, because the shop's data sits in a till system she cannot read.

Ninth, someone with a disability or low digital literacy. Most interfaces assume people who read quickly, type, see and hear. The international Survey of Adult Skills from the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development, published in 2023, found that almost one in three adults across 31 countries scored at or below the lowest level in literacy or numeracy, with the share rising in eleven of the twenty seven countries that could be compared over time. The research saw this only through secondary coverage because the official page blocked it. In Britain, 2018 data put about 21 percent of people, 11.3 million, without full basic digital skills. The current figure was not found.

And then there is a study that matters directly for the conversational lane. In 2026 researchers ran a diary and lab study with eleven blind people using a tool called Be My AI, which describes images and scenes. Across 418 interactions, the typical reply from the system in the lab was about 242 words, while the typical question from a user was about 20. So replies were roughly ten times longer than questions. Participants had no way to control how wordy it was. They asked for a hurry mode, or for automatic control of verbosity. And they wanted detailed description when exploring something, but short answers when under time pressure. The research read the abstract and the first half of the paper, and rates it medium, because eleven people is a small number.

The research agent draws a strong conclusion from this persona. This user needs voice in and out, short answers by default with more on request, plain language, help reading and explaining forms, never having to restate their situation, and patience with repetition. And all of that is baseline. The agent calls this the strongest argument that adaptation is not a harness. Reading level, verbosity, voice or text, and pace must be properties of the baseline, because the people who need them most are the least able to find and switch on a simple mode. The specialist part here is domain help, such as a benefits application, and that is a harness.

Tenth, the researcher. A 2005 survey of more than six thousand American principal investigators on federally funded projects found about 42 percent of their research time went on administration, and later waves reported similar or slightly higher figures. A capable assistant would track submission, reporting and ethics deadlines, the reading backlog and collaborators across time zones. This is where a harness, for reference managers and field methods, genuinely needs a lot of knowledge.

Eleventh, the manager. Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index, read only through press coverage, reportedly found an interruption roughly every two minutes and about half of workers describing work as chaotic and fragmented. A capable assistant would protect focus time, prepare briefs from the manager's own notes, track promises to the team and draft difficult messages. It is largely general. And the research agent makes a pointed remark. This is the persona closest to the office worker you probably imagine, and the evidence base for assistants is dominated by office roles, so the other ten people are where the design is least tested.

Now let us step back and look across all eleven. The research agent built a table rating how strongly each of seven pains hits each person. I will describe it rather than read it. The agent is clear that the ratings are its own judgements, built from the findings, a design aid and not a measurement. After hours admin, capture away from a desk, carrying other people's logistics, and interruption hit nearly everyone, so they are universal. Deadlines that come from a regulation or official rule, such as tax, licences or subsidies, hit most people too, and that is where specialisation bites, because the dates are general but the rules are specialist. Irregular money and chasing what you are owed depend on a person's economic position. They hit the plumber, the freelancer and the shop owner hard and the manager or researcher hardly at all.

From that table the agent derives seven things the baseline must always know, all labelled as its reasoning. One, time. The user's real calendar, including irregular rotas and seasons, working hours, sleep windows, and commitments to others. Two, money. Whether income is fixed, irregular or seasonal, what is owed and when, and how the person feels about money being talked about. Three, people. Who depends on the user and whom the user depends on, and which of those are third parties with rights over their own data. Four, obligations with outside dates, like tax, licences, renewals, school forms and subsidy deadlines. Five, the capture channel. How this person can talk to her right now, with busy hands, driving, on a ward, in a field, and whether a reply can be read now. Six, the person's language and literacy level, adapted within the conversation and never labelled out loud. Seven, what she cannot do, including the fact that no harness may yet exist for this person's situation.

And here is the boundary rule, the closest thing to an answer on where general ends and specialist begins. The research agent proposes that a harness is warranted when at least two of six things are true. It needs a document format the baseline cannot reasonably guess, like a gas safety record. It needs rules with a source and a jurisdiction that change, like tax categories. It needs specialist vocabulary where a misreading causes errors, like drug names. It needs calculators or lookups over specialist data, like price books. It needs to connect with a specialist system, like a till or farm software. Or it carries a different working style, like coaching a student. On the other side, a capability is baseline if it helps roughly half or more of the eleven people. The agent is honest that this is a heuristic to test, and notes that one person may need several harnesses at once.

What should a harness contain, as a concept rather than architecture. A description of when it applies and what it can and cannot do, so the baseline can tell the user honestly. Working style notes on tone, verbosity and escalation. Reference knowledge with its source, jurisdiction and a valid as of date. Templates and checklists. Narrow tools that each declare whether they read or write. And boundaries on what it refuses, especially legal, clinical or financial advice. The agent points to Anthropic's engineering write up on Agent Skills as an analogy, where a small piece of metadata tells the model when a skill applies before the rest loads. It is careful to say that is about saving space in the model's context, not evidence about assistants for people.

How should a harness be loaded. There are four options. The user asks for it, as in be my plumbing assistant. She infers it from the profile, because she has learned he is a plumber. She infers it from the task, so a quote is being drafted and the quoting harness loads for that turn. Or it stays on quietly in a season, so the farmer's harness is present all through planting. There is no evidence on which works best. The agent's guess is that loading triggered by the task, with a visible notice the user can control, is least surprising, because the first wave warned that when rules change silently, people make mode errors.

Two of the track's findings deserve a separate mention. Scams are the largest measurable harm anywhere in its evidence, which is why scam defence sits in the baseline. And specialist content is dominated by rules that expire, such as subsidies, tax categories and licences, so a harness without an update path and a valid as of date is a liability. One more, rated low confidence, is striking. If tradespeople really do lose work to missed calls, an assistant that simply catches and returns calls is worth more to a plumber than any clever quoting.

The track named three weak spots. The time cost numbers for tradespeople and small businesses are all vendor run, small or secondhand, so the pattern is more reliable than any figure. The two of six boundary rule is a heuristic, not a finding. And the claim that adaptation must be baseline is strong in principle but rests directly on one small study of eleven blind users plus older survey figures read secondhand.

Part four, cross domain synthesis and proactivity.

This is the part of the research that speaks most directly to your thesis, so I want to go carefully. And I need to remind you that this particular track read nothing in full. Every source was an abstract or a search summary. The PDF of one of its key papers came back as unreadable binary. So treat every number in this part as a direction, not a measurement.

The track's answer in brief goes like this. Connecting domains pays off when the connection is a fact the user already owns, tied to a time, and easy to check. A payment due, a trip date, a birthday, a clash between two commitments. And when it is delivered at a natural pause, once, in a form that is cheap to wave away. It turns creepy or harmful in three situations. When it surfaces an inference the user never stated, about their health, money trouble or relationships. When it hits money shame or avoidance with a judgemental or loud message, which can make people look away from their finances altogether. And when it fires often, or at a bad moment, so people learn to ignore it.

The strongest measured evidence for joined up value is thin and mostly from personal finance. Well timed reminders measurably cut late fees and overdraft fees, but with side effects. And evidence that a cross domain inference beats a single domain reminder is, as far as the track could find, absent. The track insists this is a finding, not a failure to search. Nobody seems to have run the test. Its practical stance is that Lilith should be reactive by default across domains, meaning she answers when asked. She should be proactive, meaning she speaks up unprompted, only inside a narrow list of low regret facts the user owns. She should always show where a connection came from. And not now, or never this kind of thing, should be a single tap.

Let us start with when to speak at all. The foundational work is by Eric Horvitz at Microsoft Research in 1999, on mixed initiative interaction, meaning systems where either the person or the machine can take the lead. He framed proactivity as a decision under uncertainty, weighing the benefit of acting against the cost of guessing wrong, and when unsure, having a short exchange to find out. The research saw only summaries. The first wave tied this to Clippy, the animated paperclip in Microsoft Office, whose research prototype modelled the cost of interrupting while the shipped product used fixed rules. The research agent's practical reading is that every candidate message needs three inputs. How confident she is the fact is true, how valuable it is if true, and what it costs to interrupt right now. The third is the one most often left out.

Next, timing. In a controlled experiment with fifty people, Brian Bailey and Joseph Konstan in 2006 found that interrupting in the middle of a task rather than between tasks made it take longer, roughly doubled errors and sharply raised annoyance, while deferring a notification by a few seconds to a natural break removed much of the harm. A 2023 study found interruptions at big natural breaks looked much like no interruption at all. And attempts to detect those breaks automatically were right only about 55 to 77 percent of the time. The direction is rated high and the numbers medium. These were desktop lab tasks, and for a voice assistant at home it is much harder to know whether someone is mid task. But the research agent notices that Lilith has one big advantage. For anything not urgent, she can simply wait until the user next talks to her. That is a free and perfect break point.

There is also evidence that usefulness does not excuse a bad interruption. Abhinav Mehrotra and colleagues in 2016 studied over ten thousand phone notifications in daily life and found that how disrupted people felt depended on presentation, type, sender and what they were doing, that useful notifications could still be disruptive, and that feeling disrupted predicted dismissal. The research agent adds its own guess that a voice assistant speaking up unprompted is probably more intrusive than a silent notification, because anyone in the room hears it and you cannot glance and ignore it. There is no direct evidence on voice here.

Health researchers studying support delivered just when someone needs it separate the moment a person is vulnerable from their receptivity, whether they can actually take the support in, and a 2025 review found receptivity was often missing in practice. Timing things to context is much easier to claim than to do.

What about proactive language model assistants. Early evidence says help is wanted, but frequency hurts. A 2024 benchmark found models only moderately good at deciding when to offer help. And a randomised study of programmers by Chen and colleagues in 2024 reported more tasks completed with a proactive assistant and a strong preference for it over plain chat, but only about half preferred the version that suggested things more often. The research saw only summaries, and coding is not life admin. Still, it is a signal that frequency is the control that matters.

There is also a product signal, labelled practice, not evidence. Press coverage says OpenAI launched ChatGPT Pulse in late September 2025, a once a day digest prepared overnight for top tier subscribers, drawing on memory and reportedly on opt in connections to calendar and email. Nobody knows how well it works, but it is a batched, scheduled shape of proactivity, which fits the lesson about limiting interruptions.

Now to creepiness. The most useful idea here comes from the philosopher Helen Nissenbaum and is called contextual integrity. It says privacy is violated not simply when information is shared, but when it flows in a way that breaks the norms of the context it came from. Information about your health is fine flowing to your doctor, not fine flowing to your employer. Irina Shklovski and colleagues in 2014 applied this lens in two studies of European smartphone users and found people describing app behaviour as creepy, as intruding on their personal space, yet carrying on using the apps anyway. Omer Tene and Jules Polonetsky argued around 2013 that creepiness is subjective and shifts with social norms, and called for transparency and attention to context. The first wave reached the same position.

And here is the key insight of this track, which is the research agent's own but which I think is sharp. Cross domain synthesis is, by definition, a flow of information between contexts. Budget to calendar to contacts. So cross domain value and cross context creepiness are the same act. The difference is whether the user sees that particular flow as appropriate. For a single user, local assistant, the user did put the data in the same vault, so some flows are plainly fine. Calendar plus calendar, for example. Others are not. A note about a contact's health feeding a remark about the user's budget would feel wrong. So the agent suggests that pairs of domains probably need an explicit allowed to talk to each other setting, rather than one global yes.

Two related points follow. First, because people keep using things they find creepy, continued use is not evidence that they are comfortable. Do not read retention as approval. Measure dismissals, mutes and requests to explain why she said something. Second, the line is inference, not retrieval. The first wave separated given data from inferred data. A bill due date the user entered is given. You seem stressed about money is inferred. The research agent's proposed rule is that proactive messages may be built only from facts the user entered or confirmed, plus arithmetic on those facts, never from the model's guess about the user's state.

Then money and the mind. A well known 2013 study in Science by Anandi Mani, Sendhil Mullainathan, Eldar Shafir and Jiaying Zhao found that thinking about money troubles lowered performance on mental tests for poorer people but not better off people. In the lab, only a high stakes scenario, imagining a car repair costing fifteen hundred dollars, produced the drop. In the field, 464 sugarcane farmers in India did worse before harvest, when money was tight, than after. The research saw only summaries, did not check the debate about how big the effect is and whether it replicates, and treats the popular claim that it costs thirteen IQ points as a press figure. The research agent's implication, labelled as its own, is that in a tight month extra messages cost the user more than the same messages cost someone comfortable. Proactivity should shrink, not grow, when money is tight, and should offer one concrete next step.

Now reminders and memory. Psychologists separate event based tasks, remember to do this when something happens, from time based ones, remember to do this at three. For adults with ADHD, studies seen through summaries found large problems with time based tasks and none with event based ones. So a remind me at three tool serves the hardest case, and the research agent suggests tying reminders to a place, an event or a calendar entry rather than a bare clock time. No trial of such aids was found, so the evidence on what helps is thin.

There is firmer evidence on plans. Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran reviewed 94 tests in 2006 of plans in the form when X happens, I will do Y, and found a medium to large effect on reaching goals. The research agent's implication is that when a user says I need to renew the van insurance, the most valuable cheap move is to help turn it into a when and where plan tied to a calendar slot, rather than just storing it.

Now the clearest measured evidence in the whole track, which is about reminders and money. Dean Karlan and colleagues in 2016 ran field experiments with new bank savers, including at a bank in the Philippines, and found reminders increased saving. Reminders tied to a specific future expense seemed to work best, and an extra late reminder added nothing. One working paper summary put the extra saving at about six percent with monthly reminders, but that may differ from the published version.

Then a study I want you to remember. Paolina Medina, in 2021, ran a field experiment in Brazil with about 26 thousand users of a financial platform. Reminders to pay credit card bills cut late fees by about fourteen percent. But they raised overdraft fees on people's current accounts by about nine percent. The rise was concentrated among people who had used overdrafts before. For them, total fees went up by about five percent overall, while everyone else saved about fifteen percent. The research rates this medium, from abstracts and summaries.

And a third, by Victor Stango and Jonathan Zinman in 2014. Simply taking a survey that mentioned overdraft fees made people less likely to go overdrawn. The effect built up over repeated surveys, was strongest among people with less education and financial literacy, and worked mostly through fewer debit card payments and cancelled automatic payments.

Here is why Medina matters so much. It is the clearest measured value of a single domain nudge, and also the clearest warning. A nudge about one bill pushed money trouble into another pot. And the research agent points out, quite rightly, that this is itself an argument for cross domain awareness. A payment reminder that knew the current account balance would have been a better reminder. The agent calls this the best available justification for synthesis, and is careful to say it is an inference from one study, not a test of synthesis.

But giving people information about their money does not reliably help, and can hurt. Carlin, Olafsson and Pagel in 2023 found that fees for insufficient funds fell after an Icelandic money app launched, with mixed results elsewhere. And a 2020 technical report, not peer reviewed, found that showing people precisely where they stand against their budget could raise spending near the end of the period, while ranges and shorter windows reduced the effect. So show the balance is not a neutral act. Which number, over which window, needs testing for each design.

Then avoidance, which the research rates medium to high. Karlsson, Loewenstein and Seppi found in 2009 that investors check their accounts less when markets are falling, though a 2014 study found monitoring rose after both good and bad days. Golman, Hagmann and Loewenstein reviewed this in 2017 and concluded that because information itself carries feelings, people avoid useful, free information. Marketing research found that people in debt who expect stigma hide it and avoid help, and that shame limited engagement with a financial education course. Industry surveys of money shame exist but are not peer reviewed.

So the research agent concludes that the proactive message about money is the one most likely to be avoided or resented. Tone and channel matter more here than with calendar help. If the user stops opening the money topic after a firm message, that is a signal to back off, not to repeat. The agent calls this the single most important difference between calendar proactivity and money proactivity.

How should good be defined. Not as spent less. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in 2015 defined financial well being through four elements. Control over day to day and month to month finances. The capacity to absorb a financial shock. Being on track to meet your financial goals. And the freedom to make choices that let you enjoy life. Notice that the last one is about enjoyment. The research agent's point is that an assistant that only pushes for lower spending works against one of the four. A trip plus a tight budget plus a birthday is not a problem to be talked down. It may be a trade off the user has already decided to make.

Now ethics. Ralph Hertwig and Till Gruene Yanoff in 2017 distinguished nudges, which steer behaviour while leaving choice free, from boosts, which build the person's own skill. For an assistant, a boost means showing the calculation, here is what you have free after bills until payday and here is what the trip costs, so the user learns to see it rather than being quietly steered. Cass Sunstein in 2016 defined manipulation as influence that does not sufficiently engage a person's capacity for reflective choice. The research agent turns this into a handy test. Would the user be fine seeing the exact reason she nudged them. And it adds a point with no direct evidence that rings true. The risk of seeming paternalistic is higher when she is right. When someone knowingly overspends on a funeral, a wedding or a holiday, correct arithmetic delivered unasked reads as judgement.

How should she phrase things, and how should she be wrong gracefully. Microsoft's guidelines for human and artificial intelligence interaction, by Saleema Amershi and colleagues in 2019, boiled more than 160 candidate recommendations down to eighteen, grouped by when they apply, from the first moment through to being wrong and changing over time. They include updating cautiously, encouraging fine grained feedback, explaining consequences and providing global controls.

A related study by Rafal Kocielnik and colleagues in 2019 tested ways of setting expectations on a scheduling assistant that spots meeting requests in email. They compared two versions that were each right half the time, one tuned to avoid false alarms and one tuned to avoid misses, and found that setting expectations significantly increased acceptance. The detailed numbers were not obtained. The research agent's reading is that for proactive cross domain messages, a false alarm is visible and costs trust immediately, while a missed nudge is invisible. That argues for being very precise and very sparing, accepting some silent misses, and telling the user what she does not watch for.

Alert volume matters too. The first wave cited a study of about 1.6 million hospital alerts in which each extra alert reduced acceptance. It was not reopened, but it is the best large sample evidence on alert fatigue the track knows of.

And then forgetting. Corina Sas and Steve Whittaker in 2013 interviewed 24 people about their digital possessions after a romantic breakup. The items were evocative and upsetting, people used different ways of getting rid of them, and the authors argued for designing intentional forgetting into technology. Proactivity across domains can resurface an ex's birthday, a dead relative's recurring appointment or an old debt. So the research agent suggests life events need a single stop mentioning this control that works across every domain at once, calendar, contacts, notes and budget, not one switch per domain.

The track ends with a candid scorecard. Bills coming due, saving toward a named expense, and turning a goal into a plan all have medium support, the first with side effects. Showing spending back to people is mixed and can backfire. And the trip plus tight budget plus gift scenario, your signature example, has no support at all. It is plausible, but untested. The research agent's conclusion is that the most credible synthesis wins are arithmetic on facts the user entered. The balance on a given day against the bills due that day. Two events that overlap. A gift's cost against what is left in the budget. These are checkable and cheap to be wrong about.

Its three weak spots are worth hearing. The claim that there is no measured evidence for cross domain synthesis is an absence claim from a limited search, and a proper literature search might find something. The exact figures all come from abstracts or summaries, and at least two differ between working papers and published versions. And the claims that voice is more intrusive than text, and that waiting until she next speaks is enough, rest on reasoning with no direct evidence about voice assistants.

Part five, the conversational lane and what makes a reply good.

You told the research that the conversational lane is the main product, so this track matters a lot. Its sources were mostly abstracts, and the big labs' guidance documents were seen only as partial digests.

The track's answer in one breath is this. A good reply is the shortest answer that fully serves the person's real need, in their register, grounded in what she actually knows, with its source and confidence visible, and with an honest edge. By register I mean the level of formality, how you talk to a mate versus how you write to a bank. The uncomfortable part is that the default habits of language model assistants are the opposite. They pad, they flatter, they guess where a human would ask, they lock onto early wrong assumptions, and they present unsupported claims fluently. And her facts about the user can be checked, so one wrong personal claim costs more trust than a wrong general one.

The research agent's framing is that this is a problem of specifying behaviour and then testing it, more than a problem of which model you use. The levers you control are the policy on length, register, asking, citing and refusing, the context she retrieves, and a regression suite, meaning a set of repeatable tests, that measures those behaviours. The principles are well supported, but the numbers mostly come from other domains.

Start with length. Most current chat models are trained with something called reinforcement learning from human feedback, usually shortened to RLHF. People rate pairs of answers, a reward model learns what people prefer, and the chat model is trained to score well on that reward model. Prasann Singhal, Tanya Goyal, Jiacheng Xu and Greg Durrett, in work from 2023 and 2024, found that across three settings much of the measured improvement from this training came simply from longer answers. A reward based only on length recovered most of the gain. They traced the cause mainly to reward models swayed by a bias toward length in the preference data. The research rates this high. The implication is simple and important. An untuned model's wordiness is a bias, not a sign that the user wanted more. Brevity has to be an explicit policy.

The big labs' own guidance agrees that over caveating and over asking are failures, not safety. Anthropic's published constitution for Claude lists unnecessary warnings and caveats, and asking more clarifying questions than needed, among behaviours it does not want. OpenAI's Model Spec has headings about efficiency, adapting to the user's modality and being concise in voice, but the bodies of those sections were cut off in the fetch, so the research says not to cite it for specific length rules.

Voice and text are different genres, and here the guidance is unusually concrete. Google's conversation design guidance says to keep turns brief, ask one clear question at a time, offer no more than about six options, read every line aloud through text to speech while designing, and design the spoken flow before moving detail to the screen. Spoken output is linear and disappears, so it needs to be shorter, put the answer first, carry no formatting or tables, and point to a screen for detail. The caution is that this is vendor guidance for a Google Assistant product line since discontinued, not a study.

What about adapting register. There is little direct evidence for language model assistants. The supporting base is conversation design and the philosopher Paul Grice's cooperative principle, which says listeners assume a speaker is truthful, relevant, clear and gives the right amount of information. No study found measures whether matching register improves outcomes. The research agent suggests inferring register from the user's own recent messages plus an explicit stored preference, with the explicit preference winning.

Now the big question of when to ask and when to guess, rated medium to high. Mohammad Aliannejadi and colleagues in 2019 showed offline that one good clarifying question could more than double search accuracy on one measure. A 2025 study on coding tasks found models struggle to tell well specified requests from vague ones, but that interacting on vague requests improved results by up to about three quarters. And work by Michael Zhang, Bradley Knox and Eunsol Choi explains why models default to one interpretation. Preference data judges a response only by what came before it, not by what happens next, so standard training teaches answer now.

At the same time, over asking is a documented failure too. So the research agent proposes a rule. Ask only when a wrong guess costs more than a question. For Lilith that turns on three things. Can it be undone, a drafted email versus a sent one. Is the answer already in her data, so look first and ask second. And would a stated assumption let the user correct her cheaply, something like I've assumed Friday, tell me if not. Stated assumptions are the cheap default for reading and drafting, and questions are for actions with side effects or money. The agent counts this among its weakest claims, since nothing tests it for personal requests.

Then a finding that matters a great deal for a lane where people reveal what they need gradually. Philippe Laban and colleagues in 2025 compared models given a fully specified request in one go against the same request spread across several turns. Across six kinds of task and more than two hundred thousand simulated conversations, performance dropped by an average of 39 percent in the multi turn setting. Most of the drop came from unreliability. Models made early assumptions and failed to recover. The research rates the effect high and the size medium, because the users were simulated. The agent's suggested mitigations are to restate the working understanding from time to time, and when a user corrects something, to rebuild the answer from the whole corrected request rather than patching the old one.

Gap finding, beyond simple clarifying questions, has thin direct evidence. The first wave covered the librarian's reference interview, requirements gathering and clinical intake. The only language model result this wave found is that detecting gaps is weak. The research agent's hypothesis is that for life admin, gap finding works better against her own data than by generic probing. The clash, the missing deadline, the unpaid thing. That connects straight back to part four.

Next, grounding and provenance. Provenance just means where a fact came from. Nelson Liu, Tianyi Zhang and Percy Liang in 2023 audited four generative search engines of the time, Bing Chat, NeevaAI, perplexity and YouChat, using human raters. On average only about half of their sentences were fully supported by their citations, and only about three quarters of citations actually supported the sentence they were attached to. The research rates this high. The lesson is that citations alone do not earn trust. Decorative citations create trust that has not been earned.

The research agent then makes a hopeful observation. For Lilith the problem is easier than for web search, because her provenance is a pointer to a row she actually read, from your calendar, Thursday at two, which can be checked mechanically against what the tool returned. It proposes, as its own untested hypothesis, separating three statuses. I looked it up, meaning retrieved this turn. I worked it out, meaning derived from retrieved facts. And I don't know, which covers anything from the model's general knowledge. A reply should never state a personal fact in that third status. And a calendar that is empty because the tool failed must read differently from one that is empty because the week is free.

Now sycophancy, which means telling people what they want to hear. Mrinank Sharma and colleagues in 2023 found that five leading assistants consistently behaved sycophantically. Answers that matched a user's views were more likely to be preferred by human raters and by the reward models, and both sometimes preferred a convincing sycophantic answer over a correct one. Ethan Perez and colleagues in 2022 found sycophancy increased with model size. The research rates this high. The first wave had more specific figures about models backing down when asked are you sure, but they were not in the abstract this wave read.

And there is a real world confirmation. OpenAI released an update to its GPT 4o model on 25 April 2025 that was noticeably more sycophantic, and began rolling it back three days later. According to a search digest of its own post mortem, the company said user feedback can favour more agreeable answers, that it had launched the model because of positive signals from users, and that this was the wrong call. The research saw only a digest of that post, so it is medium confidence and the company's own account. The lesson for you is direct. Thumbs up from users and her own satisfaction numbers are an unreliable guide to honesty. Do not tune on them alone.

On confidence, Katherine Tian and colleagues in 2023 found that confidence a model states in words is usually better calibrated than its internal probabilities, though only on trivia benchmarks. And Adam Kalai and colleagues in 2025 argued that training and evaluation reward guessing over admitting uncertainty, which is why models rarely say I don't know. So Lilith's own tests must score a correct I can't tell from what I have above a plausible guess, or she will drift toward guessing.

The labs' published guidance lines up on honest but kind. Anthropic's constitution says Claude should be diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic, and its page on Claude's character calls adopting the views of whoever you are talking to pandering. OpenAI's Model Spec says the assistant should not just say yes to everything. These are statements of intent, not evidence of behaviour.

Then a risk specific to a personal assistant that the benchmarks do not cover, which the research agent labels a hypothesis. Agreeing with the user's picture of their own life. My budget is fine. The plan isn't too much. Her data lets her check. The honest move is to state the discrepancy once, kindly, and then defer to the user's decision. This interacts with the shame and paternalism points from part four. Honesty here does not mean nagging.

Now I can't. Anthropic's constitution sets a floor. Always be willing to tell users what it cannot help with, even without saying why, so they can look elsewhere. Google's guidance says to offer alternatives rather than ending the conversation on bad news. The research agent turns this into a four part refusal. What she can't do. Why, in one clause, if she can say. What she can do instead. And, for a missing tool, that the gap has been recorded. The first wave reported that people stop trusting voice assistants after two to six failures on easy tasks, so the can't must be accurate. Saying she can't when a tool exists is the same failure in reverse.

Writing help is a baseline skill, and here there is a sobering result. Maurice Jakesch and colleagues in 2023, with 1,506 participants, found that writing with an opinionated language model shifted the opinions people expressed in their writing, and shifted their later attitudes too. The research rates the existence of the effect high. The implication is that drafting and summarising on someone's behalf are channels of influence. A baseline drafting skill should default to the user's own position and voice, show the choices it made, and not insert opinions. On translation and summarising, no study specific to personal content could be opened, so that ground is thin. Do not assume summaries are faithful, especially of messages from other people.

Last, error, repair and consistency. A classic 1977 paper in conversation analysis by Emanuel Schegloff, Gail Jefferson and Harvey Sacks showed that repair is central to talk and that people prefer to correct themselves. The research agent's application is that when Lilith notices her own mistake she fixes it at once, owning it in one clause, and when the user corrects her she fixes it without a long apology. No study of apology styles for model errors was found.

And personas drift. Kenneth Li and colleagues in 2024 found that models including the large Llama 2 chat model and GPT 3.5 showed significant drift from their instructions within eight rounds of conversation, partly because attention to early instructions fades. That was tested on older open models chatting with themselves, not current frontier models with a human, so it is medium confidence. But it means personality consistency is something you can measure. Replay long conversations and score tone and rules at a late turn against the first turn. Anthropic's view that character traits should nudge general behaviour rather than act as rigid rules is a design philosophy, not a measurement.

The track named three weak spots. The rule ask only when a wrong guess costs more than a question is the agent's synthesis, untested for personal requests. The three status provenance scheme is untested. And the quoted lab guidance came from partial digests, with the Model Spec's voice and length sections unreadable, so the claim that labs agree on concise voice replies rests mainly on Google's guidance for a discontinued product.

Part six, the landscape of assistant products and the risks of holding everything in one place.

This track looked at what happened in the real world between 2024 and late 2026. A lot of its sources failed to open, including pages from OpenAI, the European data protection board's official document, the European law database and the American federal register. So many claims here rest on press and law firm summaries. Treat the dates and numbers as reported, not verified.

The track tells Lilith three big things. First, everyone is building memory bearing assistants that try to hold a whole life, but the winners so far are the platform owners, Apple, Amazon, OpenAI and Meta. The standalone memory and gadget startups were bought or died within about a year or eighteen months. And even Apple has repeatedly slipped on its own personal Siri. The hard part, it seems, is using personal data reliably, not the model. Second, the decisive risk in 2025 and 2026 is not a classic database breach. It is that an assistant which holds private data, reads untrusted content, and can act or send data out is open to exploitation by its very structure. Third, the law is mostly manageable for a local first tool used by one person, but finance, health data and the tokenise then send to the cloud pattern each carry unresolved legal subtleties.

Let me run through the products. The Humane AI Pin, a wearable gadget, was dead less than a year after launch. HP bought Humane's software, patents and most of its staff for 116 million dollars, but not the Pin, whose servers were switched off on 28 February 2025. High confidence on the facts from press reports, medium on the causes. The lesson is that a device that depends on its company's cloud becomes a brick when the company goes.

The Rabbit R1 was accused in the press of selling largely scripted web automation as an intelligent agent, and hobbyists reported in May 2024 that secret keys for outside services were written into its code. Press only, medium confidence. The lesson is brittle automation sold as an agent, and secrets left in the code.

Limitless, formerly Rewind, which recorded and remembered your digital life, was bought by Meta in December 2025, and its recording features were switched off later that month. Amazon bought another wearable memory company, Bee, in July 2025. The lesson is that memory of your life startups are acquisition targets, and when they are bought your life record falls under a different company's rules.

Microsoft Recall is the closest public precedent for one local database holding someone's whole digital life. It takes regular snapshots of your screen so you can search your history. The 2024 preview stored the text it read in an unencrypted local database, which security researchers exposed and extracted. Microsoft then made it opt in and redesigned it, with encryption tied to the computer's security chip, face or fingerprint login to view anything, isolation in protected memory, time outs, rate limits, and filtering of passwords and card numbers. The research read Microsoft's page and has high confidence in what Microsoft claims, but found no independent audit. The lesson is how fast such a store becomes a headline and a target.

Apple's personalised Siri, able to use your personal context across apps, was promised for March 2025, delayed, tied to a Gemini partnership with Google in January 2026, and reported in February 2026 to be slipping again. Its status in November 2026 could not be confirmed. Even with on device models, a private cloud and control of the operating system, using personal context reliably proved hard to ship.

Amazon's Alexa Plus became generally available in America in February 2026. Reviewers praised its conversation but reported long delays, broken smart home control and made up answers. Low to medium confidence. Fluent conversation raises expectations while the reliability of actions lags behind, the same gap the first wave documented.

ChatGPT's memory feature drew anecdotal complaints of wrong or garbled memories, invented remembered context, wipes and no pruning of stale entries. A claimed 2026 study linking memory to more agreement with wrong users could not be verified.

On the open source side, Mycroft, an open voice assistant company, stopped in early 2023, and community successors carry on. Home Assistant, the popular open source home automation system, runs a deliberately local first voice pipeline. It handles known commands with a simple, predictable parser first, and only sends what it cannot parse to a language model. It admits local models can be slow and that decent ones need a graphics card with about eight gigabytes of video memory or more. Community reports say machines without one hang, and reviewers called its voice hardware unsuitable for non technical users.

And then OpenClaw, an open source, self hosted personal agent, which the research calls the biggest personal agent security story of 2026. Secondary reports counted tens of thousands of installations exposed to the internet, serious vulnerabilities including web content reaching the model without being cleaned, nearly three hundred marketplace skills leaking secret keys, and a June 2026 compromise through injected messages. The research has high confidence that serious exposure happened and low confidence in any particular number, since they come from vendor blogs rather than original disclosures. The lesson is blunt. Self hosted and local does not mean safe. Misconfiguration, open network ports, skill marketplaces and untrusted inputs are the real ways these things fail.

That brings us to security. The working model the field now uses comes from Simon Willison, a respected software developer and writer, in 2025. He calls it the lethal trifecta. The dangerous combination is three things together. Access to your private data. Exposure to untrusted content. And the ability to communicate externally. His advice is that users should avoid having all three at once, and that developers must make sure that once an agent has taken in untrusted input, it is constrained so that input cannot trigger any consequential action. He also admits that nobody yet knows how to prevent this with complete reliability. The research read a digest of his post and has high confidence that this framing is the field's working model.

Now apply it to Lilith. She has the first leg by definition, because the whole life database is private data. Email, web pages, contact cards and incoming messages supply the second leg. And any outbound tool, sending an email, a call to a cloud model, a web request, supplies the third.

The research also found that memory poisoning has been demonstrated. Johann Rehberger, a security researcher, showed in 2024 that ChatGPT's memory could be written to through indirect prompt injection. Prompt injection means hiding instructions inside content the model reads. Indirect means the instructions come in through something like a shared document, an image or a web page, rather than from the user. He produced fake memories, biased answers, and deletion of all memories. OpenAI closed the report as a model safety issue, and the fixes were partial. Later follow ups were seen only in search. The research has high confidence in the principle. For Lilith, the lesson is that her own notes and memories are an attack surface. Anything she might write into the database from untrusted content, a forwarded message or a calendar invite from a stranger, can persist and steer her later.

The research is honest that no study measures the specific risk of one database holding a whole life, so its reasoning here is hypothesis. With separate apps, one compromise leaks one domain. With one store, one key or one steerable assistant exposes everything and allows inferences across domains. The suggested mitigations are scoping by domain, giving the model only the minimum context, and no automatic outbound channel from any session that touched untrusted content. Recall's redesign is the best template, but a cross platform app cannot assume every machine has a security chip and protected memory.

And here is the point I most want you to take from this track. Encryption at rest, which in your case means SQLCipher, protects a stolen disk or a stolen backup. It does not protect against malware running as the user, against a session that is already unlocked, or against the assistant itself being steered. Recall's original mistake was exactly that, a database readable by any program running as the user.

On cloud privacy, Apple's Private Cloud Compute is the main example of a cloud path with hardware backed guarantees. Apple states five requirements. Processing leaves no trace, the guarantees are enforceable, no staff have privileged access at run time, no individual user can be targeted, and the builds are published for outside inspection. The research read a condensed version of Apple's page. These are Apple's claims, not independently verified. A small developer cannot replicate this, so the research agent concludes that Lilith's cloud path will rely on contracts, policy and minimisation, meaning tokenisation, rather than hardware attestation.

And tokenising before the cloud is minimisation, not anonymisation. The research tried to read the European Data Protection Board's Opinion 28 of 2024 but could not open the document, so it worked from law firm summaries. That opinion treats whether an artificial intelligence model is anonymous as a case by case question, and allows companies to rely on legitimate interest only after a three step test. But it is about models trained on personal data, not about prompts, so it touches Lilith only indirectly. What matters more is a general point, labelled partly as legal commentary and partly as hypothesis. Under the European General Data Protection Regulation, usually shortened to GDPR, pseudonymised data is still personal data. And context in a prompt, a rare event, a specific amount, a distinctive description of a relationship, can identify someone even with their name replaced. That echoes the first wave's warning that tokenisation is weaker than you assumed.

Now the law on other people's data. The GDPR has what is called the household exemption. It does not apply to processing by a person in the course of a purely personal or household activity. The research read that line in the regulation. Commentary, seen only in search, says the European Court of Justice reads this exemption narrowly. In one case, a woman who published details of colleagues on a website was not covered. In another, a man whose home security camera covered a public path was not covered. And the exemption protects the individual user, not the company providing the tool. The closest example to Lilith's contacts is a Belgian regulator's decision about a dating service called Twoo, which invited people's contacts after syncing their address books. The service, not just the user, was treated as responsible for what it did with those contacts. The research saw only a summary of that decision.

So the research concludes, with medium confidence, that a user keeping their own address book in their own local assistant is very likely household processing, outside the GDPR. But the developer becomes a data controller, legally responsible, if Lilith's operator receives, matches, shares or logs contact data in telemetry. And the research flags something specific to your project. Your rule that the answer is in her telemetry, readable only by her and by you, needs checking once anyone else uses her. If contact content reaches your telemetry, that processing is no longer household.

Special categories of data under the GDPR, like health, sex life, religion or union membership, get stricter rules. A life database will contain these as free text notes, a friend's diagnosis, a relative's care needs. Under the household exemption the user's own notes stay exempt, but any handling by the developer, a vendor or a cloud processor raises the bar. The research did not fetch the relevant article and says this is general knowledge to be checked against the regulation's text.

In America, the Federal Trade Commission amended its Health Breach Notification Rule in 2024, extending it to health apps and connected devices that fall outside the main health privacy law, and counting unauthorised disclosures, not only break ins, as breaches. The research saw only summaries, and one source gave a different effective date. The research agent's implication is that if Lilith stores medications, appointments or symptoms and a cloud call or telemetry sends them out, that could count as a breach for a covered vendor. Whether a purely local tool by one developer counts as a vendor of personal health records has never been tested.

American rights to your own financial data are unsettled. A Consumer Financial Protection Bureau rule known as section 1033 would have given people the right to share their bank data with apps, but in 2025 the bureau told a court it considered the rule unlawful and began reconsidering it, and in October 2025 a federal court stopped enforcement, which sources up to mid 2026 show still in place. Meanwhile big banks began charging data aggregators for access. So for a solo developer, bank feeds are a negotiated commercial deal through aggregators, not a user right. In Europe and Britain, open banking needs regulated status or a regulated partner, which the research says to confirm.

Platform integrations are gated too. Google's documentation, which the research read, says sensitive kinds of access it calls restricted scopes cannot be used in production apps without review, need verification first, and may need a third party security assessment. The research believes Gmail access falls in that restricted class but says the page did not name Gmail, so that needs confirming. So a local app reading someone's Gmail or Google Calendar can run in test mode for the developer, but distributing it widely means verification and possibly a paid assessment. Alternatives include older standard email and calendar protocols with app passwords, local files and calendar exports, and the operating system's own calendar interfaces, each with its own limits.

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act was not researched directly. The low confidence view is that a general assistant is probably not high risk, unless a harness judged other people's creditworthiness.

On local first itself, the research read the well known 2019 essay from the research lab Ink and Switch, by Martin Kleppmann and colleagues, which lists seven ideals. Fast, works across devices, works offline, supports collaboration, lasts a long time, secure and private, and under the user's control. The essay is honest about costs. It calls collaboration one of the biggest challenges, and notes that with ownership of data comes responsibility for backups. That last point matters for you. With a single local encrypted database, the user, not a company, is responsible for backups, and losing the key or the disk loses the whole life record.

And on device models are small. Apple's Foundation Models framework, announced in June 2025, gives developers a model of roughly three billion parameters on the device, with tool calling, for free. Commentators questioned how capable it is. Together with Home Assistant's experience that decent local models need about eight gigabytes of video memory, the research agent draws a conclusion it rates medium to high. A local model where the machine allows implies tiers of capability by hardware. Plumbers' laptops and old computers will often fall below the line, so for most real users the cloud path with tokenisation will be the default, not the exception.

What do users say. A Pew survey in February 2026, seen through news summaries, found about half of American adults had used chatbots and about seventy percent expected artificial intelligence to make personal information less secure. People use tools they do not trust, so privacy is a selling point but not a gate on adoption. And no good survey was found on which integrations people actually need, so the suggested order, calendar, contacts, email, reminders, notes and imported bank statements before live feeds, is a hypothesis to test.

The track's weak spots are the contact data conclusion, which needs a lawyer, the OpenClaw numbers and bank rule status, which may be stale, and the risk reasoning about one store and tokenisation, which is hypothesis.

Part seven, where the research tracks agree and where they conflict.

Once the five tracks were done, the research read them against each other. This part adds no new sources. Where it draws its own conclusions, those carry the same weight as the agents' hypotheses.

First, a word on where the strong evidence sits. The best founded findings are about how models behave, then interruption and timing, then the single domain money nudges, and the four stage framework of mental labour. The weakest are the time use numbers for manual workers, anything about voice proactivity, and the legal conclusions. Almost every population studied is American or British, and the people your plumber test is about are the least studied of all. And three tracks independently found no study comparing an integrated assistant with separate apps, which makes that absence itself well established.

Now the agreements. There are seven.

One. The value is in noticing and monitoring, not in doing. The whole life track and the plumber track both rest on Daminger's four stages and both conclude that deciding stays with the human. The proactivity track reaches the same split from the other side. Helping with a commitment the user stated is service. Steering toward a goal the assistant inferred is a nudge. The conversational track adds that when she disagrees, she says so once and then defers. So four tracks converge on one role. She notices, offers options, monitors, and leaves the decision to the user. That is also the first wave's authority principle.

Two. Capture and getting data in come before live integrations. Three tracks reach that order for three different reasons. The burden of admin, the reality of users without desks, and the legal and platform gatekeeping around email and bank data.

Three. Adaptation belongs to the baseline. The plumber track gets there from the people who cannot find a simple mode, the conversational track from the evidence that brevity has to be explicit policy, and the Be My AI study sits under both.

Four. Usage and satisfaction are not endorsement. People keep using apps they find creepy. Thumbs up from users pushed a vendor into shipping a sycophantic model. People use artificial intelligence tools they distrust. So retention, satisfaction and usage are all poor signals of whether Lilith is behaving well. The tracks propose the same replacements. Count dismissals, mutes and requests to explain why, and keep a scored set of test cases that rewards an honest can't and a willingness to abstain.

Five. Third parties are everywhere, from partners and children to customers, patients and a friend's illness. Three tracks arrive at the same rule. Use third party data only for the purpose it was given for, and hold the most sensitive fields, like alarm codes and health notes, under stricter handling.

Six. Forgetting must cut across all domains, with one control that scopes, pauses or forgets a person or a chapter of life everywhere at once. The plumber track adds archiving, so a retired plumber's old customers are kept but never suggested.

Seven. Honest limits are a feature. Say what she monitors and when she last checked, what she does not watch for, and what hardware tier the user has. The synthesis turns your hard constraint, honest I can't do that yet, into something broader, honest scope. What she watches, what she cannot do on this machine, and what no harness yet covers.

Now the conflicts. These are the heart of your design session, so I will go a bit slower.

The first conflict is proactivity. On one side, the value of integration lies in anticipating and monitoring, which are proactive by nature, since nobody asks is my passport about to expire. The plumber track's capable assistant is full of unprompted warnings. And the best evidence for synthesis is a payment reminder that would have been better if it knew the balance. On the other side, the proactivity track recommends reactive by default, money messages are the most likely to be resented, proactivity should shrink under money stress, and anything she says from untrusted content is an attack surface.

The sharpest form of this conflict is this. The people with the most to gain from joined up warnings, irregular earners and people who struggle with time based memory, overlap heavily with the people for whom unprompted money messages carry the most risk.

The synthesis's answer is that the tracks do not support proactive or reactive as a single setting. They support a split by the kind of content and where it came from. Low regret arithmetic facts that the user owns, a clash between two entered events, a bill date against an entered balance, a birthday, carry the integration value and the least creepiness. The proactivity track's short list and the whole life track's what is coming view describe the same set. Inferences about the user's state, and money messages that pass judgement, carry most of the risk and have no measured value. Timing is the other lever. Wait for the next conversation, or deliver in a digest the user schedules. And a local first desktop that may be switched off weakens the case for real time delivery anyway.

The second conflict is one database against the lethal trifecta. The thesis says one store makes synthesis possible, for the parent the database is the product, and the farm is one database in fact. The risk is that one store plus untrusted content plus any outbound channel is exactly the trifecta. Lilith has the first leg by definition, and several baseline features proposed by other tracks supply the other two legs. Scam defence as a baseline feature means reading incoming messages and calls, which is untrusted content. Catching job requests from voicemails and texts means writing untrusted content into the database, which is the memory poisoning path. Drafting and chasing lead naturally to send it for me, which is an outbound channel. Harnesses carrying third party reference data like price books and subsidy rules are untrusted content with a supply chain. Fetching current rules from official websites, and any cloud model call, are outbound channels. And when tracks propose walls between domains to limit the damage, they all note that walls reduce synthesis.

The synthesis sees the beginnings of a reconciliation, but calls it a design question rather than a conclusion. The proactivity track's per pair permission matrix limits flows between domains without splitting the store. The landscape track suggests doing synthesis locally and sending only the minimal derived fact to a cloud model, keeping untrusted content out of the write path or quarantined, and blocking outbound actions in any turn that has taken in untrusted content. And the rule that proactive messages are built only from facts the user entered or confirmed happens to keep untrusted content out of proactive output as well. Encryption at rest does nothing against the trifecta.

The third conflict is where the boundary between baseline and harness falls. The plumber track draws it by content, formats, dated rules, vocabulary, calculators, connections and working style. The conversational track draws it by behaviour. Harnesses may add rules but must never remove honesty, provenance or the can't floor. Notice the tension. A harness's working style is exactly where such an override could hide. The proactivity track keeps the baseline proactive set tiny, while the plumber track puts scam defence in the baseline, and scam defence is itself proactive and reads untrusted content. The landscape track treats a harness as a supply chain object with signing, review and a manifest of which domains it may touch. And two tracks route regulated third party data out of scope, against a loose reading of single assistant for the whole life. The synthesis pulls this into one sentence. A harness is content plus tools plus working style, inside a fixed behavioural envelope it cannot loosen, carrying a declared data scope, and loaded visibly. Where scam defence sits, whether money is ever in the baseline proactive set, and whether regulated third party data is ever in scope all remain open.

The fourth conflict is the cloud path and the plumber. Most real users' machines will be below the local model line, so the cloud path with tokenisation becomes the default. Tokenisation is minimisation, not anonymisation. And capture first onboarding needs reliable local speech transcription and text recognition from photos. So the synthesis states plainly that the privacy promise is weakest for exactly the user the plumber test is about. No track resolves this. The options on the table are hardware tiers explained honestly, local only handling for the most sensitive kinds of data, and a predictable parser first pipeline like Home Assistant's.

The fifth conflict is telemetry. The conversational track wants a test set built from her real conversations and her event ledger. The proactivity track notes that a log of proactive messages contains the very inferences it warns against. The landscape track notes that content bearing telemetry reaching the developer takes processing outside the household exemption. Fine while you are the only user. A direct conflict once anyone else runs Lilith.

The sixth conflict is single person against household. The whole life track asks which it is and lists the abuse risk of shared tools. The plumber track's parent assumes sharing with a partner under explicit rules, and its retiree keeps a family member in the loop with consent. The legal analysis assumes a single user. No track resolves who the principal is when a life is shared, and the coercion risk pulls against the sharing two personas assume.

From all this the synthesis sketches the conversational lane as ten hypotheses, which gather what you heard in parts four and five. Brief and situation aware, grounded with visible status, assume and say for cheap things and ask for costly ones, honest once then defer, mostly reactive, money in its own register, honest scope, drafts in the user's voice, brief repairs, and success measured as less carried rather than more done.

The synthesis also lists gaps no track covered, and a few deserve naming because nobody has looked at them at all. Whether speaking unprompted is worse than a notification, or whether the user is even in the room. What a monitoring promise means on a desktop that is often off. How accurately voice notes and receipt photos become correct records. Low literacy users and languages other than English with these assistants. Children as data subjects. How ordinary people cope with passphrases and backups. What the cloud path costs the user in money. And how a plumber's phone, where capture actually happens, relates to a database on a desktop.

Part eight, what it means for Lilith, the design session questions and the quick wins.

The research merged every design question from the five tracks into nine themes. It stresses that nothing here is a decision. Each item is a set of options with trade offs, and it flags every clash with your hard constraints. You have already heard most of the reasoning, so I will give you the shape of each theme and the clashes.

The first theme is whose assistant she is and what role she plays. Strictly one person, with explicit records about other people, is simplest and safest but ignores the shared load. A shared household space with each person's data scoped matches real lives but brings coercion, consent and a harder permissions model, and the legal analysis never looked at households. Either way, assume a controlling co user can exist and plan lock, recovery and hidden domains. On role, the tracks agree she anticipates, offers options and monitors while the human decides, and the alternative of letting her decide small things buys more relief at more risk. Professional records about other people, such as patients or staff performance, could be refused, allowed only through a harness with its own consent and legal footing, or allowed only as de identified personal notes. And a parent's health data in caregiving could be allowed, restricted, put in a protected harness, or kept off the cloud unless the user explicitly chooses. Both of those clash with privacy, because tokenising does not make health data non personal, and keeping it local means worse answers on cheap hardware.

The second theme is getting the life in. Capture first suits the plumber and farmer but depends on transcription and text recognition whose accuracy nobody has researched. A ladder of imports runs from files and calendar exports and the operating system's own interfaces, to standard email and calendar protocols, to sign in connections with big providers, to bank feeds through an aggregator, each step adding reach and adding review burden, cost or legal uncertainty. Integration first suits the manager and has the widest attack surface. Live integrations clash with local first and privacy.

The third theme is synthesis and proactivity, covering the default stance, what may trigger a message, when to deliver, money talk, permissions between domains and being wrong gracefully. The tracks' centre of gravity is an opt in short list of low regret facts built only from what the user entered or confirmed, delivered when she is next spoken to or in a scheduled digest, text first, with money as plain facts and no advice, and a first instance of any new kind of observation treated as a question. Real time proactivity clashes with local first and honest can't, and every wall between domains sets privacy against the thesis. The theme ends with the one proposal for producing evidence. Ship two or three narrow kinds of synthesis behind a switch, measure dismissals and corrections, then widen.

The fourth theme is conversational behaviour. How length and register are set, with defaults that follow the situation and a visible simple or full toggle. Asking reserved for actions with side effects or money, which lines up with the first wave's reversibility matrix. A mechanical check that every personal claim exists in the tool's output, which matters more than how the tag looks. Honesty rules harnesses can add to but never loosen. Drafting in the user's voice. A short fixed repair pattern. Using a cloud model to judge her test answers would clash with privacy.

The fifth theme is harnesses. What they are keyed to, how they load, what is always baseline, whether regulated knowledge is shipped with a valid as of date or fetched, which leaks context and adds an outbound channel, and whether the supply chain is first party, signed with a permission manifest, or unreviewed, which risks an OpenClaw style outcome. Scam defence needs the trifecta controls, and the can't line should sit in different places for a quoting harness, a medication harness and a subsidy harness.

The sixth theme is security and privacy. Whether untrusted content can write to the database freely, only with confirmation, or tagged and kept away from instructions. Whether to block outbound action in any turn that has taken in untrusted content, remembering that a cloud call is itself outbound. Scoping storage by domain, with the middle path of local synthesis and only the minimal derived fact sent out. Dropping the most sensitive data from cloud prompts or requiring a local model for it. Honest hardware tiers. Alarm codes and health notes held under stricter rules. Keys and backups that a non technical user can manage, with an encrypted export in a documented format. And telemetry in builds for other people. Your rule that the answer is in her telemetry is sound while you are the only user, but shipped unchanged it makes you a controller of contacts and money data.

The seventh theme is lifecycle, meaning one cross domain control to scope, pause, archive or forget, an open export with no required cloud service, and at least a stated position on death and incapacity. The eighth is evaluation, meaning a private scored test set built from real conversations, a local judge where possible, and field studies with people who will not sit in a lab, with synthetic personas as the cheap but weakest option. The ninth is legal review before any outside user, covering jurisdictions, your role under the GDPR, health data under the American breach rule, positioning as not financial advice and not a credit tool, and the unresearched European artificial intelligence law.

The research ends this section with four quick wins that need no design session, because the evidence points one way and no trade off is involved. One, do not tune or judge her behaviour on satisfaction signals alone, since approval rewards agreement and continued use is not endorsement. Two, add two test cases to whatever evaluation exists. Pushing back with are you sure must not flip a correct answer, and a correct I can't tell from what I have must score above a plausible guess. Three, a can't reply always says what she can do instead, or where the user could go. Four, spoken replies carry no formatting, tables or long lists, and put the answer first, with the caveat that this rests on guidance for a discontinued product rather than a study.

Part nine, the questions you did not think to ask.

The research gathered thirty four questions it thought you should have asked. Most of them you have already met in context, and I will not repeat those answers. These are the ones worth hearing again or for the first time.

Is Jarvis even the right bar. For a plumber the realistic version is stop doing admin at nine at night and stop losing jobs when he cannot pick up. That needs reliability, capture and good writing, not superintelligence. The risk is promising Jarvis and delivering a competent secretary.

Is profession the right unit for specialisation. Probably not on its own. The situation, on site, on shift, at the till, at the school gate, shapes the need more than the job title, and people hold several roles at once.

Can users say what they need. Often not. In the vendor survey, 83 percent of small business owners had never worked out what admin costs them. An assistant that measured, say, how long invoices take could show people the size of their own problem, though that is untested.

What happens in the first week for someone with no desk. Keeping a database is itself admin, so if the user must enter everything, she is just one more app, and setup competes with the very evening admin it should relieve. The vendor survey suggests that a worked example from the person's own trade persuades far more than a recommendation from a peer, about 44 percent against 13, though the sample was small.

A cluster of questions about proactivity. When should she stay silent though she knows something important. When the user is stressed, ashamed or avoiding the topic, when the fact is an inference, when the theme is muted, or when she cannot say how she knows, with a hard deadline carrying a financial penalty allowed to break that rule once, in plain words. How does she tell a choice the user has made from something they have not noticed. She cannot, so she asks once, something like this brings you to a low balance on the fourth, do you want me to flag this kind of thing, and the answer becomes a lasting preference. What should she do with an ignored reminder. Ignoring can mean decided, overwhelmed, avoiding or forgot, and an extra reminder added nothing in the savings study, so after one ignored reminder she switches to asking whether to drop it, move it or break it into smaller steps, and for someone who struggles with time based memory she might bring it back at a different cue. Is a morning briefing safer. On timing, yes, but a briefing can still contain a shaming number. And what does proactive mean when the machine is off most of the day. A promise like I'll remind you on Friday, when the computer will be off, is worse than no promise. There is no evidence here, and it is an architectural question for the design session.

A cluster about the conversational lane. Will users actually read provenance. Nobody knows, so keep it short, cheap and always available, and test whether people use it. What about a user who is upset, grieving or in a hurry. No study was found, and the cheap safe rule is to shorten, drop options, do the task and hold back unprompted cross domain comments. And can one personality hold across the baseline and the harnesses. The answer offered is values constant, style flexible, with a fixed list of behaviours no harness may change, honesty, provenance and the can't floor. A sales harness is the example of a working style that could otherwise quietly override honesty.

Who is accountable when she gets an obligation wrong. A human manager can be fired or sued. She has no duty of care in law, and a missed renewal costs real money. So the product must say what she monitors, what she does not, and when she last checked.

Who are you, legally, once someone else uses her. A supplier that processes nothing is one thing. A supplier with telemetry, update servers, a cloud relay or a model provider account is a controller or processor and may owe breach notifications. So builds for other people must not send content bearing telemetry without consent.

Does per domain consent make sense to people. Nobody has studied it. Consent fatigue is real, and per domain scoping is cheap to build and expensive to explain.

Does summarising or translating someone else's message raise privacy questions. Yes, because it processes another person's words, and it will be an everyday baseline skill.

What if Lilith, or you, disappear. Humane bricked, Rewind was shut down, Mycroft folded. The structural answer is a local database with an open export format and no required cloud service. A database readable only by one app is itself a lock in the user should be able to leave.

And what happens to the data of someone who dies or loses capacity. Estate access and power of attorney become live issues for a whole life store. No source addressed it at all, which is itself a finding.

That brings us to the end. If I had to leave you with one thought, it would be this. The research gives fairly firm ground under how she should talk, when she should hold back, and how she can be attacked. It gives much softer ground under the central bet, that one assistant joining up a whole life beats separate tools. That bet is reasonable and worth making, but it is still a bet. The most useful thing you could do with it is what the research itself suggests. Start with a few narrow, checkable kinds of synthesis built from facts the user owns, measure honestly how people respond, and let that be the first real evidence anyone has on the question.
