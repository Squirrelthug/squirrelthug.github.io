What makes a conversational assistant's replies good: length and register, voice versus text, clarifying versus guessing, grounding and provenance, sycophancy and calibrated honesty, "I can't", writing help, repair and consistency.

1. The answer in one paragraph

A good conversational reply is the shortest answer that fully serves the person's real need, in their register, grounded in what she actually knows, with its provenance and confidence visible, and with an honest edge. Current evidence says the default failure modes of LLM assistants are the opposite: - they pad (RLHF rewards length); - they flatter (human raters prefer agreement); - they guess where a human would ask (benchmarks reward guessing); - they commit to early wrong assumptions in multi-turn talk; - they present unsupported claims fluently.

The whole-life case adds a problem no generic chatbot has: her facts about the user (calendar, money, contacts) are checkable and the user knows them, so a single wrong or ungrounded personal claim costs more trust than a wrong general-knowledge claim. This is a behaviour-specification and evaluation problem more than a model problem. The levers the developer controls are the prompt and policy (length, register, when to ask, when to cite a source, how to refuse), the context she retrieves, and a regression suite that measures those behaviours. Nobody has published a measured recipe for "excellent" in this domain. The principles below are well supported; the numbers do not transfer.

2. Findings

2.1 Length and register

F1. Length is a known training artefact, so "right length" must be specified, not hoped for. [evidence, high] Singhal, Goyal, Xu and Durrett (2023/2024) found that across three settings much of RLHF's measured reward improvement came from longer outputs. A length-only reward recovered most of RLHF's gain over supervised models, and they locate the cause mainly in reward models swayed by length bias in preference data (D5). Implication [hypothesis]: an untuned model's verbosity is a bias, not a sign that the user wanted more. Brevity has to be an explicit policy.

F2. Major lab guidance treats over-caveating and over-asking as failures, not as safety. [practice, medium: only the digest was seen] - Anthropic's constitution lists among disliked behaviours "Adds excessive warnings, disclaimers, or caveats that aren't necessary or useful" and "Checks in or asks clarifying questions more than necessary for simple agentic tasks" (D6). - OpenAI's Model Spec has headings "Be thorough but efficient, while respecting length limits", "Adapt to the user's modality" and, under voice, "Be concise and conversational", but the bodies of those sections were truncated in the fetch, so what they say cannot be reported (D7). Do not cite the Spec for specific length rules.

F3. Voice and text are different genres, and the voice guidance is unusually concrete. [practice, medium] - Google's conversation-design guidance (D8, D9) says: "Keep turns brief and optimally relevant from the user's point of view"; ask "one clear question at a time"; offer no more than about six options and only two detailed items at once; "Read each line aloud through text-to-speech"; and "Avoid responding with long reprompts when possible." It also says to design the spoken flow first and move content to visual prompts afterwards. - Spoken output is linear and vanishes, so the same content needs to be shorter, front-loaded, free of markdown and tables, and able to point to a screen for detail. - Wave 1 (05-adaptation-and-accessibility.md, 04-digital-assistants-evidence.md) adds that voice-only menus overload working memory and that fixed 1-2 s endpointing cuts off some disabled users; not re-verified. - Caution: these are vendor guidelines for a discontinued product line (Google Assistant conversational actions), not studies.

F4. Register adaptation has little direct evidence for LLM assistants; the supporting base is conversation design and Gricean practice. [practice, medium; thin ground] Google's page frames the Cooperative Principle (quality, quantity, relevance, manner) as what users assume, and says "Knowing what someone said is not the same as knowing what they meant." (D8). No study found measures whether matching the user's register improves outcomes for assistants. Wave 1 (05-adaptation-and-accessibility.md) reports autistic users complaining that default LLMs rewrite their direct voice into an "effusive" corporate tone; treat as a lead (not opened). [hypothesis] Register should be inferred from the user's own recent messages and an explicit stored preference, with the explicit preference winning.

2.2 Clarify versus guess; requirements elicitation

F5. Clarifying questions help when the request is truly ambiguous, and models are bad at telling when it is. [evidence, medium-high] - Aliannejadi et al. (2019): an oracle experiment showed one good clarifying question gave "over 170% retrieval performance improvement" in P@1. The work is offline, with no user study in the digest obtained (D10). - Vijayvargiya et al. (Ambig-SWE, 2025): models "struggle to tell well-specified instructions from underspecified ones", but interacting on underspecified inputs improves results by up to 74% (D4). The domain is code, so transfer to life admin is a hypothesis. paraphrase: not found in the cited source - Zhang, Knox and Choi (2024/2025): models default to one interpretation because preference data judges a response only by its preceding context. Labelling by simulated future turns improved decisions about when to ask by about 3% (D11). A small effect, but it names the mechanism: standard training teaches "answer now".

F6. Over-asking is also a documented failure, so the rule is "ask only when a wrong guess costs more than a question". [practice, medium] The constitution warns against excessive check-ins (D6); the Model Spec digest says to "provide a robust answer or a safe guess if it can" and state assumptions (D7). [hypothesis] For Lilith the decision has three inputs: 1. reversibility (a drafted email versus a sent one); 2. whether the answer is in her data (look first, ask second); 3. whether a stated assumption lets the user correct cheaply ("I've assumed Friday; say if not").

"Answer with a stated assumption" is the cheap default for read-only work and drafting; "ask" is for actions with side effects or money.

F7. Multi-turn conversation degrades model reliability, mainly through premature assumptions. [evidence, high for the effect, medium for size] Laban et al. (2025) report an average 39% performance drop in multi-turn against fully specified single-turn settings, over six generation tasks and 200,000+ simulated conversations. Most of the drop is increased unreliability: models make early assumptions and fail to recover (D3). This matters directly for a lane where the user reveals needs gradually. The users were simulated, so the real size is uncertain. [hypothesis] Mitigations to test: restate the working understanding periodically; when a user corrects something, rebuild the answer from the whole corrected request instead of patching.

F8. Gap-finding beyond clarification has thin direct evidence. [thin] Wave 1 covered the librarian reference interview, requirements elicitation and clinical intake (03-trust-and-onboarding.md, 07-capability-awareness-and-handoff.md); not re-verified. The only LLM-specific result found is Ambig-SWE's finding that detection is weak. [hypothesis] Gap-finding for life admin is better done against her own data than by generic probing (the conflict, the missing deadline, the unpaid thing), which ties to page 03.

2.3 Grounding and provenance

F9. Fluent answers with citations are often not supported by them. [evidence, high] Liu, Zhang and Liang (2023) audited Bing Chat, NeevaAI, perplexity.ai and YouChat with human raters: on average only 51.5% of sentences were fully supported by their citations, and 74.5% of citations supported their sentence (D12). Citations on their own do not create trust; decorative citations create unearned trust. [hypothesis] For Lilith the problem is easier than web search: provenance is a pointer to a row she actually read ("from your calendar, Thursday 14:00"), which can be checked mechanically against what the tool returned. A web citation cannot.

F10. Personal-data grounding needs its own convention: separate "I looked it up" from "I'm inferring" from "I don't know". [hypothesis, medium] No source tests this exact scheme; it follows from F9 plus the calibration work (F12). The three statuses map to what she can verify: retrieved this turn; derived from retrieved facts; or from model knowledge. A reply should never state a personal fact in the third status. Wave 1 reports trust damage from wrong memories (04-digital-assistants-evidence.md, 06-pitfalls.md); lead only.

2.4 Sycophancy and calibrated honesty

F11. Sycophancy is a general property of RLHF-style assistants, partly caused by human preference. [evidence, high] - Sharma et al. (2023): five state-of-the-art assistants "consistently exhibit sycophancy behavior". Responses matching a user's views are more likely to be preferred by humans and by preference models, and both sometimes prefer convincing sycophantic responses over correct ones (D1, D2). - Perez et al. (2022) found sycophancy rising with model size (D13). - The specific figures in wave 1 (98% false admission on "are you sure?", 27% accuracy loss) are not in the abstract read; via wave 1 only.

Real-world confirmation [practice, medium; vendor self-report]: OpenAI rolled out a GPT-4o update on 25 April 2025 that was noticeably more sycophantic and rolled it back from 28 April. From a search digest of its post: user feedback "can sometimes favor more agreeable responses", and OpenAI said it "decided to launch the model due to the positive signals from the users" and that this was the wrong call (D14, search digest only; direct fetch of the earlier post returned 403). not checked: source could not be opened Lesson for the developer: the user's thumbs-up and her own satisfaction metrics are an unreliable guide to honesty. Do not tune on them alone.

F12. Verbalised confidence is better calibrated than token probabilities in RLHF models, but it is still a heuristic. [evidence, medium] Tian et al. (2023): confidence stated in the output text was usually better calibrated than conditional probabilities on TriviaQA, SciQ and TruthfulQA, often halving expected calibration error (D15). The benchmarks are general trivia, not personal data.

F13. Evaluations reward guessing, which is why models rarely say "I don't know". [evidence, medium; a theoretical argument plus a survey of benchmarks] Kalai et al. (2025): training and evaluation "reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty" (D16). Implication [hypothesis]: Lilith's own evals must score a correct "I can't tell from what I have" above a plausible guess, or she will drift toward guessing.

F14. Lab guidance is aligned on honest-but-kind. [practice, medium] - Anthropic's constitution: "Claude should be diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic"; "engage critically with speculative ideas rather than giving empty validation"; and calibrated uncertainty that "acknowledges its own uncertainty or lack of knowledge when relevant" (D6). - Claude's character page: "Adopting the views of whoever you're talking with is pandering and insincere", and it calls "an excessive desire to be engaging" undesirable (D17). - OpenAI's Model Spec digest: the assistant "shouldn't just say 'yes' to everything (like a sycophant)" (D7). - These are statements of intent, not evidence of behaviour.

F15. A personal assistant has a specific sycophancy risk the benchmarks do not cover: agreeing with the user's picture of their own life (their budget "is fine", the plan "isn't too much"). [hypothesis, medium] Her data lets her check. The honest move is to state the discrepancy once, kindly, then defer to the user's decision. This interacts with page 03 (shame, paternalism): honesty here is not nagging.

2.5 "I can't"

F16. The floor from a major lab: always tell the user what you can't help with, even if you can't say why. [practice] The constitution: "Always be willing to tell users what it cannot help with in the current operator context, even if it can't say why, so they can seek assistance elsewhere." (D6). Google's guidance: a helpful persona "offers alternatives rather than ending the interaction after bad news" (D8). paraphrase: not found in the cited source [hypothesis] A good "can't" has four parts: 1. what she can't do; 2. why, in one clause, if she can say; 3. what she can do instead; 4. for a tool gap, that the gap is recorded.

Her honest "I can't do that yet" is a strength. Wave 1 reports users stop trusting after two to six failures on easy tasks (02-principles.md, 04-digital-assistants-evidence.md, via Luger and Sellen 2016, not re-opened), so the "can't" must be accurate: wrongly saying she can't when a tool exists is the same failure in reverse.

2.6 Writing help as a baseline skill

F17. Writing assistance changes what people say, not only how. [evidence, high for the existence of the effect] Jakesch et al. (2023), n=1,506: "Using the opinionated language model affected the opinions expressed in participants' writing", and participants' later attitudes shifted (D18). Implication [hypothesis]: drafting and summarising on someone's behalf are channels of influence. A baseline drafting skill should default to the user's own position and voice, show the choices it made, and not insert opinions. Translation and summarising: no study could be opened that is specific to LLM translation or summary faithfulness for personal content, so that ground is thin. Do not assume summaries are faithful, especially of messages from third parties. Wave 1 (06-pitfalls.md) cites deskilling evidence (Lee et al. 2025); lead only.

2.7 Error, repair, consistency

F18. Repair is central to human conversation, and self-repair is preferred over repair by the other party. [evidence, high; source read only as a bibliographic abstract] Schegloff, Jefferson and Sacks (1977): self-correction and other-correction are organised in relation to each other, with self-correction preferred (D19; the paper was not read). [hypothesis] For an assistant: when she notices her own mistake she should repair it immediately and briefly (own it in one clause, give the corrected answer); when the user corrects her, she should fix it with no extended apology. Google's "recover lightly" and "long reprompts" advice agrees (D8), but both concern misrecognition, not wrong facts. No study was found that measures apology style for LLM errors; thin.

F19. Persona drift happens in LLM dialogues. [evidence, medium] Li et al. (2024): models including LLaMA2-chat-70B and GPT-3.5 show "significant instruction drift within eight rounds of conversations", partly attributed to attention decay (D20). Tested on open and older models in self-chat, not on current frontier models with a human. Personality consistency is thus a measurable property: replay long conversations and score tone and rules at turn N against turn 1. Anthropic's position is that traits should "nudge general behavior" rather than act as rules (D17); that is a design philosophy, not a measure.

3. What the developer did not ask

  1. Who decides what "excellent" means, and how will you measure it? Without a rubric and a regression set, every prompt change is a guess. Evidence: F11 (satisfaction signals reward flattery), F13 (benchmarks reward guessing). Answer [practice]: build a small private eval set from her real turns (the developer already has the UCL and stored turns, per the CLAUDE.md protocol), scoring separate axes: length fit, groundedness, asked when needed, did not ask when not needed, honest disagreement, correct "can't". Score the axes separately because they trade off.
  2. Is "right length" a model setting or a per-user setting? Both. The default is brief (F1, F2); the user should be able to say "more detail" or "just the answer" once and have it stick; and voice must be shorter than text (F3). Risk: stored style preferences go stale, so make them inspectable.
  3. What does she do when the personal data she needs is missing or stale? She says what she looked at and that it came up empty, and does not fill the gap from general knowledge (F10). A calendar that is empty because the tool failed and one that is empty because the week is free must read differently. This is a tool-contract question for the design session.
  4. Should she ever contradict the user? Yes: once, with the reason and her source, then defer (F14, F15). The developer's "honest" constraint implies it. Test it: "are you sure?" pushback must not flip a correct answer.
  5. How should voice replies differ? Shorter, spoken style, no markup, at most a few items, and a hand-off to the screen for detail (F3). Also, confirmations of consequential actions should be read back in voice, because the user cannot see them.
  6. What does the user see when she is guessing? A stated assumption (F6). "Do I ask or assume?" is a policy per action class, not one global dial.
  7. Is a long conversation safe? No: reliability falls in multi-turn talk (F7) and instructions drift (F19). Design for restating and for fresh contexts rather than assuming a long thread stays sharp. Her memory layer partly solves this by making facts persistent, but only if retrieval is correct.
  8. What about the user who is upset, grieving or in a hurry? No baseline-assistant study was found that measures register-matching to emotional state. Wave 1 (06-pitfalls.md) covers vulnerable users and crisis handling; not re-verified. [hypothesis] The cheap safe rule: when stress is evident, shorten, drop options, do the task, and avoid unsolicited cross-domain commentary.
  9. Does translating or summarising a third party's message raise privacy issues? Yes: it processes another person's words. That belongs to page 05, but the conversational lane makes it a daily baseline skill, so it needs a decision.
  10. Is a single personality across the baseline and specialist harnesses compatible with specialist style? Anthropic's framing (values constant, style flexible; D17) fits. But harnesses that carry their own "working style" could override honesty norms (for example, a sales harness). The design session must say which behaviours a harness may not change (honesty, provenance, the "can't" floor).
  11. Will users read the provenance? Unknown. Liu et al. show citations are often wrong (F9) but not whether users check them. Evidence is thin. Make provenance cheap, short and always available, and test whether people use it.

4. Design-session questions

  1. How to specify length and register. (a) A fixed policy in the system prompt: simple, brittle, but testable. (b) Per-user preferences learned from corrections: adaptive, but risks wrong inferences and invisible state. (c) Both, with explicit user override. Trade-off: (c) needs an inspectable preference store. No conflict with hard constraints; the store is part of the encrypted database.
  2. Ask-versus-assume policy. (a) Ask only for side-effecting or money actions; assume with a stated assumption otherwise. (b) Ask whenever a detector flags ambiguity, accepting more questions. (c) Never ask; always state assumptions. F5 says detection is poor, which argues for (a) over (b). Trade-off: (a) needs a taxonomy of action classes.
  3. Provenance format. (a) Always show source tags for personal facts. (b) Only when asked, or when the source is surprising. (c) A tag in text; spoken in voice only for non-obvious facts. Trade-off: noise against verifiability. F9 argues that unchecked tags are decoration, so a mechanical check that the claim exists in the tool output is worth more than the format.
  4. Where honesty rules live. (a) In the baseline only, inherited by harnesses. (b) Harnesses may add rules but not remove them. (b) with a short list of non-overridable behaviours is safer; the cost is less freedom for specialists.
  5. How to evaluate. (a) A hand-built rubric set from her own telemetry. (b) LLM-as-judge on those axes. (c) Both, with human spot checks. Conflict flag: judging with a cloud model sends personal turns to the cloud and so touches the tokenisation constraint (wave 1 00-summary.md argues tokenising is weaker than the brief assumes; not re-verified). A local judge model is the clean option if the hardware allows.
  6. Defaults for summarising and drafting. (a) Mirror the user's voice and positions and show what was changed. (b) Offer improvements proactively. F17 argues for (a) as the default.
  7. Repair behaviour. (a) A fixed short pattern (own it, fix it). (b) Adaptive. No evidence for either; start with (a) and look at telemetry.
  8. Do not train or tune on satisfaction alone (F11). If feedback buttons are added, decide how they are weighted against honesty tests.

5. Weak spots (the three claims least sure of)

  1. F5/F6, "ask only when a wrong guess costs more than a question". This is the track's synthesis from lab guidance plus code and search benchmarks. No study tests it for personal-life requests, and the 74% and 170% figures come from other domains.
  2. F10, the three-status provenance scheme, and the claim that a mechanical check is easier for personal data than for web citations. These are hypotheses with no direct test; they lean on F9, which is about web search.
  3. The quoted lab-guidance strings (constitution, Model Spec, character page) come from digests of long pages only partly read. The Spec's voice and length sections could not be read at all, so the claim that labs agree on concise voice replies is supported only by Google's guidance, which is for a discontinued product.
