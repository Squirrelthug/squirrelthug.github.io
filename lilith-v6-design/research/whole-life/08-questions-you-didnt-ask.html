How far to trust this page. This is AI research agents' synthesis, and most sources were read only as abstracts or summaries. No study was found comparing one integrated assistant with separate apps, so the value of joining everything up is a hypothesis. Quotations were checked by script: those marked not checked could not be tested, and those marked paraphrase were not found in the source they were credited to. Treat the structure and hypotheses as leads and open a source before relying on any claim. What was checked and what failed.

The five tracks' "what the developer did not ask" lists, merged and de-duplicated. Where several tracks raised the same question, their answers are combined and every origin is cited (page and question or finding, for example "01 Q1"). Answers are hypotheses unless the cited finding says otherwise. Wave 1 already asked related questions on onboarding, tokenisation, specialist-mode isolation, telemetry and companion framing (wave 1 08-questions-you-didnt-ask.md); they are not repeated here.

Framing

1. Is "Jarvis" even the right bar? (02 Q10) Stark's Jarvis removes friction between intention and action. For a plumber the realistic version is "stop doing admin at 9pm" (a vendor founder's phrase, B1) and "stop losing the job when I can't pick up". Neither needs a superintelligence; both need reliability, capture and good writing. The risk is promising Jarvis and delivering a competent secretary. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

2. Is the target "more done" or "less carried"? (01 Q2) The wellbeing harm in the research comes from carrying anticipation and monitoring, not from doing chores (01 F3). An assistant that raises output but still needs the user to notice everything will not relieve the load. Measure "things the user no longer has to remember".

3. Is profession the right unit for specialisation, and for proactivity? (02 Q1; 03 Q7) Probably not on its own. The situation (on site, on shift, at the till, at the school gate) shapes the need more than the job title, people hold several roles, and a farm is household and business at once. Key harnesses to tasks and situations as well as occupations, allow several at once, keep the baseline proactive set tiny and universal (hard deadlines, conflicts, birthdays), and let richer proactivity come through harnesses and configuration.

4. Can users say what they need? (02 Q9) Often not: 83% of surveyed small-business owners had never calculated what admin costs them (B1, vendor survey). Wave 1 covered compromised queries. An assistant that measures (for example, how long invoices take) could show users the size of their own problem; untested.

Whose life it is

5. Whose life is it: one person or a household? (01 Q1, Q9, F13; 02 persona 2.7) Most lives are shared, and the mental-load and caregiving research is about couples and other people. Decide early whether Lilith is strictly single-principal, and what she does when asked to remember a partner's commitments or a parent's medication. Assume an abusive or controlling co-user can exist: shared tools are documented surveillance channels in intimate-partner abuse (A22, seen in summary only). Design lock, recovery and hidden-domain behaviour; do not assume trust between household members.

6. Who are the other people in the database, and what are you allowed to do with their data? (01 Q6; 02 Q4; 03 Q3; 05 Q1, Q2) In every one of the eleven persona lives the user is not the only data subject: customers (with addresses and alarm codes), children, partners, parents, friends. - Sensitivity is set by the trade: an alarm code is far more dangerous than a name (02 Q4). - Use for the stated purpose only: a friend's birthday for a gift reminder is within norms; a friend's illness used for any inference about the user is not (03 Q3). - Law: the user's own address book in their own local assistant is very likely household processing under GDPR, but the exemption stops at the operator. Contact data that reaches a developer server, telemetry, a crash report or a cloud processor makes the operator a controller or processor (05 Q1, F16; medium-low confidence; take legal advice). Tokenising names does not make data non-personal. - Cloud prompts: treat third-party special-category data (a friend's health or finances) as never leaving the device unless the user explicitly chooses (05 Q2).

7. Can she serve a nurse, a carer or a manager without becoming a clinical or HR record system? (02 Q5) Suggested scope line: in baseline use she is a life assistant, and professional records about third parties are out of scope unless a harness is built with its own consent and legal footing. This conflicts with a loose reading of "whole life" and needs a design-session decision.

8. Does translating or summarising someone else's message raise privacy issues? (04 Q9) Yes: it processes another person's words, and the conversational lane makes it a daily baseline skill. It needs a decision alongside question 6.

Getting started

9. What happens in the first week, for someone with no desk and paper obligations? (01 Q3, Q8; 02 Q2, Q11) Maintaining a database is itself admin, so ingestion cost is a first-order risk: if the user must enter everything, she is one more app. The plumber is the hardest user for the whole baseline (hands busy, phone in a pocket, setup competing with the evening admin it should relieve). Many real users keep their life in a notebook, receipts and a call log, and some obligations (cash jobs, a parent who only phones) never become digital. So capture from photos, voice notes and one-liners comes first. Vendor surveys suggest a worked example for the user's trade persuades more than a feature list (B1, small base). How well capture works for these users is unresearched.

10. What does the user already use, and can the bank data be had at all? (01 Q10; 05 Q7) Whole lives sit in a calendar, email, a bank app and a spouse's memory, so working with existing stores may matter more than replacing them. Bank feeds are not a user right in the US at present (CFPB 1033 enjoined and under reconsideration, 05 F19); access comes through aggregators on commercial terms or through user-supplied statements. Design a budget capability to work from manual import first.

Proactivity

11. Is a proactive assistant even what users want? (03 Q1) Mixed. In a coding study most preferred a proactive assistant to chat-only, but only about half liked more frequent suggestions (03 F5); proactive upselling failed in voice assistants (wave 1). No evidence exists for whole-life assistants. Build reactive excellence first; add proactivity as a narrow, opt-in, instrumented layer.

12. When should she stay silent even though she knows something important? (03 Q4, Q12; 01 Q5) When the user is stressed, ashamed or avoiding the topic; when the fact is an inference; when the user has muted the theme; when she cannot say how she knows (03 F9, F10, F15). An integrated assistant knows the bad number, and people avoid unwelcome financial information (01 F10). Do not volunteer bad news without a rule; let the user choose when, how and whether. Severity tiers: a hard deadline with a financial penalty may break the rule once, in plain language. If the situation is bad (debt, arrears), organise and display facts and point to human services, but avoid advice on what to do about debt (wave 1 argues for hard scope ceilings).

13. How does she tell "decided" from "unaware"? (03 Q5) She cannot, so she asks once: "this brings you to X on the 4th; want me to flag this kind of thing?" The reply becomes a durable preference. The first instance gets a question, not an action.

14. What does being wrong cost, and does it differ by domain? (03 Q2; 02 Q8) A wrong calendar fact is a bug; a wrong connection ("you can't afford this") is a judgement about the user's life and may cause shame. Present connections as observations with their inputs so the user corrects inputs instead of arguing with a verdict. The cost also differs by domain: a wrong quote loses a few hundred, a wrong drug detail harms a patient, a wrong subsidy date loses a farm its year, a wrong scam reassurance loses savings. So the "can't" line should sit in different places for different harnesses.

15. What should she do when a reminder is repeatedly ignored? (03 Q9) Ignoring can mean decided, overwhelmed, avoiding or forgot, and an extra late reminder added nothing in a savings study (03 F13). After one ignored reminder, switch from reminding to asking: drop it, move it, or break it into smaller steps? For users with weak time-based prospective memory, resurfacing at a different cue may beat repeating at the same time (03 F11).

16. Is a morning briefing safer? (03 Q8) On timing, yes: user-scheduled, batched, one interruption a day. It does not solve content risk; a briefing can still contain a shaming number, so the content rules still apply.

17. Is helping keep a commitment different from steering behaviour? (03 Q10) Yes. Helping with a stated commitment is service; steering toward a goal the assistant inferred is a nudge, and an invisible nudge is a manipulation risk (03 F17, F18). Act only on user-stated goals, show the reasoning, run no hidden engagement metrics.

18. What does "proactive" mean when the machine is off most of the day? (03 Q6) A local-first desktop may not be running when a deadline passes. A promise she cannot keep ("I'll remind you Friday") is worse than none. No evidence; an architectural question for the design session.

The conversational lane

19. Who decides what "excellent" means, and how is it measured? (04 Q1) Without a rubric and regression set, every prompt change is a guess. Satisfaction rewards flattery (04 F11) and benchmarks reward guessing (04 F13). Build a small private eval set from real turns and score separate axes: length fit, groundedness, asked when needed, did not ask when not needed, honest disagreement, correct "can't".

20. Is "right length" a model setting or a user setting, and how do voice and text differ? (04 Q2, Q5; 02 Q3) Both. Brief by default; the user says "more detail" or "just the answer" once and it sticks (inspectable, because stored preferences go stale). Voice is shorter, spoken in style, unmarked, with a few items at most and a hand-off to the screen; consequential confirmations are read back aloud. The same person needs different verbosity in different situations (B18, n=11), so defaults should follow the situation (hands busy, noisy, typing) rather than the person.

21. What does she do when the data she needs is missing or stale? (04 Q3) Say what she looked at and that it came up empty; do not fill from general knowledge. An empty calendar because the tool failed must read differently from an empty calendar because the week is free.

22. Should she ever contradict the user, and what does the user see when she is guessing? (04 Q4, Q6) Yes, once, with the reason and her source, then defer; "are you sure?" pushback must not flip a correct answer. When guessing, she states the assumption. Whether to ask or assume is a policy per action class, not one dial.

23. Is a long conversation safe? (04 Q7) No. Reliability falls in multi-turn talk (04 F7) and instructions drift (04 F19). Design for restating and fresh contexts; persistent memory helps only if retrieval is correct.

24. What about a user who is upset, grieving or in a hurry? (04 Q8) No study was found on register-matching to emotional state. Cheap safe rule: when stress is evident, shorten, drop options, do the task, avoid unsolicited cross-domain commentary. Wave 1 covers crisis handling.

25. Will users read provenance? (04 Q11) Unknown. Citations are often wrong (04 F9), but nobody measured whether users check them. Keep provenance cheap, short and always available, and test whether people use it.

26. Can one personality hold across baseline and harnesses? (04 Q10) "Values constant, style flexible" fits (D17), but a harness's working style could override honesty norms (a sales harness, for example). Decide which behaviours no harness may change: honesty, provenance, the "can't" floor. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

Trust, accountability and security

27. Who is accountable when she is wrong about an obligation? (01 Q4) A human manager can be fired or sued; she has no duty of care in law, and a missed renewal costs real money. The product must say what she monitors, what she does not, and when she last checked.

28. Does encryption at rest, or being local, make her safe? (05 Q3, Q4) No. Encryption at rest covers disk theft. The live risks are malware running as the user, the assistant being steered by untrusted content (05 F10, F11), and key loss (05 F22). Recall shows what happens when "local" means "readable by anything on the machine" (05 F4). OpenClaw was self-hosted and local, and tens of thousands of instances were exposed (05 F9). Local-first changes who holds the data, not whether the agent can be tricked.

29. Who writes and maintains harnesses, and do they change the security surface? (02 Q7; 05 Q10) A harness is mostly data and instructions, which makes it a supply chain: a trade body's price book or a government's subsidy rules would go inside one. Third-party skill marketplaces were the OpenClaw vector (05 F9). Options: first-party only; curated or signed third party with a permission manifest; user-authored.

30. Is telemetry a privacy problem, and who is the developer legally once others use Lilith? (03 Q11; 05 Q6) A log saying "she warned about the budget" is sensitive data in its own right, and the project's debugging rule (read the UCL) means the log would hold the inferences page 03 warns about. A supplier that processes nothing is one thing; a supplier with telemetry, update servers, a cloud relay or a model-provider account is a controller or processor and may owe breach notification (05 F16, F18). End-user builds must not send content-bearing telemetry without consent.

31. Does per-domain consent make sense to users? (05 Q8) Unstudied. Consent fatigue is real; per-domain scoping is cheap to build and expensive to explain.

Lifecycle

32. What happens when the user's life changes or falls apart? (01 Q7; 02 Q6) Bereavement, divorce, job loss, diagnosis, retirement: whole-life data suddenly holds things the user wants gone, hidden or retired. Provide a way to scope, pause and forget a domain or person (Sas and Whittaker, C33 and wave 1). Harnesses must be unloadable; a retired plumber's customers and tools should be archived, not deleted, and not suggested.

33. What if Lilith, or her developer, disappears? (05 Q5) Humane bricked, Rewind shut down, Mycroft folded (05 F1, F3, F8). The structural answer is a local database with an open export format and no required cloud service. A database readable only by one app is itself a lock-in the user should be able to leave.

34. What happens to the data of a user who dies or loses capacity? (05 Q9) Estate access and power of attorney become live issues for a whole-life store. No source addressed it: an unmet need and thin ground.
