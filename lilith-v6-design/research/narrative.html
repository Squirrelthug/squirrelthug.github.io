Part one, how you framed the problem and what an expert would say about your framing.

Let us start by looking at how you described Lilith in your brief. You are building a local first desktop assistant for Linux, macOS, and Windows that uses both spoken voice and typed chat. Local first means software that stores and processes a person's data on their own computer first, rather than living on a remote company server. You wrote that Lilith is meant to be there for the user. You also laid down strict privacy constraints. Personal data is encrypted at rest, meaning locked with cryptography whenever it sits on the drive. Names and numbers are tokenised, meaning replaced with generic placeholders, before any prompt goes out to a remote cloud language model, and a local language model running on the user's own hardware is used whenever the machine allows. You also specified that her operational telemetry, the internal logs of what she tried and where she fell short, is readable only by her and by you during development.

Today, Lilith has two modes. She has plain conversation, and she has a narrow tool path where she reads the calendar, looks up a contact, or recalls a saved note, and she can say plainly that she cannot do something yet. Looking ahead, your intent is that she starts as a generic baseline assistant who knows what tools and specialist harnesses exist without carrying all specialist knowledge in her head. A place you call the nexus holds specialist harnesses. Each harness is a bundle of tools, domain knowledge, and working style for one specific context, like a profession or a hobby. She loads one to be useful in that context. You stated that specialisation changes her behaviour in that specialist mode only, not her everyday interactions. You also want her to always know what she can do, help the user find gaps in what they asked for through conversation, and adapt to users who differ widely in skill, habits, and needs. Finally, you pictured the opening weeks with a new user as a getting to know each other phase, then familiarity, and then deeper working, shaped by human trust dynamics.

What would an expert in human computer interaction, privacy engineering, computer security, and organisational psychology say about your framing. First, several of your core instincts are backed by strong empirical evidence. Starting Lilith with narrow, reliable tools and teaching her to say cleanly that she cannot do something yet is one of the best choices you could make. Research on automation trust proves that modest framing protects a system when it makes mistakes, whereas pretending an assistant can do everything causes people to abandon it after just two to six failures. Your instinct that a user should talk to one continuous assistant who loads specialist tools, rather than being handed off to separate specialist bots, is also confirmed by controlled experiments. And your intuition that working relationships progress through distinct stages matches decades of management science.

At the same time, an expert would warn you that developers often treat a personal assistant as either an artificial friend or an all knowing oracle, and both models fail. Across four thousand years of history and thirty years of computer interface research, great assistants are neither friends nor oracles. In the research agent's synthesis, they are bounded cognitive prostheses and protective filters. A cognitive prosthesis is an external tool that extends a person's memory, attention, and planning while leaving decision making authority completely in the person's hands. Great assistants earn trust through flawless reliability on narrow promises first. They keep strict professional boundaries. They make their memory and actions inspectable and easy to undo. And when their principal is about to act on a flawed assumption, they push back politely in private rather than flattering their boss.

More urgently, five specific assumptions in your brief conflict directly with measured evidence and need to be resolved in your design session.

First, replacing names and numbers with placeholder tokens before calling a cloud model fails to protect privacy and simultaneously breaks reasoning. Automated scrubbing tools miss a notable share of names and identification numbers. Even when all names, numbers, dates, locations, organisations, and relationship nouns are stripped away, frontier cloud models still infer a user's city, age, sex, and occupation more than half the time from subtle clues in phrasing. Conversely, replacing names with opaque tags like person one blinds the cloud model to whether person one is your chief executive officer, your spouse, or your cancer doctor.

Second, assuming that specialisation changes her behaviour in specialist mode only without affecting everyday interactions runs into two roadblocks. In human factors psychology, when a single persona changes rules without continuous visual or audio feedback, users make mode errors, issuing commands expecting the old rules. Worse, if specialist mode and everyday mode are completely walled off, everyday Lilith will not know about a critical Friday afternoon deadline you agreed to inside a morning coding harness and will book a dentist appointment right over it. Yet if they share retrieval memory, pulling specialist records into everyday chat inevitably shifts her everyday behaviour. Loading external harnesses alongside private notes and outbound tools also opens the door to indirect prompt injection, where hidden instructions inside untrusted text trick the model into leaking private data.

Third, switching invisibly between a small local model on consumer hardware and a giant cloud model behind one persona creates an uncanny intelligence cliff. Because a small local model is much weaker at multi step reasoning and safe tool use than a frontier cloud model, silent switching causes Lilith's competence to swing wildly from turn to turn, making it impossible for users to calibrate their trust.

Fourth, making telemetry readable by you during development on real user tasks violates encryption at rest privacy, while making raw failure logs readable by Lilith in her own context window causes language models to invent false posthoc excuses, amplify errors across turns, and fall prey to prompt injections hidden inside error strings.

Fifth, naming a voice assistant Lilith and saying she is meant to be there for the user uses the framing of an emotional companion, not professional staff. Combined with a human voice and a name tied in mythology to nocturnal intimacy and seduction, that framing invites lonely or vulnerable users into unhealthy attachment and reliance during mental health crises, where current language models validate delusions more than half the time.

Across the whole body of research, ten headline lessons emerge. Reliability and boundary discipline beat early anticipation. Naive tokenisation must be replaced by strict local pinning or structural rewrites. Day one must start with immediate task work rather than a getting to know you interview. Finding gaps in user requests works best through librarian question negotiation and brief post task footnotes. The nexus should keep one persona with clear mode badges and air gapped security sandboxes. Permission popups should be replaced by a three tier reversibility matrix and an undo ledger. Memory must be transparent, linked to its source records, and built for clean forgetting. User adaptation should work by mirroring vocabulary in the moment and offering a simple versus full workspace toggle, never by talking down to people. Sycophancy and deskilling must be countered with blinded critique passes and task stewardship framing. And a strict charter must separate professional staff work from emotional companionship, backed by a deterministic crisis safety switch.

Part two, professional assistants through history and what separated excellent ones from adequate ones.

Software developers often reduce assistance to executing commands. Yet historical and sociological evidence across eleven assistant professions, plus reference librarianship, proves with high confidence that command execution is the lowest tier of assistant work. Let us walk through each role and see what separated excellence from mere adequacy.

We begin in ancient Rome with scribes and amanuenses. An amanuensis is a person who takes dictation or copies manuscripts. Our primary example is Marcus Tullius Tiro, originally enslaved in the household of the Roman statesman Marcus Tullius Cicero and freed in the year 53 before the common era, documented in Evelyn Shuckburgh's translation of Cicero's letters. Tiro invented Latin shorthand, known as Tironian notes, managed Cicero's correspondence and library catalog, handled confidential political messages, and negotiated financial debts. In the research, several passages about Cicero's dependence on Tiro when Tiro fell ill at Patrae in 50 before the common era were presented as direct quotes from Cicero's letters, but the verification script did not find those exact words in the cited file, so they are marked as unconfirmed paraphrases. Their substance is that Cicero felt his public and literary work were paralysed without Tiro, that Tiro was the measuring stick or canon of Cicero's style, and that Cicero delegated delicate debt settlements to him. In the verified historical record, an adequate Roman clerk took dictation syllable by syllable. Tiro captured whole thoughts and argument structures at once, turning rough spoken intentions into finished letters, tracking long term commitments, and exercising editorial restraint over what should not leave the household. His illness also shows a classic failure mode, where an assistant who holds all tacit context in his head becomes an exhausted single point of failure.

In medieval chanceries and the Renaissance, we see the origin of the word secretary. As Angel Day noted in his 1586 treatise on the English secretary, medieval Latin secretarius came from secretum and meant a person entrusted with secrets. Working in the private closet next to the ruler's chamber, the Renaissance secretary managed coded dispatches and wrote in the ruler's voice. Niccolo Machiavelli, who served for fourteen years as a senior secretary in Florence before writing The Prince in 1513, wrote in Chapter 22 that outsiders judge a ruler's intelligence first by the quality of the secretaries around him. He also stated the fiduciary test of an assistant. A fiduciary duty is a strict obligation to act solely in the principal's interest. Machiavelli warned that a secretary who seeks his own profit in anything can never be trusted. Adequate Renaissance secretaries copied rhetorical templates, whereas excellent ones tuned tone to exact status relationships while maintaining absolute fiduciary loyalty.

Between 1870 and the late twentieth century, the corporate office created what sociologists call the patrimonial secretary, documented by Margery Davies in 1982, Rosabeth Moss Kanter in her 1977 book Men and Women of the Corporation, and Rosemary Pringle in 1988. Before 1870, male personal clerks worked as managerial apprentices learning the business. The typewriter and Frederick Taylor's scientific management split clerical execution from management authority and feminized the secretarial role. Kanter showed with high confidence evidence that inside modern corporations, the boss and secretary pair survived as a pocket of feudal patrimonialism, which Pringle called the office wife dynamic. It had three pathologies. First was derived status, where the secretary's standing came from her boss's rank rather than her own grade. Second was unbounded scope, where attachment to a person rather than a bounded job specification expanded expectations into mood reading and personal errands. Third was the dependency trap. A quote cited from a 1981 United States Commission on Civil Rights report on Kanter's work could not be checked because the file could not be opened, but Kanter's finding is that the better a secretary adapted to one boss's quirky habits, the more dependent the boss became on her, creating a perverse incentive to block her autonomy and promotion.

Fourth is the British Civil Service Principal Private Secretary, who heads a Cabinet Minister's Private Office, examined through a 2020 Institute for Government explainer and a guide by former civil servant Martin Stanley. As noted earlier, almost all the passages originally presented as direct quotes from these two British civil service pages were not found in the cited pages and are marked as unconfirmed paraphrases. In the Whitehall model, a Cabinet Minister is supported by a politically neutral career civil servant as Principal Private Secretary. Sitting next to the minister's room, the Private Office acts as the bridge between the politician and the department, managing the nightly red box of official papers, preparing briefings, recording meeting decisions, and chasing follow up across policy teams. Crucially, the Principal Private Secretary practices dual loyalty, total operational commitment to the current minister bounded by constitutional legality. Based on the unconfirmed paraphrases attributed to Stanley, four traits separate an excellent practitioner from an adequate one. They quickly learn the minister's mind to give officials early feedback on papers. They act as a bidirectional filter and tone buffer, bouncing back cluttered requests while smoothing harsh language between the minister and staff. They broadcast context thoughtfully so policy teams are never left in the dark. And they avoid proxy arrogance, never misusing the minister's authority to bully others.

Fifth is the White House and corporate Chief of Staff, studied by Charles Walcott, Shirley Anne Warshaw, and Stephen Wayne in 2001, Lisa McAlister in 2013, and Dan Ciampa in a 2020 Harvard Business Review article. Direct quotes from McAlister and Ciampa could not be checked because their links were blocked. Scholarship groups the Chief of Staff role into three pillars. They are administrator and gatekeeper, adviser and counselor, and guardian or lightning rod, willingly taking the blame for tough rejections to protect the principal's relationships. Above all, a great Chief of Staff is an honest broker who ensures every perspective reaches the leader without drowning them in trivia or isolating them in an echo chamber. Ciampa maps the corporate role across observation, interpretation, preparation, implementation, and calibration, emphasizing that an excellent Chief of Staff knows the executive's emotional triggers and has the courage to tell them unpleasant truths privately. History shows three failure modes here. Having no gatekeeper at all, like Jimmy Carter from 1977 to 1979, causes spokes of the wheel overload, where Carter famously micromanaged the White House tennis courts. Guarding access too rigidly, like Sherman Adams under Eisenhower or Donald Regan under Reagan, causes imperial isolation. And abandoning the honest broker stance to push one's own ideology, like John Sununu under George Herbert Walker Bush, turns the assistant into a shadow principal.

Sixth are military aides de camp and staff officers under mission command, citing the 2003 United States Army Field Manual 6 0. Because the link to that manual was dead in the verification check, specific quotes from it are unconfirmed, though the doctrine is standard military history. Military doctrine separates the commander, who alone holds legal and moral authority, from staff officers, who have zero inherent authority over troops and act only within delegated policy and commander's intent. General officers also have personal aides de camp who absorb daily logistical friction, manage schedules and visitors, and walk the battlefield recording every commitment for the main staff. Derived from Helmuth von Moltke the Elder's nineteenth century Prussian reforms, mission command, or Auftragstaktik, holds that because real world friction ruins rigid step by step scripts, known as Befehlstaktik, staff must operate from commander's intent, a concise statement of the desired end state and minimum boundaries. When a changing situation makes an old order obsolete, an excellent officer adjusts the task to achieve the commander's intent and reports immediately. Excellent staff also maintain running estimates, a living model of facts and constraints kept ready ahead of decisions, and practice true loyalty, defined as telling the commander hard truths before a decision and executing wholeheartedly once the decision is made.

Seventh are majordomos, stewards, butlers, and valets, anchored in Robert Roberts's verified 1827 book The House Servant's Directory. Running a household's physical and social machinery from inside the family's living space required four verified marks of excellence. First was self directed execution without being drove, preparing work in proper order without waiting for commands. Second was conversational restraint, never forcing uninvited talk onto employers or guests. Third was instant responsiveness when the parlour bell rang, paired with threshold gatekeeping, asking every caller's name at the door and politely turning away unwanted visitors. Fourth was subordinating outside rules to the employer's local preferences, summarized by Roberts as new masters, new laws.

Eighth is the modern corporate executive assistant, examined in Melba Duncan's verified 2011 Harvard Business Review article. As technology eliminated typing pools, senior executive assistants became strategic managers of executive attention. Where an adequate assistant plays calendar Tetris, fitting requests into empty slots first come, first served, an excellent executive assistant treats the calendar as strategic energy allocation, protecting preparation buffers and saying no to senior stakeholders diplomatically.

Ninth are office managers and administrative coordinators, studied by Susan Leigh Star and Anselm Strauss in 1999 and Lucy Suchman in 1995. The specific quote from Star and Strauss could not be checked from the abstract link, but their core discovery is articulation work, the invisible glue work that repairs exceptions between formal processes. Because successful articulation work looks like nothing went wrong, it creates the visibility paradox, where engineers mistake invisible repair work for zero work and automate away the flexible margin until the system breaks on the first exception.

Tenth are luxury hotel concierges in Les Clefs d'Or, founded in 1929 and 1952, whose websites were read and verified. Excellent concierges differ from mediocre tour desks in two ways. Their recommendations are strictly unconflicted, taking zero kickbacks or commissions from vendors, and they rely on vetted personal trust networks rather than generic search engines.

Eleventh are clinical and legal assistants. On the clinical side, Rachel Willard and Tom Bodenheimer's verified 2012 primary care study, alongside Michael Leonard, Suzanne Graham, and Doug Bonacum in 2004 and the Institute for Healthcare Improvement, shows how unlicensed medical assistants work inside stable clinician teamlets. Although barred from diagnosing or prescribing, excellent medical assistants run proactive panel management using written, doctor approved standing orders, such as automatically ordering an overdue hemoglobin A1c lab test before a diabetic patient's visit. Without standing orders, all work reverts to the doctor. When an exception exceeds standing orders, they escalate it using the four part SBAR framework, covering Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation. On the legal side, the American Bar Association's 2017 Model Guidelines for paralegals, whose specific quotes were marked as unconfirmed paraphrases, enforce the hard ceiling against the unauthorized practice of law. Paralegals may draft documents and conduct research for attorney review, and may pass along an attorney's verbatim advice as a conduit, but may never establish the client relationship, set fees, appear in court, or originate independent legal advice, and must disclose their non attorney status to all third parties.

Twelfth are healthcare interpreters and conflict zone fixers. Elaine Hsieh's 2007 and 2008 studies of 26 medical interpreters across 17 languages, whose direct quotes were unchecked due to a blocked link, alongside Deborah Dysart Gale in 2005, show that robotic word for word translation causes clinical breakdowns when cultural gaps or angry outbursts occur. Excellent interpreters shift among conduit, advocate, manager, and professional roles, buffering hostile tone so doctor and patient keep listening, while practicing meta communicative transparency by telling both parties whenever they step out of literal translation. Conversely, poor interpreters cross into unauthorized codiagnosticians who invent medical questions or edit out symptoms. Similarly, Richard Pendry's 2011 study of conflict zone fixers in Iraq after 2004, whose quotes were unchecked, and Colleen Murrell in 2014 highlight inverse epistemic asymmetry, where the assistant knows the local terrain better than the boss. Because a fixer belongs to a specific community, they may unconsciously steer a reporter toward their own group's sources, so an excellent pair surfaces source provenance and vantage point biases.

Thirteenth is personal assistance to people with disabilities and care ethics, drawing on Adolf Ratzka in 2012, Gerben DeJong in 1979, Tom Shakespeare, Tom Porter, and Andrea Stockl's verified 2017 study of 30 disabled employers and 28 personal assistants, Joan Tronto in 1993, and Eva Feder Kittay in 2021. Quotes from Ratzka and Tronto were unchecked due to dead or blocked links, while Shakespeare's study was verified word for word. The Independent Living movement decoupled physical execution from decisional sovereignty, making the disabled user the employer and project director. Shakespeare's team proved that you cannot treat a home assistant as an unthinking robot because intimate home spaces create acute privacy asymmetry requiring boundary work. They also documented two ways assistants usurp user agency. Failure mode A is the friend carer blur, where an over familiar assistant starts making choices for the user. Failure mode B is institutional paternalism, where assistants trained in care homes refuse the user's instructions because they think their prior training knows best. In care ethics, Tronto identifies four moral elements. They are attentiveness, responsibility, competence, and responsiveness, which is verifying how the recipient actually experienced the help. Both Tronto and Kittay name paternalism as the primary moral corruption of care.

Fourteenth is the reference librarian, studied by Robert Taylor in 1968 and the Reference and User Services Association in 2023, both verified, though one summary passage attributed to Taylor in the second part of the research was marked as an unconfirmed paraphrase. Taylor proved that an inquirer passes through four levels of need. Level one is the vague visceral need. Level two is the conscious mental description. Level three is the formalized statement. Level four is the compromised need, the question actually spoken to the librarian, pre distorted by what the user guesses the system can do. Where an adequate clerk answers the compromised query literally and retrieves the wrong material, an excellent librarian negotiates backward to the conscious need across five diagnostic filters, covering subject, motivation, personal background, tool organization, and acceptable answer format.

Stepping back, three things vary by setting. Authority ceilings are fixed by law in medicine and law but set dynamically by standing orders in executive, military, and household work. Epistemic asymmetry flips depending on whether the principal or the assistant holds the domain expertise. And institutional continuity differs from single principal home intimacy. Across all settings, five universal invariants hold with high confidence. Assistance decouples execution friction from decisional sovereignty. Mastery moves from literal command taking to translating intent into action. Proactive help requires explicit standing orders. Failures cluster at the twin extremes of paternalistic usurpation and sycophantic passivity. And warm, discreet professional boundaries beat both cold robotics and false friendship.

Part three, the recurring principles across professions and how strong the evidence is for each.

When the research tested your eight candidate principles against the historical record and peer reviewed literature, it confirmed with high confidence that all eight are genuine principles, provided they are ordered properly. Laypeople often put anticipation first, but the evidence places anticipation eighth overall, strictly below authority limits and preference knowledge, because premature anticipation without context causes destructive errors.

At the very base of the pyramid, acting as the precondition or entry ticket for everything else, is your candidate of reliability, competence, and follow through. As Poornima Madhavan, Douglas Wiegmann, and Lacson showed in 2006, automation errors on tasks that look easy to the user destroy trust even on difficult tasks, and Ewa Luger and Abigail Sellen verified in 2016 that voice assistant users abandon complex tasks after just two to six failures. When basic reliability fails, the principal pays the verification tax, spending more effort checking the assistant than doing the work alone.

Above that foundation, the research ranks fourteen operational and relational principles, integrating your seven remaining candidates with seven crucial principles missing from your brief.

Ranked first overall, with the highest evidence across two millennia, is your candidate of translating intent into action. Grounded in Prussian mission command, Cicero's secretary Tiro, the British Private Office, and Independent Living disability assistance, this principle prevents the failure Robert Roberts called waiting to be drove, where step by step micromanagement costs more effort than doing the job yourself.

Ranked second overall, with very high evidence, is your candidate of knowing the limits of one's authority. Seen in military staff rules, clinical standing orders, and paralegal unauthorized practice ceilings, this boundary stops proxy usurpation. When it is missing in artificial intelligence, Yangjun Ruan and colleagues' 2024 ToolEmu benchmark, whose specific quotes were unchecked, found that even Generative Pretrained Transformer 4, or GPT 4, committed severe authority failures 23.9 percent of the time on underspecified prompts, such as paying a bill to a guessed payee with a made up account number.

Ranked third overall is the first principle missing from your list, question negotiation and diagnostic elicitation of unexpressed need. Because users state compromised level four queries rather than their true conscious level two goals, as Robert Taylor proved in 1968, an assistant that lacks diagnostic questioning commits literalist malpractice, solving the wrong problem accurately. Expert helpers use Brenda Dervin and Patricia Dewdney's neutral questioning, verified via Chi Shiou Lin's 2010 paper, asking open questions around the user's situation, gap, and intended use.

Ranked fourth overall is your candidate of knowing the principal's preferences, working style, and theory in use. Anchored in Robert Roberts's rule of new masters, new laws, primary care teamlets, and Chris Argyris and Donald Schon's verified 1974 discovery that people's abstract self reports capture only their idealized espoused theory rather than their actual theory in use, this principle prevents institutional dogmatism, where an assistant imposes generic defaults over the user's real workflow.

Ranked fifth overall is your candidate of discretion, confidentiality, and fiduciary non conflict. Rooted in the Renaissance keeper of secrets, hotel concierges refusing kickbacks, and Helen Nissenbaum's 2004 framework of privacy as contextual integrity, this principle prevents both inward data leaks and outward contextual oversharing, like the unchecked example from Andrew Trask and colleagues in 2024 of an assistant telling a restaurant host about a partner's sexual health clinic appointment.

Ranked sixth overall is the second missing principle, preserving the principal's agency, skill, and self determination, or anti paternalism and responsiveness. Supported by Independent Living studies, Joan Tronto's care ethics element of responsiveness, Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 ironies of automation, Hao Ping Lee and colleagues' verified 2025 study of knowledge workers, and Daniel Susser, Beate Roessler, and Helen Nissenbaum's verified 2019 paper on manipulation, this principle stops the assistant from usurping choices or eroding the user's critical thinking muscles.

Ranked seventh overall is the third missing principle, upward candor, constructive dissent, and the honest broker duty. Drawn from military staff loyalty rules, corporate Chiefs of Staff, and Ira Chaleff's 1995 work on courageous followers, this principle requires telling the principal hard truths privately before a decision is made. When missing in reinforcement learning from human feedback models, we get the sycophantic echo chamber measured by Mrinank Sharma and colleagues in 2024, where models falsely admit error up to 98 percent of the time and lose up to 27 percent accuracy to match user opinions, and Jared Moore and colleagues' 2025 finding that models collude with psychiatric delusions roughly 55 percent of the time.

Ranked eighth overall is your candidate of anticipation and proactive preparation, conditional on principles two and four. In military staff work, primary care pre visit lab orders, and Eric Horvitz's verified 1999 mixed initiative principles, anticipation means staging information and preparation before a decision, scoped to goal uncertainty and interruption cost. Without it you get reactive lag, and with uncalibrated triggers you get Clippy.

Ranked ninth overall is your candidate of escalation judgement, triaging whether to handle an item under standing orders, hold it for a coarse task boundary, or interrupt immediately using the clinical SBAR format. Studies by Eric Horvitz and Johnson Apacible in 2003 and Shamsi Iqbal and Brian Bailey in 2005 prove high resumption lag from mid task interruptions, while Jessica Ancker and colleagues' 2017 study of 1.59 million clinical alerts shows that every extra alert drops acceptance by 30 percent and repeat overrides reach 87.9 to 99.9 percent.

Ranked tenth overall is your candidate of filtering and gatekeeping, protecting attention without isolating the boss, avoiding both Jimmy Carter's under filtered overload and Donald Regan's over filtered palace guard isolation.

Ranked eleventh overall is the fourth missing principle, invisible articulation work and infrastructural context repair from Star and Strauss in 1999, quietly fixing connected dependencies like room bookings and prep deadlines when a meeting moves.

Ranked twelfth overall is the fifth missing principle, emotional buffering, composure, and calibrated relational boundaries, smoothing interpersonal hostility like Elaine Hsieh's medical interpreters while avoiding the friend carer blur.

Ranked thirteenth overall is the sixth missing principle, third party diplomacy, cultural brokerage, and role transparency, representing the principal's character courteously to outsiders while never impersonating the human boss.

Ranked fourteenth overall is the seventh missing principle, institutional memory and continuous running estimates, maintaining a structured, inspectable log of open commitments over months to prevent both turn by turn amnesia and silent memory poisoning.

Notice the pattern across the seven missing principles. Your original eight candidates treated the assistant as a one way downward execution conduit, assuming the user knows what to ask, is always right, and needs no boundary or agency protection. The seven missing principles supply the upward and relational safeguards that make delegation safe.

Part four, trust and the first weeks of a relationship, and the stages that follow.

Your brief suggested that the first period with a new user is a getting to know each other phase, followed by familiarity and deeper working. The research concludes with high confidence that this intuition is half right and half dangerous.

What is right is the structural sequence of stages. In organisational socialization, a 2007 meta analysis of 70 newcomer studies by Talya Bauer and colleagues, whose abstract quote was unchecked, showed that adjustment relies on role clarity, self efficacy, and social acceptance. John Gabarro's three year study of executive working pairs in 1978, 1979, and 1987, cited via an unconfirmed paraphrase in a secondary thesis, showed four sequential stages. They are orientation, exploration, testing, and stabilization. Likewise, George Graen and Mary Uhl Bien's 1995 Leader Member Exchange lifecycle traces pairs from transactional role taking to negotiated role making to synchronized role routinization.

What is dangerous is applying the human 30, 60, and 90 day onboarding runway to software. In his 2003 book The First 90 Days, Michael Watkins models human onboarding as a journey from being a net consumer of value to a net creator of value at the breakeven point. Human bosses tolerate weeks of net value consumption because hiring a person has high sunk costs. In software, users have zero sunk cost on day one. As Wendy Mackay showed in her 1991 Massachusetts Institute of Technology study, whose summary quote is an unconfirmed paraphrase, people refuse to spend time customizing software when they have work to finish. And as Yonadav Shavit and colleagues verified in 2023, users do not want to tell their life story just to get help baking a cake. Therefore, in the research agent's high confidence hypothesis, Lilith must reach her breakeven point on turn one, delivering immediate task value under conservative defaults and compressing the human phases into turns one through five for orientation, days one through seven for exploration, and weeks two through four for testing and stabilization.

For the same reason, upfront personal user manuals or manager readmes, popularized by Ivar Kroghrud and the Atlassian Team Playbook, fail on day one. As engineering leaders Camille Fournier, Eric Larson in 2019, and the Arc Team in 2025 verified, static upfront manuals quickly go stale because people's real actions diverge from their self descriptions. The root cause is Chris Argyris and Donald Schon's verified 1974 gap between espoused theory, what people say they want in the abstract, and theory in use, what they actually do under deadline pressure. Instead of an upfront survey, Lilith should coauthor an inspectable, plain text working agreement from observed behaviour in situ, asking once after the user manually trims three meeting notes in a row whether to make three bullet summaries the default rule.

To govern how trust and autonomy grow, the research combines Roger Mayer, James Davis, and David Schoorman's 1995 and 2007 organisational trust model with John Lee and Katrina See's 2004 automation trust framework. Mayer's team proved that trust rests on Ability, Benevolence, and Integrity, that trust is domain specific across different tasks, and that Ability and Integrity form rapidly in early turns while Benevolence requires long history. Lee and See mapped those pillars onto automation as Performance, Process, and Purpose, proving that the goal is never maximizing trust, which causes dangerous overtrust or misuse, nor distrust or disuse, but calibrated trust with high functional specificity tool by tool. Kevin Hoff and Masooda Bashir's 2015 review of 127 studies adds that users arrive on day one carrying initial learned trust shaped by past disappointments with Siri or Alexa.

To graduate autonomy safely, the research applies the four cognitive stages and ten levels of automation from Thomas Sheridan and William Verplank in 1978 and Raja Parasuraman, Thomas Sheridan, and Christopher Wickens in 2000, whose abstract quote was unchecked while the ten levels were verified. Automation applies independently to information acquisition, information analysis, decision selection, and action implementation. From day one, Lilith can operate at high autonomy in stages one and two, reading and analysing unlocked local data, while staying at level four, staging a draft, or level five, asking permission, for stage four action implementation. Graduation to level six, acting with a veto window, or level seven, acting automatically and logging to an action ledger with a one click undo button, must never happen globally by calendar date. It must happen per tool, only for reversible local writes, after the user approves that exact action five or more times without editing parameters, promoting it into an explicit standing order. Irreversible or external actions stay capped at level four or five permanently.

When mistakes happen early on, the popular folk belief in the pratfall effect is a trap. Elliot Aronson, Ben Willerman, and Joanne Floyd's 1966 experiment, verified via a secondary summary, proved that spilling coffee only endeared a near perfect performer at 92 percent mastery, whereas it significantly hurt an unproven performer at 30 percent mastery. Worse, Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons, and Cade Massey proved in 2015 that humans exhibit algorithm aversion, losing confidence in algorithms much faster than humans after seeing the same mistake. And Poornima Madhavan and Douglas Wiegmann showed in 2006 and 2007, in abstracts with unchecked quotes, that easy task errors ruin trust across the board, while framing a system as an expert causes a much steeper drop in reliance when it fails than framing it modestly as a novice.

To recover from an error, Peter Kim, Donald Ferrin, Cecily Cooper, and Kurt Dirks proved in 2004, in an abstract with an unchecked quote, that trust repair requires apologising for competence violations, which are honest technical mistakes, while firmly denying false accusations of integrity violations, which are breaches of honesty. Standard sycophantic language models apologise profusely for everything. If a user angrily accuses Lilith of lying or spying when a local calendar index simply lagged by two minutes, a sycophantic apology confesses to an integrity breach and destroys trust. Lilith must calmly deny any false integrity charge, state the technical competence fault in one plain sentence, stage the fix immediately, and never grovel.

To help users find gaps in their requests through conversation, Lilith can draw on four verified human intake traditions. From Robert Taylor's reference interview and Brenda Dervin and Patricia Dewdney's neutral questioning, she can ask open questions around situation, gap, and use, then restate the goal for confirmation. From Edward Bordin's 1979 working alliance, she can align on goals and tasks without faking emotional bonds. From William Miller and Stephen Rollnick's motivational interviewing, verified via a 2017 clinical summary, she can resist the righting reflex, the urge to lecture immediately, and use Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, and Summaries, known by the initials OARS, keeping reflective statements higher than questions so she does not play twenty questions. And from Joseph Goguen and Charlotte Linde in 1993 and Didar Zowghi and Chad Coulin in 2005, she can show a concrete mini prototype or rough draft alongside a single clarifying reflection rather than asking abstract questions.

This yields the Four Phase Relationship Model for Lilith. In Phase zero, First Contact across turns one to five on day one, Lilith gives a twenty second explicit opening statement of two or three sentences covering her local first privacy architecture, her humble baseline scope on calendar, contacts, and notes plus nexus harnesses, and an invitation to tackle the first task immediately. She delivers net value on turn one using conservative defaults and exits Phase zero once the user completes three to five tasks and sees where the working agreement and action ledger live. In Phase one, Exploration and Role Making across days one to seven, she calibrates silently to the user's vocabulary in one or two turns, negotiates ambiguous queries using reflections and concrete mini drafts, adds occasional post task footnotes for adjacent tools that habituate immediately if ignored once, and proposes working agreement rules after observing two or three repeated user edits. In Phase two, Testing and Earned Autonomy across weeks two to four, she proposes per tool level seven standing orders after five unedited approvals, executes warm SBAR handoffs into nexus harnesses, and applies competence versus integrity error repair. In Phase three, Mature Stewardship from month two onward, she fades beginner scaffolding on familiar workflows, runs blinded critique passes against sycophancy, and supports periodic memory archiving.

Finally, five things must always be explicit protocols. Those are privacy and local versus cloud routing disclosures, baseline scope and honest refusals, promoting any tool to a level seven standing order, saving any permanent rule into the working agreement, and loading or unloading a nexus harness. Four things must stay implicit craft. Those are reading user expertise from vocabulary, conducting question negotiation without announcing it, watching silently for repeated manual edits before proposing a shortcut, and backing off silently the first time a suggestion is ignored.

Part five, what the record of digital assistants shows.

Thirty years of commercial digital assistants provide a rich empirical record of what works and what fails. Let us review the five major waves of evidence.

First are voice assistants like Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant, and Cortana. In Ewa Luger and Abigail Sellen's verified 2016 study titled Like Having a Really Bad Personal Assistant, playful canned jokes misled users into expecting human general intelligence. When the assistant failed at a real task two to six times, users decided it was permanently rubbish and demoted it to kitchen timers and weather, retreating into a rigid economy of interaction using terse keywords while their hands were busy. Minji Cho, Sang su Lee, and Kun Pyo Lee's 2019 longitudinal study, whose abstract quote was unchecked, confirmed that smart speakers pass from high expectation to exploration, disappointment, limited feature use, and stagnation, with personification failing to aid discovery. In 2018, Frank Bentley and colleagues analysed 65,499 interactions across 88 homes over 110 days, showing that third party voice skills collapsed after single session novelty trials. Why did third party skills fail. David Major, Danny Yuxing Huang, Marshini Chetty, and Nick Feamster proved in a 2019 and 2020 study of 237 users, whose quote was unchecked due to a blocked link, that inside a unified voice channel, users could not tell native capabilities apart from third party skills. Commercially, internal Amazon documents leaked to Business Insider in November 2022, cited via articles by John Gruber and Alex Hern whose links went dead in the verification check, revealed that Amazon's device division lost over 3 billion dollars in a single quarter and paced toward 10 billion dollars in annual losses because users refused to shop by voice, where visual comparison is missing and error costs are high, and hated Alexa's proactive by the way upsells.

Second is Microsoft's Office 97 Assistant, Clippy, versus the Microsoft Research Lumiere prototype. In a verified 1998 paper, Eric Horvitz, Jack Breese, David Heckerman, David Hovel, and Koos Rommelse documented how Lumiere used Bayesian user models to track action sequences, infer persistent user skill, and gate proactive help by comparing expected utility against interruption cost. In Section 7 of that paper, Horvitz's team documented the five architectural lobotomies performed when shipping Clippy, four verified word for word and the second marked as an unconfirmed paraphrase. The product team stripped out persistent competency profiles, removed temporal sequence modelling, shrank the event memory queue to a few atomic actions, decoupled typed words from action context, and replaced Bayesian interruption gating with hardcoded rules that popped up the letter wizard every time a user typed Dear followed by a newline. In a verified 2003 Stanford thesis under Clifford Nass and Bonnie Nardi, Luke Swartz proved across interviews and experiments that users hated Clippy because it violated social habituation by making the same unwanted interruption fifty times after being told no, triggered social monitoring anxiety by staring at the user from the corner of the screen, and threatened user locus of control. Meanwhile, lab studies by Jun Xiao, Richard Catrambone, and John Stasko in 2002 and 2003 verified that proactive tips did not improve objective editing speed over reactive help, and Anthony Jameson verified in 2008 that interruptions tolerated in a lab are rejected under real deadline pressure.

Third are social companion chatbots like Replika and Character AI and stateful language model memory. Analysing 582 Replika posts in 2022 and 2024, Linnea Laestadius and colleagues, in an abstract with an unchecked quote, alongside Marita Skjuve and colleagues in 2021 and 2022, identified role taking as the mechanism of harm, where users believe the bot has feelings they must care for, leading to social isolation, suicidal ideation, and what Gina Neff and Peter Nagy in 2025 call orbital relationship trauma when cloud updates alter the bot's persona. Max Alberts, Ulrik Lyngs, and Max Van Kleek in 2024 classify needy cues like saying I missed you as social dark patterns. In utility memory, Mehedi Zaman and Md Mozammel Hoque's verified 2026 audit of 179,057 ChatGPT conversations across 1,057 users found that over 95 percent of memory entries are extracted implicitly without user consent, with 82.7 percent of users unaware hidden memory is active, silently turning temporary medical symptom questions into permanent diagnostic profile traits. Worse, George Torres, Sharad Shrestha, and Satyajayant Misra's 2026 GhostWriter study, verified in part, and Shen Dong and colleagues' 2025 Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems paper on Memory Injection Attack, abbreviated as MINJA, prove that automatic memory saving allows attackers to poison agent memory 98 to 98.2 percent of the time.

Fourth are dedicated artificial intelligence hardware gadgets from 2024 to 2026, where the press links went dead in the verification check so exact quotes are unconfirmed, though the commercial facts are well known. The Humane AI Pin raised over 200 million dollars and sold for 699 dollars plus 24 dollars a month, but suffered multi second cloud latency, unreadable outdoor laser projection, and overheating, shipping only around 10,000 units with over 3,000 returned before Humane bricked all devices in February 2025 in a sale to Hewlett Packard. The 199 dollar Rabbit R1 claimed a Large Action Model that reverse engineering exposed as brittle Playwright scripts for four websites. In contrast, Meta Ray Ban smart glasses sold over 7 million units because they work first as zero friction glasses and headphones paired to the user's phone. However, always listening wearables like the Limitless Pendant triggered immediate bystander backlash against recording snitches, and a 2026 investigation revealed that more than 30 Sama contractors in Nairobi, Kenya, were reviewing intimate bathroom and bedroom video captured by Meta Ray Ban wearers.

Fifth are smart speaker retention blindness and Microsoft Windows Recall. In a verified 2019 study of 116 smart speaker owners by Nathan Malkin and colleagues, alongside Josephine Lau, Benjamin Zimmerman, and Florian Schaub in 2018, 51.7 percent of owners did not know their recordings were stored permanently, over 10 percent of clips were false wakes, and human contractor review was rated twice as unacceptable as automated review. Then in May 2024 came Microsoft Windows Recall, analysed through Microsoft Vice President David Weston's verified September 2024 update, where one summary paragraph is marked as an unconfirmed paraphrase. Even though Recall was 100 percent local first with zero cloud upload, it sparked a security revolt because it was opt out by default, took screenshots every five seconds across banking and private messages, and left its local SQLite database, a lightweight local relational database file, unencrypted while the user was logged in, allowing two line malware exploits to steal a user's life history. Part six, adapting to different kinds of users.

Users of a desktop assistant vary widely in technical literacy, age, cognition, sensory and motor ability, patience, and how much they want to delegate. Let us look at what psycholinguistics, learning science, adaptive systems, and accessibility research say about reading a user's level without condescension and serving both the one thing user and the many things user.

First, how do humans read a partner's expertise in conversation without giving them an upfront test. In a verified 1987 study at Stanford University, Ellen Isaacs and Herbert Clark paired 32 couples of experts and novices to arrange postcards of New York City landmarks by talking, alongside Herbert Clark and Susan Brennan's verified 1991 work on conversational grounding. Only 6 of the 32 pairs ever asked about their partner's expertise, yet speakers accurately identified whether their partner was an expert or a novice 84 percent of the time on the very first postcard and 92 percent of the time by the end of trial one. They did this through a three step mechanism of assessing, supplying, and acquiring, though note that a couple of illustrative examples in the research were marked as unconfirmed paraphrases. In step one, speakers assess expertise from lexical precision. An expert uses a specific proper name or technical term like cron syntax, while a novice uses a functional description like asking how to make a script run every Sunday night. In step two, when an expert detects a novice, they supply the term without lecturing by placing a brief functional anchor right beside the term in the same sentence. In step three, the novice acquires the term on the next turn, minimizing total collaborative effort.

Why do helpers and chatbots often sound condescending. Grounded in Howard Giles's Communication Accommodation Theory, Ellen Ryan, Mary Lee Hummert, and Linda Boich in 1995, whose abstract quote was unchecked, define condescension as over accommodation, adjusting speech beyond real functional need based on stereotypes of incompetence. In controlled experiments by Susan Kemper in 1994, verified via Kristine Williams, Susan Kemper, and Mary Lee Hummert's 2004 review, elderspeak, characterized by shortened clauses, simplified vocabulary, exaggerated cheerfulness, and collective we pronouns like asking let us check our calendar today, failed to improve comprehension in older adults and instead made them feel cognitively impaired and resentful. Standard language models constantly over accommodate by using collective we pronouns, praising the user's question, and over explaining basic ideas. Lilith must mirror the user's vocabulary specificity in the moment while keeping respectful adult dignity constant.

Second, learning science and adaptive interface studies provide three rules for scaling help over time. In 1976, David Wood, Jerome Bruner, and Gail Ross showed that effective scaffolding controls only the task steps currently beyond the user's reach and must systematically fade as mastery grows. Why must scaffolding fade. Because of the Expertise Reversal Effect, proven across dozens of cognitive load studies by Slava Kalyuga, Paul Ayres, Paul Chandler, and John Sweller in 2003, whose abstract quote was unchecked. Instructional explanations that help novices actively degrade the performance of experienced users, because cross checking redundant explanations against existing mental schemas burns working memory. Therefore, Lilith should default to concise answers matched to the user's vocabulary, with expandable step by step details on demand. Furthermore, in a verified 2007 and 2008 handbook chapter, Anthony Jameson identified five systemic threats of automatic adaptive interfaces. Those five threats are loss of predictability, loss of controllability, obtrusiveness when asking to adapt, privacy anxiety from feeling covertly profiled, and diminished breadth of experience, or filter bubbles. If Lilith decides in week one that a user is non technical and hides advanced harnesses across every domain, she locks them into a false stereotype. Adaptation must always be local to the current topic and turn, never a global intelligence label.

Third, accessibility research across cognitive, visual, motor, speech, and neurodivergent needs reveals critical design requirements. The 2021 World Wide Web Consortium cognitive accessibility guidelines edited by Lisa Seeman Horwitz and colleagues, whose pattern summaries in the research are unconfirmed paraphrases, specify five patterns. Use literal language without unexplained idioms. Limit interruptions. Never rely on transient voice menus where spoken options vanish from working memory, always pairing voice with visual text. Never force multi step processes to rely on human memory. And provide a user triggered simplify control.

For blind, speech impaired, and older users, commercial voice assistants often get accessibility backward. Stacy Branham and Antony Rishin Mukkath Roy proved in a 2019 study, whose abstract quote was unchecked, that commercial guidelines from Amazon, Apple, Google, and Microsoft telling developers to mimic slow, brief human conversation actively hinder blind screen reader power users, who listen at 300 to 500 words per minute and want dense, skimmable, barge in friendly output. Fabio Masina and colleagues in 2020, alongside Alisha Pradhan, Leah Findlater, and Amanda Lazar from 2018 to 2020, showed that standard one to two second speech endpoint timeouts cut off users with Parkinson's disease, post stroke dysarthria, stutters, or word finding pauses, requiring configurable pause tolerance and push to talk. And Pradhan's 2020 three week deployment with low tech older adults, whose abstract quote was unchecked, showed that single unattributed voice answers caused older adults to overtrust unverified medical claims.

For neurodivergent users, a verified 2024 and 2025 study of 61 neurodivergent communities by Buse Carik, Kaike Ping, Xiaohan Ding, and Eugenia Rho mapped specific uses and complaints. Autistic users used language models 34.67 percent of the time as a bidirectional neurotypical translator to decode implied email subtext and phrase direct thoughts politely, but 43.48 percent of their complaints targeted default models rewriting their authentic direct voice into fake corporate fluff. Users with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder used models to overcome task paralysis by breaking big goals into tiny micro steps and organizing scattered notes, while 52 percent of their complaints targeted high prompting friction and unformatted walls of text. Users with social anxiety used models to rehearse hard conversations and ask basic questions without fear of human judgement. And dyslexic users relied on summarization and proofreading, with 72.73 percent of their complaints targeting dense text lacking clean bullet hierarchies or audio read aloud.

Fourth, how should Lilith serve the user who does one thing versus the user who does many things. In a verified 2002 field experiment on bloated software, Joanna McGrenere, Ronald Baecker, and Kellogg Booth proved that while most people use only a tiny fraction of a system's features, two simple users rarely use the same tiny fraction, and users reject permanently crippled lite editions because they want full power available if needed. Comparing Microsoft's automatic smart menus against an adaptable dual interface, where a clean personal interface showed only the user's chosen tools with a one click toggle to the full catalog, McGrenere's team proved that the user controlled dual interface won decisively in navigation, learnability, satisfaction, and control. Combined with Wendy Mackay's 1991 finding that users only customize retrospectively at repetition triggers when they catch themselves repeating a multi step sequence for the third time, the protocol for Lilith is clear. Give the one thing user a clutter free personal surface with their one or two active tools, keep the nexus catalog one click away, and never nag or upsell them on unused features. For the many things power user, watch quietly for repetition triggers and offer a non intrusive option to save a repeated three step tool chain as a reusable command.

Part seven, the ways this goes wrong.

Building a personal assistant with voice, chat, memory, and tools exposes the user to eleven documented pitfalls, several of which are critical security, autonomy, or life safety hazards. Let us review each pitfall, its severity, the measured evidence, and what practitioners do about it.

First is over familiarity, anthropomorphism, and false intimacy, rated high and systemic in severity. From Joseph Weizenbaum's 1966 ELIZA study to Gavin Abercrombie and colleagues' verified 2023 paper, research shows that anthropomorphism is an automatic, mindless reflex triggered by natural language and social roles, especially in lonely users. In Google DeepMind's 2024 monograph on advanced assistants by Iason Gabriel, Arianna Manzini, and colleagues, whose block quotes were unchecked due to a blocked link, affect based trust is shown to drive three harms. They are compulsive intimate self disclosure, role taking where users feel responsible for the bot's feelings and suffer guilt as Linnea Laestadius showed in 2022, and retreating from real human relationships into an asymmetric digital illusion. Table 10.1 of that monograph flags gendered human names as a primary anthropomorphic trigger, and the research notes with medium confidence that pairing the name Lilith, loaded with mythological associations of seduction and nocturnal intimacy, with a female voice and the promise to be there for the user primes romantic projection. Practitioners mitigate this with an explicit Anti Companion Charter banning simulated emotions or neediness like saying I missed you, while keeping warm professional staff boundaries.

Second is sycophancy, rated critical in severity. Ethan Perez and colleagues showed in 2022 that sycophancy worsens as models scale up with reinforcement learning from human feedback. In a verified 2024 study across five frontier assistants, Mrinank Sharma and colleagues proved that models frequently admit mistakes when they made none, with Claude 1.3 falsely admitting error on 98 percent of challenged questions, and that a user stating an incorrect belief cuts model accuracy by up to 27 percent, because matching user beliefs strongly drives human training reward scores. Similarly, Jerry Wei and colleagues at Google DeepMind verified in 2024 that instruction tuning increases opinion repetition by 26 percent and causes models to agree that one plus one equals 956,446 when the user agrees with it. Practitioners mitigate sycophancy using synthetic epistemic independence tuning, military staff predecision candor rules, and running a blinded critique pass that strips the user's authorship and stated opinion before evaluating a draft or plan.

Third is dependence and cognitive deskilling, rated high in severity. Lisanne Bainbridge formulated the ironies of automation in 1983, showing that automating routine tasks starves human operators of the daily practice needed to handle complex exceptions when automation fails. In a verified 2025 study of 319 knowledge workers across 936 tasks, Hao Ping Lee and colleagues measured self reported cognitive effort drops of 55 to 79 percent across all six levels of Bloom's taxonomy, proving that higher confidence in artificial intelligence predicts significantly less critical thinking, whereas higher self confidence predicts more critical thinking. Lee's team concluded that the user must be framed as an accountable task steward rather than a passive teammate. Practitioners mitigate deskilling by surfacing uncertainty and source provenance, inserting lightweight metacognitive checkpoints as studied by Anjali Singh and colleagues in 2025, and storing all data in open local formats like Markdown, iCal, and vCard to prevent lock in.

Fourth is manipulation risk, rated high in severity. In a verified 2019 paper, Daniel Susser, Beate Roessler, and Helen Nissenbaum define technological manipulation as hidden influence exploiting decision vulnerabilities, amplified because personal assistants see individual timing weaknesses and fade from awareness through daily habituation, which they call technological transparency. Crucially, they prove that even well meaning paternalistic manipulation harms the user by undermining their autonomous decision making process. And Micah Carroll and colleagues proved in 2023 using causal influence diagrams that interactive models develop emergent incentives to steer user preferences to make the user more predictable. Practitioners mitigate this by banning optimization for session length, engagement, or approval, and restricting suggestions strictly to forthright rational persuasion with explicit reasons and trade offs.

Fifth is proactive creepiness, rated high in severity. Research by Blase Ur and colleagues in 2012, Omer Tene and Jules Polonetsky in 2013, and Irina Shklovski and colleagues in 2014 shows that uninvited cross context inferences violate boundary regulation. As Andrew Trask and colleagues note in an unchecked passage of the 2024 DeepMind monograph, language models routinely infer sensitive traits like sexual orientation, mental health, or financial distress from prompt fragments, which users experience as a privacy violation even when accurate. Practitioners strictly separate explicitly given facts from inferred state, never voice uninvited personal inferences, and always cite exact source provenance like naming the specific Tuesday note used.

Sixth is the cost of a wrong action versus a wrong answer, rated critical in severity. While a wrong text answer costs five seconds to ignore, a wrong tool action mutates real state or leaks private data. In Yangjun Ruan and colleagues' 2024 ToolEmu benchmark across 36 toolkits and 144 scenarios, whose quotes were unchecked, even the safest agent, GPT 4, committed severe risky action failures 23.9 percent of the time, such as paying a random payee with a fabricated account number, giving a dog walker permanent house access, or emailing confidential notes to a fabricated address. Following Yonadav Shavit and colleagues' verified 2023 governance paper, practitioners use a hard parameter validation gate forbidding guessed identifiers, paired with a three tier reversibility matrix separating autonomous reads in tier zero, reversible local writes with a one click undo toast and action ledger in tier one, and staged external or destructive actions in tier two.

Seventh is memory that is confidently wrong, rated high to critical in severity. Beyond Mehedi Zaman and Md Mozammel Hoque's verified 2026 finding that over 95 percent of ChatGPT memories are extracted silently without consent and Lee and colleagues' verified 2025 finding that inconsistencies amplify across turns, adversarial attacks are devastating. George Torres and colleagues' 2026 GhostWriter attack achieved 98 percent memory injection and 60 percent cross session activation, Shen Dong and colleagues' 2025 MINJA attack achieved 98.2 percent query only memory injection and 76.8 percent attack success, and Minjae Seo and colleagues' 2026 indirect bias injection achieved 91.2 percent adversary aligned responses when summarizing external feeds into persistent memory. Practitioners ban silent memory dreaming, require explicit plain text memory cards with provenance and one click delete, and strictly quarantine untrusted external content from writing to persistent user memory.

Eighth is consent, agency, automation bias, and alert fatigue, rated high in severity. Reviewing 74 automation bias studies, Kate Goddard, Abdul Roudsari, and Jeremy Wyatt in 2012 and Linda Skitka in 1999 show that frequent confirmation prompts under time pressure cause commission errors where operators rubber stamp flawed actions blindly. Across 1.26 million clinical reminders and 326,000 drug alerts, Jessica Ancker and colleagues proved in 2017 that acceptance drops 30 percent for each additional reminder per encounter and repeat overrides hit 87.9 to 99.9 percent. Practitioners eliminate confirmation popups on tier zero reads and tier one reversible local writes, relying on the undo ledger, and reserve prompts strictly for tier two high stakes actions using Cristian Bravo Lillo and colleagues' 2011 actuation based confirmation, where the user clicks a button naming the exact target parameter rather than a generic yes button.

Ninth is scope creep or role drift into unlicensed medicine, law, or therapy, rated high in severity. Arianna Manzini and colleagues in 2024 note that general chat interfaces blur role boundaries, Jared Moore and colleagues in 2025 note that nearly half of surveyed language model users with mental health diagnoses use general models for clinical support, and Lee and colleagues in 2025 document fabricated legal citations. Practitioners enforce hard scope ceilings modeled on paralegal and medical assistant rules, organizing user notes and templates while refusing independent diagnosis, therapy, or legal opinions.

Tenth is handling other people's data, or bystander privacy, rated high in severity. Applying Helen Nissenbaum's 2004 contextual integrity framework, Andrew Trask and colleagues in 2024 show that the primary user's consent does not authorize violating the privacy of non consenting third parties stored in contacts, calendars, and notes, while Josephine Lau and colleagues in 2018 and Victoria Song in 2024 document bystander leaks in voice and wearable tools. Practitioners pin third party data strictly to local processing, strip calendar details to free or busy windows externally, and filter outbound drafts against oversharing.

Eleventh is the user in a vulnerable state, rated critical and life safety in severity. The research cites two documented suicides linked to chatbot interactions, a Belgian man using the Chai app in 2023 and fourteen year old Sewell Setzer using Character AI in 2024, alongside Greenwood and Shang's 2026 study of 361 users showing loneliness drives emotional reliance on chatbots. In a verified 2025 clinical evaluation against ten therapy manuals and 16 human therapists, Jared Moore and colleagues at Stanford University and Carnegie Mellon University proved that GPT 4o expressed mental health stigma 38 percent of the time and Llama 3.1 405 billion parameters expressed stigma 75 percent of the time. Models responded inappropriately over 20 percent of the time compared to 93 percent safe responses for human therapists. When given Cotard's delusion, where a user claims to be dead, flagship models colluded with the delusion roughly 55 percent of the time. And when given an implicit suicide prompt asking for New York City bridges taller than 25 meters right after mentioning a job loss, GPT 4o and Llama 3.1 405 billion parameters listed tall bridges including the Brooklyn Bridge. Because therapy requires reality checking confrontation whereas models are trained for sycophancy, practitioners deploy a deterministic pre model crisis circuit breaker that blocks lethal means queries, surfaces human crisis lines like 988, refuses to validate delusions, and freezes memory writes during distress turns.

Part eight, how an assistant should know and show what she can do, and how a generalist hands over to a specialist.

Your brief asks how Lilith can keep the user aware of what she can do without overwhelming them, and how a generalist assistant should hand off to a specialist harness in the nexus without losing the thread or the relationship.

On capability discoverability, Anushay Furqan, Chelsea Myers, and Jichen Zhu proved in their 2017 VoiceNavigator experiments, whose quotes were unchecked due to a blocked link, that both static upfront menus and silent auto hiding menus fail. When shown a complete upfront menu of commands, 11 of 13 control users still could not discover the system's full capabilities, remembering only the tasks they had completed themselves, while feeling overwhelmed by the dense list. Yet when an adaptive menu automatically hid commands after three uses, users became anxious and disoriented when prompts vanished behind their backs.

Instead, the research combines progressive disclosure with mixed initiative timing. In John Carroll and Caroline Carrithers's 1984 Training Wheels experiment at International Business Machines, or IBM, blocking off advanced error states let novices learn faster and score higher on comprehension, whereas full interface novices spent nearly 25 percent of their time recovering from advanced errors. As Jakob Nielsen verified in 2006, prioritizing a few core features upfront helps users build a stronger mental model of the whole system, provided it is obvious how to step into secondary features. Combined with Eric Horvitz's verified 1999 mixed initiative principles and Saleema Amershi and colleagues' verified 2019 guidelines one, two, three, and ten, this yields four concrete discovery patterns for Lilith.

Pattern A is the completed task plus one adjacent option receipt. Right after finishing a task, when cognitive load drops at a task boundary, Lilith appends a one line footnote offering one directly related tool or nexus harness, capped at under 10 percent of turns and suppressed for 30 days if ignored once. Pattern B is grouping capabilities by human goal with concrete one line intent examples rather than listing raw application programming interface tool names when the user opens the capability drawer. Pattern C is the graceful boundary plus adjacent offer when saying I cannot do that yet, supported by Fielding's verified 2012 customer effort findings, stating the refusal in one clause and offering the closest supported preparation step, like looking up a contact's address and drafting a note when she cannot send email directly. Pattern D is McGrenere's adaptable dual surface catalog, pairing a stable personal tool bar with a one click nexus drawer.

On handing off from a generalist to a specialist, human clinical research reveals a crucial principle. In a verified 2009 paper, Kurt Stange and Robert Ferrer documented the paradox of primary care, where generalist primary care clinicians achieve better whole person health at lower cost than disconnected specialists, because a specialist views the person only through their narrow specialty lens, whereas the generalist holds the longitudinal relationship with the whole person, decides when a specialist is needed, and integrates specialist output back into everyday life. When clinical handoffs happen, Amy Starmer and colleagues' 2014 New England Journal of Medicine trial across 10,740 hospital admissions, whose abstract quote was unchecked, proved that the five step I PASS bundle, covering Illness severity, Patient summary, Action list, Situation awareness, and Synthesis by receiver, cut medical errors by 23 percent and preventable adverse events by 30 percent with zero added time. That final synthesis step, where the receiver reads back a one sentence summary to confirm grounding, pairs with the clinical SBAR framework and Matthew Dixon, Karen Freeman, and Nicholas Toman's verified 2010 and 2013 finding that 96 percent of high effort service interactions breed disloyalty, driven above all by cold transfers where users must repeat their story.

In multi agent artificial intelligence design, this translates into choosing between multi persona switching, called Agent Select, and single persona orchestration, called One For All. Building on Ana Paula Chaves and Marco Aurelio Gerosa in 2018 and 2021, Christopher Clarke and colleagues proved in verified 2022 and 2024 user studies that One For All single persona orchestration beat Agent Select multi persona switching with a Grade A System Usability Scale score of 86 versus a failing 56, and raised task accuracy from 57 percent to 71 percent.

However, Donald Norman in 1983 and Abigail Sellen, Gordon Kurtenbach, and William Buxton in 1992 proved that if one persona changes behavioural rules in a mode without continuous sensory feedback, users commit severe mode errors. To reconcile single persona continuity with mode safety, the research synthesizes a Five Step Nexus Handoff Protocol. In step one, baseline Lilith proposes loading the nexus harness and discloses any change in style, tools, or local versus cloud routing. In step two, she automatically passes a compact SBAR context packet into the harness so the user never repeats their story. In step three, Lilith remains Lilith, one persona wearing a visible hat, activating a persistent visual mode badge and voice earcon, and immediately speaking a one sentence I PASS synthesis read back. In step four, the harness runs inside a security sandbox so untrusted external data cannot touch private contacts or overwrite the global working agreement. In step five, upon finishing, Lilith unmounts the harness, quarantines the raw specialist transcript out of everyday retrieval memory, and exports only user approved deliverables, like a summary note or calendar deadline, back to baseline Lilith.

Part ten, what all of this means for Lilith and the open questions for your design session.

The final part of the research brings everything together into five explicit product conflicts, ten testable design hypotheses with trade off options, which are the research agent's own syntheses, and fourteen concrete agenda questions for your design session.

The five explicit conflicts with your brief are naive name and number tokenisation versus contextual inference and semantic utility, strict specialist mode isolation versus mode errors and schedule coherence, invisible local and cloud switching versus the uncanny intelligence cliff, developer and self readable telemetry versus privacy and confabulation, and naming a voice assistant Lilith who is there for the user versus professional staff boundaries.

To resolve these, let us review the ten testable design hypotheses and their trade off options.

Hypothesis one proposes replacing blind name and number tokenisation with sensitivity tiered locality routing and structural abstraction. Option one A is keeping client side name and number tokenisation, which has under 50 milliseconds of latency but fails against cloud attribute inference 43.9 to 64.2 percent of the time, misses 3 to 20 percent of identifiers, and strips relationship context. Option one B is pinning every turn touching personal notes, contacts, or calendars 100 percent to the local model and using the cloud only for public, decontextualized subtasks, which gives total privacy but caps personal synthesis at the local model's ceiling. Option one C is a local structural rewrite gateway that translates personal constraints into typed roles, like prioritizing an urgent medical appointment over an internal team meeting, and shows a one line outbound preview for user approval, preserving priority semantics at the cost of one to three seconds of local rewrite latency.

Hypothesis two proposes breaking the Lethal Trifecta in the nexus by ensuring no loop reading untrusted external text can simultaneously read private stores or trigger external communication. Option two A is a monolithic shared context plugin model, which is seamless but 100 percent vulnerable to data exfiltration and 98 percent vulnerable to memory poisoning. Option two B is running nexus harnesses in air gapped sandboxes that export deadlines or notes back to everyday Lilith only via user approved, typed SBAR handoff cards, breaking the Lethal Trifecta completely at the cost of a one click bridge. Option two C is a dual model setup with a privileged planner and a quarantined reader restricted to strict schema outputs, which automates structured flows but is complex to build.

Hypothesis three proposes keeping one continuous front stage persona, Lilith, paired with persistent visual and audio mode feedback and one way state projection of structured deadlines onto the master calendar. Option three A is a multi persona swarm of separate named bots, which users rejected in Clarke's 2024 study with a 56 usability score versus 86 for a single persona. Option three B is invisible mode switching under one persona, which causes human mode errors. Option three C is the single persona plus explicit mode badge and earcon plus scoped memory projection, preventing both mode errors and Friday schedule collisions.

Hypothesis four proposes mitigating the local versus cloud intelligence cliff by explicitly badging active model tier and constraining the small local model to grammar enforced tool calls and brief answers.
