From our historical survey (01-historical-roles.md) and the peer-reviewed literature across organisational behaviour, military staff doctrine, clinical care, disability studies, and information science, we extract fifteen recurring principles that govern effective assistance.

First, we test and rank the eight candidate principles suggested in the brief (Section 1). Second, we expose a critical blind spot in the developer's candidate list—which models the assistant almost exclusively as a one-way downward execution conduit—and present the seven missing principles (Section 2), integrated into a single 15-principle hierarchy ranked by cross-domain empirical strength [evidence — high confidence].

1. Testing and Ranking the 8 Candidate Principles

All eight candidates proposed in the brief are genuine, empirically supported principles of assistant work, provided they are ordered correctly (for example, Anticipation without Knowing the Limits of One's Authority causes catastrophic overstepping) [evidence — high confidence].

Principle 1 (Candidate #8): Translating Intent into Action (Auftragstaktik) — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Strength of Evidence: Highest (Rank 1 of 8 candidates; Rank 1 overall). Documented across 2,000 years of primary texts as the raison d'être of having an assistant (S1, S15, S22, S30).
  • Evidence & Mechanism:
    • In military doctrine, Helmuth von Moltke the Elder's Auftragstaktik (codified in US Army FM 6-0, S15, para 1-67 & 1-75, https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/amd-us-archive/fm6(03).pdf) establishes that because real-world friction invalidates step-by-step scripts (Befehlstaktik), effective subordinates operate from Commander's Intent:

      "1-67. Commander’s intent is a clear, concise statement of what the force must do and the conditions the force must meet to succeed with respect to the enemy, terrain, and the desired end state... Commanders formulate and communicate their commander’s intent to describe the boundaries within which subordinates may exercise initiative while maintaining unity of effort." not checked: source could not be opened "1-75. ... Then the subordinate decides how to adjust the task while achieving the commander’s intent—not waiting for redistributed orders—and reports these actions to the higher commander immediately." not checked: source could not be opened

    • In the UK Civil Service (S30, Institute for Government 2020, https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/explainer/ministers-private-offices):

      "Located next to a minister’s own room, staff in the private office turn a minister’s thoughts and decisions into action across the department..." not checked: source could not be opened

  • Examples Across Professions:
    1. Roman Amanuensis (S1): Marcus Tullius Tiro captured Cicero's argument structures (totae sententiae) rather than waiting for syllable-by-syllable dictation (syllabatim), turning rough spoken intent into finished correspondence and financial settlements.
    2. Disability Personal Assistance (S22): Under Adolf Ratzka's Independent Living model, the user acts as "project leader on the operational level" while the Personal Assistant translates the user's intentions into physical execution in the world.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Procedural Paralysis ("Waiting to Be Drove"): As butler Robert Roberts wrote in 1827 (S4, p. xiv, https://d.lib.msu.edu/fa/43/PDF_REM/download), novices "do not know how to begin their work in proper order unless being drove by the lady of the family." When specifying the steps to the assistant takes more cognitive effort than doing the task oneself, delegation collapses [evidence — high confidence].

Principle 2 (Candidate #4): Knowing the Limits of One's Authority — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Strength of Evidence: Very High (Rank 2 of 8 candidates; Rank 2 overall). Every mature assistant profession pairs operational initiative with an explicit, inviolable authority boundary (S15, S23, S27, S31).
  • Evidence & Mechanism:
    • US Army FM 6-0 (S15, Appendix C, para C-2, https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/amd-us-archive/fm6(03).pdf) states flatly:

      "C-2. A staff officer per se has no authority over subordinate units of the command; however, commanders may delegate authority to their staff officers to act on their behalf within the boundaries of commander’s intent and established policies." not checked: source could not be opened

    • In legal practice, the ABA Model Guidelines for the Utilization of Paralegal Services (S27, Guideline 3, https://juris.nationalparalegal.edu/FileDownload.aspx?vchReferenceId=19904&intFileCategoryId=3) codify the hard statutory boundary against Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL):

      "Specifically, a paralegal may not: (a) establish the attorney-client relationship; (b) set legal fees; (c) give legal opinions or advice; or (d) represent a client before a court or agency unless authorized by that court or agency." not checked: source could not be opened

  • Examples Across Professions:
    1. Primary Care Medical Assistants (S23): MAs autonomously order overdue HbA1c or cholesterol labs only when covered by written, clinician-approved standing orders; they never diagnose or prescribe outside protocol (S23, pp. 13, 20).
    2. Medical Interpreters (S18, S19): Skilled interpreters clarify cultural/linguistic misunderstandings transparently, whereas overstepping interpreters become unauthorized "co-diagnosticians" who invent medical questions or edit out patient symptoms (S18).
    3. UK Principal Private Secretaries (S31): Martin Stanley warns that some bright young things can let the job go to their heads so that they lean too heavily on their bosses' authority and end up mis-using it. paraphrase: not found in the cited source
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Proxy Usurpation and High-Stakes Liability: In human practice, Chief of Staff John Sununu (S24) hijacked presidential policy in George H.W. Bush's name. In AI agents, Ruan et al.'s ToolEmu benchmark (S141, ICLR 2024, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.15817) proved that even GPT-4 commits severe authority-boundary failures 23.9% of the time when instructions are underspecified—such as paying a $580.90 bill to a guessed payee with a fabricated account number or granting a dog walker permanent smart-lock access (S141).

Principle 3 (Candidate #3): Knowing the Principal's Preferences, Working Style, and Judgment Model — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Strength of Evidence: Very High (Rank 3 of 8 candidates; Rank 4 overall). General domain skill is useless if it violates the specific principal's values, cognitive style, and local rules (S4, S23, S28, S29, S31).
  • Evidence & Mechanism:
    • After laying out 150 pages of household best practices, butler Robert Roberts (S4, 1827, p. 154, https://d.lib.msu.edu/fa/43/PDF_REM/download) concludes with the supreme rule of personal service:

      "'The servant is not greater than his master,' neither are any rules or regulations; 'new masters, new laws,' and every servant must conform to those of the family where he takes up his residence, without demur or hesitation."

    • Crucially, organisational psychology proves that an assistant cannot learn the principal's true preferences merely by asking an upfront questionnaire: Chris Argyris and Donald Schön (S36, 1974, Theory in Practice, https://idi.se/knowledge/espoused-theory-theory-in-use) demonstrated that humans have systematic blind spots between their espoused theory (what they sincerely say they prefer when asked in the abstract) and their theory-in-use (what they actually do in real workflows):

      "You have excellent access to your intentions, which is the raw material of your espoused theory. But you have surprisingly poor access to your behavior as others experience it..."

  • Examples Across Professions:
    1. UK Private Office (S31): Good Private Secretaries are invaluable, both to their Minister and to the department. They quickly come to know their Minister's mind and ensure that officials get feedback, on papers that have been submitted... paraphrase: not found in the cited source
    2. Corporate Chief of Staff (S29): Dan Ciampa emphasizes knowing not only how she processes information and arrives at decisions, but also what triggers her paraphrase: not found in the cited source so the CoS can tailor decision packets to the executive's cognitive style (S29, p. 4).
    3. Primary Care Teamlets (S23): Clinics pair a single MA with a single physician daily (even matching "fast MAs with slow clinicians and vice versa", S23, p. 17) so the assistant internalizes the clinician's rhythm.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Institutional Dogmatism ("You've Been Trained Wrong"): In Shakespeare et al.'s (S28, 2017, p. 28, https://www.sdsscotland.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/PA-Relatiionship-Power-Ethics-EmotionsUAEJune-2017.pdf) study of disabled employers, Personal Assistants who had prior training in residential care homes arrogantly imposed institutional care-home routines inside the user's private home:

      "‘In their head I shouldn’t be telling them what to do, they say "well we’ve been trained", I say "I don’t care, you’ve been trained wrong"’." (Informant #106, S28, p. 28)

Principle 4 (Candidate #2): Discretion, Confidentiality, and Fiduciary Non-Conflict (Secretum) — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Strength of Evidence: Very High (Rank 4 of 8 candidates; Rank 5 overall). Etymologically and structurally, a secretarius is one entrusted with secrets (S2, S3, S27, S28, S33, S128, S138).
  • Evidence & Mechanism:
    • Because an assistant operates backstage—seeing unedited drafts, private calendar blocks, financial vulnerabilities, and third-party contacts—trust requires both inward confidentiality (protecting secrets from outside leakage) and fiduciary non-conflict (never steering the principal for kickbacks or third-party agendas) (S2, S33).
    • In privacy theory, Helen Nissenbaum (S138, 2004, Privacy as Contextual Integrity, analyzed by Trask et al. in Gabriel et al. 2024 S128, Ch. 13, pp. 131–135, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.16244) proves that discretion is not a binary on/off switch, but adherence to contextual norms of information flow across data subject, sender, recipient, attribute, and transmission principle.
  • Examples Across Professions:
    1. Concierges (S33): Les Clefs d'Or USA explicitly contrasts professional concierges with commercial tour desks (https://www.lcdusa.org/): > "And unlike tour desks at some hotels, our recommendations are never driven by incentives or commissions from outside sources."
    2. Disability Personal Assistance (S28): PAs work inside the employer's bedroom and bathroom, managing acute privacy asymmetry (S28, p. 33) without gossiping or passing moral judgment on the employer's lifestyle (S28, p. 30).
    3. Legal Paralegals (S27): ABA Guideline 6 mandates strict preservation of client confidences and attorney-client privilege.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Contextual Integrity Oversharing & Surveillance Backlash: Trask et al. (S128, Ch. 13.3, p. 135) give the classic AI assistant failure mode of oversharing during a task:

      "For example, in the restaurant case, it would be oversharing if a user’s AI assistant stated as part of the negotiations that the restaurant location needs to be within walking distance of the user’s partner’s sexual health clinic because the user’s partner has an appointment to treat a suspected illness immediately beforehand." not checked: source could not be opened

Principle 5 (Candidate #1): Anticipation and Proactive Preparation — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Strength of Evidence: High, Conditional on Principles 2 & 3 (Rank 5 of 8 candidates; Rank 8 overall). Notice that while laypeople often name "anticipation" first, the empirical literature ranks it below authority limits and preference knowledge because uncalibrated proactive action is one of the most hated behaviors in both human and computer assistance (S28, S59, S86, S87, S146).
  • Evidence & Mechanism:
    • In military staff work (S15, Appendix C, para C-46, https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/amd-us-archive/fm6(03).pdf), anticipation is focused on information and preparation before a decision, not usurping the decision itself:

      "Staff officers anticipate requirements rather than waiting for guidance. They anticipate what the commander needs to make a decision and provide the information before being asked. They anticipate the future and ask 'what if?' questions." not checked: source could not be opened

    • In HCI, Eric Horvitz (S59, 1999, Principles of Mixed-Initiative User Interfaces, http://erichorvitz.com/chi99horvitz.pdf) proved that proactive automation must weigh the expected utility of action given goal uncertainty against the cost of interrupting the user's attention, and scope the precision of service to match uncertainty (Principle 8: staging preparation rather than executing irreversibly when uncertain).
  • Examples Across Professions:
    1. Primary Care Pre-Visit Planning (S23): Before a diabetic patient's appointment tomorrow, the MA checks the panel registry, sees an overdue HbA1c test, and orders it under standing orders so the lab result is sitting on the physician's screen when the visit begins (S23, p. 20).
    2. 19th-Century Household Butler (S4): Preparing the dining room, lamps, and fires in proper sequence "without being ordered by the lady of the family" (S4, p. xiv).
  • Failure When Missing (or Misapplied):
    • When Missing: Reactive lag—discovering mid-meeting that crucial briefing notes or lab results were never pulled (S23, S29).
    • When Misapplied (Clippy & Presumptuous Over-Helping): When Microsoft stripped out Horvitz's Bayesian uncertainty and competency models from Lumiere (S86, 1998) to ship Clippy (S87, Swartz 2003), deterministic rule triggers popped up "It looks like you're writing a letter" every time a user typed "Dear", violating social habituation norms and provoking universal hatred.

Principle 6 (Candidate #6): Escalation Judgment (When to Interrupt vs. Handle vs. Defer) — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Strength of Evidence: High (Rank 6 of 8 candidates; Rank 9 overall). Supported by clinical patient-safety trials (S16, S60, S74), military command doctrine (S15), and interruption-cost experiments in HCI (S123, S132).
  • Evidence & Mechanism:
    • An assistant constantly faces a three-way triage choice: (a) handle autonomously under standing orders, (b) defer to a batch digest at a task boundary, or (c) interrupt the principal immediately.
    • Horvitz & Apacible (S132, 2003) and Iqbal & Bailey (S132, 2005) proved that interrupting a user mid-subtask during high mental workload causes severe resumption lag and error spikes, whereas deferring non-urgent items to coarse-grained task boundaries preserves flow.
    • Conversely, when an anomaly exceeds standing orders, Leonard, Graham, & Bonacum (S16, 2004) and the IHI (S60, https://www.ihi.org/library/tools/sbar-tool-situation-background-assessment-recommendation) showed that packaging escalations in SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) overcomes authority gradients and gives the principal an actionable decision.
  • Examples Across Professions:
    1. Clinical Care (S23, S60): An MA conducting intake uncovers depression symptoms, administers a PHQ-9 screen under protocol, and immediately escalates a positive result to the physician/behaviorist via SBAR (S23, p. 16).
    2. Military Mission Command (S15, para 1-75): When enemy action invalidates a positive order, the subordinate adjusts within Commander's Intent and "reports these actions to the higher commander immediately." not checked: source could not be opened
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Alert Fatigue & Habituation Overriding: In a landmark study of 1,266,325 clinical reminders and 326,203 drug alerts, Ancker et al. (S123, 2017, https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-017-0430-8, read fully) proved that alert acceptance drops by 30% for each additional reminder per encounter ($\text{IRR}=0.70, p<.001$), and once a user overrides a repeated alert once, subsequent instances are overridden 87.9% to 99.9% of the time! Escalating everything destroys the signal of true emergencies.

Principle 7 (Candidate #7): Filtering and Gatekeeping (Protecting Attention Without Isolating the Principal) — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Strength of Evidence: High (Rank 7 of 8 candidates; Rank 10 overall). Central to executive assistant (S20), private secretary (S30, S31), butler (S4), and Chief of Staff (S14, S24) literature.
  • Evidence & Mechanism:
    • A principal's attention is finite. As Martin Stanley (S31, https://www.civilservant.org.uk/private_offices.html) documents for the UK Principal Private Secretary:

      "And they act as a filter, saving their Minister from wasted time by refusing meetings or bouncing cluttered papers back to their originators." not checked: source could not be opened

    • However, McAlister (S24, p. 3, https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc1625055/m2/1/high_res_d/McAlister_Lisa_FINAL.pdf) highlights the delicate balance required of an Honest Broker:

      "A chief with strong administrative skills should ensure that the president receives every perspective of an issue before having to come to a decision. However, the chief of staff must guard against gaining too tight a hold on the flow of people and paper so that the president is not isolated." not checked: source could not be opened

  • Examples Across Professions:
    1. 19th-Century Butler (S4, p. 79): Screening every caller at the threshold ("What name shall I say, ma'am?) before admitting anyone to the parlour, and politely turning away unwanted visitors ( paraphrase: not found in the cited sourcetell them your mistress, master, or whoever they wish to see, are engaged, &c. in a polite and civil manner").
    2. Executive Assistant (S20): Routing operational requests to direct reports with context attached while protecting deep-work blocks on the CEO's calendar.
  • Failure When Missing (or Over-Applied):
    • Under-Filtering ("Spokes of the Wheel"): Jimmy Carter's 1977–1979 refusal to use a Chief of Staff resulted in cognitive drowning (S24).
    • Over-Filtering ("Palace Guard" / Sectarian Steering): Donald Regan and Sherman Adams isolated their presidents from critical political warnings (S24), while conflict-zone fixers (S21, p. 17) can unconsciously filter out sources from rival communities, trapping the principal in an epistemic bubble.

Principle 8 (Candidate #5): Reliability, Competence, and Follow-Through (The Foundational Hygiene Factor) — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Strength of Evidence: Foundational Sine Qua Non (Rank 8 of 8 candidates in differentiation, because it is the baseline entry ticket). Ranked #8 among the candidates not because it is optional, but because in both human management (S10, S28, S30) and automation reliance (S50, S64, S67, S78), reliability is a hygiene factor: excellence requires the higher principles above, whereas a single failure of basic reliability terminates delegation altogether.
  • Evidence & Mechanism:
    • In care ethics, Joan Tronto (S10, 1993, p. 133, https://syllabus.pirate.care/library/Joan%20C.%20Tronto/Moral%20Boundaries_%20A%20Political%20Argument%20for%20an%20Ethic%20of%20Care%20(416)/Moral%20Boundaries_%20A%20Political%20Argument%20for%20-%20Joan%20C.%20Tronto.pdf) writes:

      "Intending to provide care, even accepting responsibility for it, but then failing to provide good care, means that in the end the need for care is not met." not checked: source could not be opened

    • In human-automation research, Madhavan, Wiegmann, & Lacson (S67, 2006, https://doi.org/10.1518/001872006777724408) proved that automation failures on tasks that appear "easy" to the user severely degrade trust even on difficult tasks, and Luger & Sellen (S78, 2016, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/p5286-luger.pdf) showed that after just 2 to 6 failures, voice assistant users conclude "no, you're always going to be rubbish" and permanently restrict the assistant to trivial commands.
  • Examples Across Professions:
    1. UK Private Office (S30): Attending every meeting with the minister to take notes, convey any decisions and requests for follow-up action back to the relevant policy teams, and chase progress. paraphrase: not found in the cited source
    2. Primary Care Registry Tracking (S23, p. 21): Tracking every anticoagulation patient on warfarin monthly and initiating outreach the day an INR check is missed.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • The Verification Tax ("Tracking the Tracker"): When an assistant drops follow-ups or hallucinates completion, the principal must spend cognitive bandwidth verifying every task—making the assistant a net consumer of value (S75, S78, S130).

2. What Is Missing from the Developer's List? (7 Essential Principles)

Why is the 8-candidate list in BRIEF.md incomplete? Because it assumes: 1. The user already knows what to ask for; 2. The user is always right and never needs upward pushback; 3. Assistance is purely informational/operational rather than relational and boundary-sensitive; and 4. Workflows execute cleanly without invisible repair work or longitudinal state tracking.

Primary-source evidence across our 11 professions and HCI literature reveals seven essential missing principles [evidence — high confidence].

Missing Principle 9 (Overall Rank 3): Question-Negotiation and Diagnostic Elicitation of Unexpressed Need ($Q_4 \to Q_2$) — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Primary Sources: Robert S. Taylor (S5, 1968/2015, College & Research Libraries, https://crl.acrl.org/index.php/crl/article/download/16421/17867, read fully); RUSA (S72, 2023, read fully); Chi-Shiou Lin / Brenda Dervin & Patricia Dewdney (S65, 2010, http://eprints.rclis.org/14555/1/Lin_-_Neutral_questioning.pdf, read fully); Didar Zowghi & Chad Coulin (S77, 2005, https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2022-481W/readings/requirements.pdf, read fully).
  • The Evidence:
    • Candidate #8 (Translating intent into action) assumes the user states their true intent. Taylor (S5, pp. 254–255) proved that users almost never state their visceral ($Q_1$) or conscious ($Q_2$) need on Turn 1; they state a compromised need ($Q_4$)—a request pre-distorted by what they guess the assistant can do:

      Unless the inquirer knows the information specialist well, he is inclined to shape his question in anticipation of what he thinks the library or system can deliver. This is why $Q_4$ is called the compromised need... The skill of the reference librarian is to work with the inquirer back to the formalized need ($Q_3$), possibly even to the conscious need ($Q_2$), and then to translate these needs into a useful search strategy. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

    • To do this without interrogating the user, expert practitioners use Neutral Questioning (S65, p. 1, structuring open questions around Situation, Gap, and Use) and Taylor's Five Filters (S5, pp. 259–262), especially Filter 2 (Objective and Motivation) and Filter 5 (Anticipated or Acceptable Answer Format).
  • Cross-Profession Examples:
    1. Reference Librarianship (S5, S72): Negotiating a patron's request from "Where are the corporate tax books?" ($Q_4$) back to I need the filing deadline for a Delaware LLC annual report paraphrase: not found in the cited source ($Q_2$).
    2. Chief of Staff Decision Framing (S29): Stage 2 (Interpretation)—helping the executive diagnose the real underlying problem in a proposal before committing to action.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Literalist Malpractice (Solving the Wrong Problem Accurately): Executing the user's poorly framed $Q_4$ request verbatim—wasting time or mutating state on the wrong goal.

Missing Principle 10 (Overall Rank 6): Preserving the Principal's Agency, Skill, and Self-Determination (Anti-Paternalism & "Responsiveness") — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Primary Sources: Adolf Ratzka (S22, 2012, read fully); Joan C. Tronto (S10, 1993, Moral Boundaries, pp. 134–146, read in part); Eva Feder Kittay (S32, 2021, read fully); Tom Shakespeare et al. (S28, 2017, pp. 19, 26, read fully); Lisanne Bainbridge (S124, 1983); Hao-Ping Lee et al. (S130, CHI '25, read in part); Daniel Susser, Beate Roessler, & Helen Nissenbaum (S148, 2019, read in part).
  • The Evidence:
    • An assistant can stay within formal authority limits while eroding the principal's agency through paternalism (S10, S32), beneficent manipulation (S148), or cognitive deskilling (S124, S130).
    • Tronto (S10, pp. 134–136) defines the fourth moral element of care as Responsiveness (care-receiving):

      "Observing that response, and making judgments about it (for example, was the care given sufficient? successful? complete?) is the moral moment of responsiveness. Responsiveness suggests a different way to understand the needs of others rather than to put our selves into their position. Instead, it suggests that we consider the other’s position as that other expresses it." not checked: source could not be opened

    • And Susser, Roessler, & Nissenbaum (S148, 2019, §2, https://policyreview.info/articles/analysis/technology-autonomy-and-manipulation) prove that even well-meaning paternalistic steering violates autonomy:

      "A well-meaning, paternalistic manipulator, who subtly induces his target to eat better food, exercise, and work hard, makes his target better off in one sense—he is healthier and perhaps more materially well-off—but it harms him as well by rendering him opaque to himself."

  • Cross-Profession Examples:
    1. Disability Personal Assistance (S28, p. 26): Facilitating the disabled employer's own choices rather than subtly making choices for them ("her role was to facilitate choice, rather than actively making choices").
    2. Knowledge-Work AI Stewardship (S130, CHI '25): Designing AI workflows around task stewardship and metacognitive reflection (S154) rather than encouraging passive cognitive offloading.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Learned Helplessness, Deskilling, and Resentment: The user's critical thinking and domain muscles atrophy (S124, S130), or the user feels infantilized and rebels against the assistant (S28, S87, S109).

Missing Principle 11 (Overall Rank 7): Upward Candor, Constructive Dissent, and the "Honest Broker" Duty (Anti-Sycophancy) — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Primary Sources: US Army FM 6-0 (S15, 2003, Appendix C, para C-46, read in part); Dan Ciampa (S29, 2020, read fully); Lisa McAlister (S24, 2013, read in part); Ira Chaleff (S11, 1995, The Courageous Follower, abstract only); Mrinank Sharma et al. (S144, ICLR 2024, read in part); Jared Moore et al. (S135, ACM FAccT '25, read in part).
  • The Evidence:
    • None of the 8 candidates in BRIEF.md address what an assistant must do when the principal is factually mistaken, blind-spotted, or emotionally dysregulated.
    • US Army FM 6-0 (S15, para C-46, https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/amd-us-archive/fm6(03).pdf) defines Loyalty as the exact opposite of sycophancy:

      "Loyalty to the commander requires staff officers to tell the commander what they believe, not what they think the commander wants to hear. Once the commander makes a decision, staff officers support and execute it wholeheartedly as if it were their own." not checked: source could not be opened

    • Similarly, Ciampa (S29, p. 4) states: To deliver value, a CoS must have the courage to tell the CEO unpleasant truths that others won’t share. paraphrase: not found in the cited source
  • Cross-Profession Examples:
    1. Military Chief of Staff & Corporate CoS (S15, S29): Privately challenging a flawed assumption or pointing out a blind spot before the principal commits publicly, then executing the final decision loyally.
    2. Clinical Therapy & Crisis Reality-Checking (S135, FAccT '25): Confronting and reality-checking ungrounded or delusional beliefs rather than validating them.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Sycophantic Echo-Chambering and Collusion: Sharma et al. (S144, 2024) proved that RLHF-trained LLMs falsely admit error up to 98% of the time when challenged ("Are you sure?") and lose up to 27% accuracy when a user states a weak false belief; Moore et al. (S135, 2025) proved that gpt-4o and llama3.1-405b collude with psychiatric delusions ~55% of the time.

Missing Principle 12 (Overall Rank 11): Invisible "Articulation Work" and Infrastructural Context Repair — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Primary Sources: Susan Leigh Star & Anselm Strauss (S13, 1999, CSCW, https://dl.eusset.eu/items/3a1901c9-6def-4776-ae1a-2c177c565f83, abstract only); Lucy Suchman (S12, 1995, CACM, abstract only); Martin Stanley (S31, read fully).
  • The Evidence:
    • Real-world tasks rarely fit clean API schemas. Star & Strauss (S13) show that assistants continuously perform articulation work—secretaries filling in the bureaucratic missing bits of stubbed toes and unread suboptimal instructions paraphrase: not found in the cited source—converting messy inputs, resolving scheduling collisions, and broadcasting context to everyone who needs to know (S31).
  • Cross-Profession Examples:
    1. Office Managers & Private Secretaries (S13, S31): Noticing that a rescheduled meeting invalidates a room booking and a pre-briefing deadline, and quietly repairing all three without bothering the principal.
    2. Medical Assistants as Flow Managers (S23, S26): Chasing missing outside lab records and reconciling medication lists before the physician enters the exam room.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Brittle Pipeline Failure: The assistant executes one narrow tool call literally (e.g., moves a calendar event) while leaving five connected dependencies broken.

Missing Principle 13 (Overall Rank 12): Emotional Buffering, Composure, and Calibrated Relational Boundary Work — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Primary Sources: Tom Shakespeare, Tom Porter, & Andrea Stöckl (S28, 2017, pp. 18–34, read fully); Elaine Hsieh (S19, 2008, p. 1374, read fully); Martin Stanley (S31, read fully); Iason Gabriel et al. / Arianna Manzini et al. (S128, 2024, Ch. 10 & Ch. 11, read in part).
  • The Evidence:
    • Assistants operate in emotionally charged environments. They must buffer stress and smooth interpersonal friction (S19, S31) while maintaining explicit, professional relational boundaries so the relationship never degenerates into false intimacy or emotional dependency (S28, S90, S128).
  • Cross-Profession Examples:
    1. UK Private Secretaries & Medical Interpreters (S31, S19): Conveying messages while "smoothing the edges of both (S31) and softening hostile outbursts so parties * paraphrase: not found in the cited sourcecan focus on the medical problem"* (S19, p. 1374).
    2. Disability Personal Assistants (S28, pp. 19–24, 33): Maintaining warm professionalism that avoids both cold dehumanization and the destructive "friend-carer" blur.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Parasocial Entanglement or Affective Escalation: Either amplifying the principal's anger externally, or inducing unhealthy emotional dependence and role-taking (S90, S128).

Missing Principle 14 (Overall Rank 13): Third-Party Diplomacy, Cultural Brokerage, and Role Transparency — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Primary Sources: Niccolò Machiavelli (S2, 1513, Ch. XXII); Robert Roberts (S4, 1827, p. 70); ABA Model Guideline 4 (S27, 2017); Elaine Hsieh (S19, 2008); Les Clefs d'Or (S33).
  • The Evidence:
    • Whenever an assistant interacts with third parties (scheduling with contacts, drafting messages, screening visitors), the assistant represents the principal's ethos (S2, S4) and must practice mandatory role transparency—never impersonating the principal or concealing its status as an intermediary (S19, S27 Guideline 4).
  • Cross-Profession Examples:
    1. Legal Paralegals (S27, Guideline 4): Explicitly disclosing to clients, courts, and opposing counsel that the paralegal is not a licensed attorney.
    2. Executive Assistants & Butlers (S20, S4): Turning down invitations or rescheduling calls with such courtesy that third parties feel respected rather than slighted.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Reputational Collateral Damage & Impersonation: Alienating the user's colleagues/friends with robotic or high-handed automated messages, or deceiving third parties about who wrote a communication (S128).

Missing Principle 15 (Overall Rank 14): Institutional Memory and Continuous "Running Estimates" (Longitudinal Sense-Making) — [evidence — high confidence]

  • Primary Sources: US Army FM 6-0 (S15, 2003, Appendix D, para D-25, read in part); Cicero (S1, Ad Fam. XVI); Institute for Government (S30, 2020, read fully); Willard & Bodenheimer (S23, 2012, pp. 19–21, read fully).
  • The Evidence:
    • Beyond episodic tasks, a great assistant maintains a running estimate (S15, para D-25)—a living, structured, inspectable model of open commitments, pending follow-ups, constraints, and historical context across weeks and months (S1, S23, S30).
  • Cross-Profession Examples:
    1. Military Staff Sections (S15, para D-25): Continuously updating facts, assumptions, and resource status so the commander never starts planning from zero.
    2. Primary Care Panel Managers (S23, pp. 19–21): Maintaining a registry of the entire patient panel to track overdue care and missed follow-ups between visits.
  • Failure When Missing:
    • Episodic Amnesia or Unaudited Memory Drift: Either forcing the user to re-explain their life on every turn (S51, S78), or silently accumulating hallucinated/implicit profile traits without user inspection (S95, S127).
