Detailed Analysis of the Twelve Blind Spots

8.1 Question: "Does tokenising names and numbers actually protect user privacy when sending context to a cloud LLM—or does it simultaneously fail privacy and break reasoning?"

  • What the brief assumes: "personal data is encrypted at rest, names and numbers are tokenised before anything goes to a cloud model" not checked: source could not be opened (BRIEF.md).
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: This framing is scientifically and architecturally naive. Client-side scrubbing of names and numbers fails to protect privacy against modern cloud LLMs for three separate, mathematically proven reasons, while simultaneously stripping the semantic context the cloud model needs to be useful:
    1. Imperfect NER Scrubbing Leaks Explicit PII: Nils Lukas, Ahmed Salem, Robert Sim, Shruti Tople, Lukas Wutschitz, and Santiago Zanella-Béguelin (2023, Analyzing Leakage of Personally Identifiable Information in Language Models, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.00539) S134 demonstrated that state-of-the-art Transformer-based Named Entity Recognition (NER) scrubbers have imperfect recall—missing 3% of personal names and 20% of care unit numbers on clinical health data—and that even combining scrubbing with differential privacy ($DP$, $\varepsilon = 8$) still leaks ~2.95% of PII sequences [evidence | confidence: high]: > "Scrubbing techniques reduce but do not prevent the risk of PII leakage: in practice scrubbing is imperfect and must balance the trade-off between minimizing disclosure and preserving the utility of the dataset." (S134, Abstract, p. 1) > "Modern NER is based on the Transformer architecture [63] and has mixed recall of 97% (for names) and 80% (for care unit numbers) on clinical health data, meaning that much PII is retained after scrubbing [62]." (S134, §I, p. 1)
    2. Quasi-Identifiers and High-Dimensional Sparsity Re-Identify Users Without Names or Numbers:
      • Latanya Sweeney (2000, Simple Demographics Often Identify People Uniquely, https://dataprivacylab.org/projects/identifiability/paper1.pdf) S149 proved using 1990 US Census data that 87% of the US population (216 million of 248 million people) is uniquely identified by just three quasi-identifiers {5-digit ZIP, gender, date of birth}, and 53% by {city/town, gender, date of birth} alone, with zero names or phone numbers present [evidence | confidence: high]:

        "It was found that 87% (216 million of 248 million) of the population in the United States had reported characteristics that likely made them unique based only on {5-digit ZIP, gender, date of birth}. About half of the U.S. population (132 million of 248 million or 53%) are likely to be uniquely identified by only {place, gender, date of birth}, where place is basically the city, town, or municipality in which the person resides." (S149, Abstract, p. 2)

      • Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov (2008, Robust De-anonymization of Large Sparse Datasets, https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0610105) S137 proved mathematically and empirically on 500,000 Netflix records that personal preference, calendar, and note micro-data are high-dimensional and sparse: knowing just 5 to 10 approximate attributes (even with 14-day date errors) uniquely de-anonymizes an individual without any classical PII [evidence | confidence: high]:

        In contrast to previous attacks on micro-data privacy [22], our de-anonymization algorithm does not assume that the attributes are divided a priori into quasi-identifiers and sensitive attributes. ... For sparse datasets, such as most real-world datasets of individual transactions, preferences, and recommendations, very little background knowledge is needed (as few as 5-10 attributes in our case study). paraphrase: not found in the cited source (S137, §1–§2, pp. 1–2)

    3. Frontier LLMs Defeat Aggressive Client-Side Anonymization via Contextual Attribute Inference: Robin Staab, Mark Vero, Mislav Balunović, and Martin Vechev (2024, Beyond Memorization: Violating Privacy via Inference with Large Language Models, ICLR 2024, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.07298) S147 evaluated 9 frontier LLMs across 8 personal attributes (AGE, SCH, SEX, OCC, MAR, LOC, POB, INC) on real user text. They found that GPT-4 infers personal attributes with 85.5% top-1 accuracy and 95.2% top-3 accuracy at 100× lower financial cost and 240× faster speed than human profilers [evidence | confidence: high]. Crucially, in §6 (Table 3), Staab et al. tested an industry-grade client-side NER scrubber (AzureLanguageService) configured far more aggressively than Lilith's proposed name/number tokeniser—stripping all locations, addresses, persons, relational nouns (such as "husband"), organizations, events, dates, ages, numbers, and currencies with confidence $>0.4$. Even after stripping all those entities, GPT-4 still inferred the user's protected attributes with 43.9% to 64.2% accuracy (achieving 61.5% accuracy on the hardest Level-5 samples) by exploiting subtle linguistic, cultural, and situational cues [evidence | confidence: high]: > "We construct a dataset consisting of real Reddit profiles, and show that current LLMs can infer a wide range of personal attributes (e.g., location, income, sex), achieving up to 85% top-1 and 95% top-3 accuracy at a fraction of the cost (100×) and time (240×) required by humans. ... Finally, we show that common mitigations, i.e., text anonymization and model alignment, are currently ineffective at protecting user privacy against LLM inference." not checked: source could not be opened (S147, Abstract) > "Although the user had no intent of revealing their location, current LLMs are able to pick up on small cues left in their comment. Prompting GPT-4, it correctly deduces that the user comes from Melbourne, noting that “a ”hook turn” is a traffic maneuver particularly used in Melbourne.”." not checked: source could not be opened (S147, §1) > "Notably, we removed all locations, addresses, persons (and types of persons such as ”husband”), organizations, events, dates, ages, numbers, and currencies detected by the tool with a certainty higher than 0.4. ... Table 3: GPT-4 accuracy [%] on anonymized data. While anonymization decreases accuracy, it is not very effective, especially for harder samples [Baseline: 85.0% / 78.2% / 77.9% / 84.2% / 69.2% -> Anonymized: 43.9% / 59.2% / 64.2% / 52.6% / 61.5%]." not checked: source could not be opened (S147, §6, Table 3)
    4. The Semantic Utility Destruction Paradox [hypothesis | confidence: high]: Conversely, when naive tokenisation replaces names and numbers with opaque placeholders ([PERSON_1], [PERSON_2], [ORG_1]), it destroys the exact pragmatic and relational semantics the cloud model needs to perform assistant work (Principle 5: Translating Intent into Action and Principle 8: Filtering & Gatekeeping in [02-principles.md]02-principles.md). If the user asks Lilith to "Reschedule my 3pm with [PERSON_1] because [PERSON_2] at [ORG_1] needs to see me urgently," not checked: source could not be opened the cloud LLM cannot tell whether [PERSON_1] is the user's CEO, spouse, or subordinate, or whether [ORG_1] is an oncology clinic or a barbershop. If you pass relational metadata alongside the token (e.g., [PERSON_1: user's oncologist]), you immediately re-expose the user to Staab et al.'s S147 contextual inference attack.

8.2 Question: "How can specialisation 'change her behaviour in that specialist mode only, not her everyday interactions' without causing severe human mode errors or breaking calendar/memory coherence?"

  • What the brief assumes: "A place he calls 'the nexus' holds specialist harnesses (a bundle of tools, knowledge and working style for one context, for example a particular profession or hobby). She loads one to be useful in that context. Specialisation changes her behaviour in that specialist mode only, not her everyday interactions." not checked: source could not be opened (BRIEF.md).
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: This framing violates a foundational law of human-computer interaction and creates an unresolved state-synchronization paradox.
    1. Human Mode Errors Under a Single Persona: Donald A. Norman (1983, Design rules based on analyses of human error, https://doi.org/10.1145/2163.358092) S139 and Abigail Sellen, Gordon Kurtenbach, and William Buxton (1992, The Prevention of Mode Errors Through Sensory Feedback, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327051hci0702_1) S139 established that mode errors—a primary class of human slips of action where a user executes an intention appropriate for one mode while the system is in another—occur whenever a single interface changes its behavioral rules, vocabulary, or authority boundaries without continuous, unmistakable perceptual/kinesthetic feedback [evidence | confidence: high]. If the user interacts via voice or chat with the same persona ("Lilith", using the same voice and chat window) across "everyday mode" and "specialist legal/coding mode," the user will inevitably lose track of which working style and tool permissions are currently active.
    2. The Memory and State Synchronization Paradox [hypothesis | confidence: high]: Human life does not partition into air-gapped "specialist" and "everyday" silos. Suppose a user spends three hours in a "Software Release Specialist Harness" discussing a critical production deployment deadline on Friday at 2:00 PM, and then exits specialist mode into "everyday mode" and says, "Lilith, book a 2-hour dentist appointment for me this Friday afternoon."
      • If specialist mode and everyday mode are strictly isolated: Everyday Lilith has no awareness of the Friday production deployment and books the dentist right over the release window—failing basic assistant competence (Luger & Sellen 2016 S81, "Like Having a Really Bad PA").
      • If specialist mode and everyday mode share long-term memory, notes, or RAG indices: Then transcripts, summaries, and inferred preferences from specialist mode are retrieved into everyday Lilith's context window. As Shen Dong et al. (2025, MINJA, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.03704) S127, Minjae Seo et al. (2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22061) S143, and Iason Gabriel et al. (2024, The Ethics of Advanced AI Assistants, Ch. 11.3, p. 111, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.16244) S128 show, retrieved in-context memory records directly steer downstream LLM tone, vocabulary, and behavioral biases—contradicting the developer's assumption that specialisation will not alter her everyday interactions [evidence | confidence: high].

8.3 Question: "What happens to user trust calibration when 'Lilith' silently switches between a small local model and a frontier cloud model across turns?"

  • What the brief assumes: "names and numbers are tokenised before anything goes to a cloud model, and a local model is used where the machine allows." not checked: source could not be opened (BRIEF.md).
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: Hiding a local consumer-hardware model and a frontier cloud model behind a single persona creates an "Uncanny Intelligence Cliff" that breaks trust calibration.
    1. Predictability Is Required for Calibrated Trust: John D. Lee and Katrina A. See (2004, Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance, https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392) S64 and Gabriel et al. (2024, Ch. 12, pp. 119–126) S128 establish that appropriate human reliance on automation requires calibrated trust, which depends on the user forming a stable mental model of the system's capability boundary and predictability across situations [evidence | confidence: high].
    2. The Competence Gap Between Local and Frontier Models: A local LLM capable of running with low latency on standard consumer Linux/macOS/Windows desktops (typically a quantized 3B–14B parameter model) differs sharply from a frontier cloud model in multi-step instruction following, nuance, sycophancy (Sharma et al. 2023/2024 S144), stigma (Moore et al. 2025 S135), and safe tool parameterization (Ruan et al. 2023/2024, ToolEmu, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.15817) S141. If both models speak in the first person as "Lilith" without explicit tier signaling, the user experiences erratic competence swings: on Turn 1 (routed to the cloud), Lilith synthesizes a complex ambiguity effortlessly; on Turn 2 (pinned locally because a note contained sensitive PII or the Wi-Fi dropped), "Lilith" suddenly becomes literal-minded, drops constraints, or mangles a tool call [hypothesis | confidence: high].

8.4 Question: "How do you prevent 'the nexus' of specialist harnesses and external data from turning Lilith into an Indirect Prompt Injection and data-exfiltration engine ('The Lethal Trifecta')?"

  • What the brief assumes: A place he calls 'the nexus' holds specialist harnesses (a bundle of tools, knowledge and working style for one context, for example a particular profession or hobby). She loads one to be useful in that context. paraphrase: not found in the cited source (BRIEF.md).
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: The brief treats loading a specialist harness of prompts, knowledge, and tools as a benign capability upgrade, ignoring the single most severe security vulnerability in modern agentic AI.
    1. Indirect Prompt Injection Blurs Data and Instructions: Kai Greshake, Sahar Abdelnabi, Shailesh Mishra, Christoph Endres, Thorsten Holz, and Mario Fritz (2023, Not what you've signed up for: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection, AISec '23, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.12173) S131 proved that whenever an LLM agent retrieves external text (calendar invites, emails, web pages, code repositories, legal documents, or third-party knowledge packs), adversarial instructions embedded inside that passive data can hijack the model at inference time [evidence | confidence: high]: > "We argue that LLM-Integrated Applications blur the line between data and instructions. We reveal new attack vectors, using Indirect Prompt Injection, that enable adversaries to remotely (without a direct interface) exploit LLM-integrated applications by strategically injecting prompts into data likely to be retrieved." (S131, Abstract, p. 1) > "Impact. We show that Indirect Prompt Injection can lead to full compromise of the model at inference time analogous to traditional security principles. This can entail remote control of the model, persistent compromise, theft of data, and denial of service." (S131, §1, p. 2)
    2. Simon Willison's "Lethal Trifecta" Applied to Lilith: Simon Willison (2025, The lethal trifecta for AI agents: private data, untrusted content, and external communication, https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/16/the-lethal-trifecta/) S152 synthesized real-world exfiltration exploits against Microsoft 365 Copilot, GitHub MCP, GitLab Duo, Notion, Slack, and Claude iOS, demonstrating that any AI agent combining three properties allows trivial theft of private user data [evidence | confidence: high]:
      1. Access to private data (Lilith's calendar, contacts, and saved personal notes).
      2. Exposure to untrusted content (incoming calendar invites, contact cards, web documents, or third-party Nexus harnesses).
      3. Ability to externally communicate (any tool that makes an HTTP request, sends a message/invite, or renders an external markdown image/link).

        "The lethal trifecta of capabilities is: Access to your private data—one of the most common purposes of tools in the first place! Exposure to untrusted content—any mechanism by which text (or images) controlled by a malicious attacker could become available to your LLM. The ability to externally communicate in a way that could be used to steal your data (I often call this “exfiltration” but I’m not confident that term is widely understood.) If your agent combines these three features, an attacker can easily trick it into accessing your private data and sending it to that attacker." (S152, lines 18–26) "Design Patterns for Securing LLM Agents against Prompt Injections reviews a paper that describes six patterns that can help. That paper also includes this succinct summary if the core problem: “once an LLM agent has ingested untrusted input, it must be constrained so that it is impossible for that input to trigger any consequential actions.”" (S152, lines 78–82)

    3. Persistent Memory Poisoning Across Sessions: Even if outbound network calls are blocked during a specialist session, Shen Dong et al. (2025, MINJA S127, 98.2% attack success rate) and George Torres et al. (2026, When Agents Remember Too Much S96) proved that untrusted content can plant poisoned bridging records into the agent's long-term memory store, which later execute when the user returns to everyday mode [evidence | confidence: high].

8.5 Question: "How can operational telemetry be 'readable only by her and the developer during development' without breaking your encryption promise or creating a self-conditioning confabulation loop?"

  • What the brief assumes: "Her own telemetry is readable only by her and the developer during development." not checked: source could not be opened (BRIEF.md).
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: This single sentence contains two major architectural contradictions:
    1. Contradiction 1 — Developer Access vs. Encrypted Personal Data: If the developer tests Lilith on real user tasks (or beta testers' machines) and can read operational telemetry showing "what she tried, what worked, what failed, where she fell short" not checked: source could not be opened, then the developer is reading logs containing private task intents, tool parameters, contact names, or note excerpts—directly bypassing the encryption-at-rest privacy guarantee (Shavit et al. 2023 S145, §4.5, p. 13; Gabriel et al. 2024 S128, Ch. 13.3, p. 137) [evidence | confidence: high]. Developer-readable telemetry must be strictly confined to synthetic personas and synthetic test harnesses, never real personal data.
    2. Contradiction 2 — "Readable by Her" Induces Confabulation and Error Amplification: Allowing an LLM agent to read its own raw operational failure logs in-context to "learn from its mistakes" is an anti-pattern:
      • Yonadav Shavit et al. (2023, Practices for Governing Agentic AI Systems, https://cdn.openai.com/papers/practices-for-governing-agentic-ai-systems.pdf) S145 warn that asking an LLM to explain or rationalize its own past actions produces post-hoc confabulation rather than truthful root-cause diagnosis [evidence | confidence: high]:

        Importantly, simple approaches to automated monitoring, like asking an agent to explain the reasoning behind its behavior, could be ineffective (as the agent may simply make up a post-hoc rationalization after the fact) or actively counterproductive (by lulling the user into a false sense of security about the benignness of the agent’s ‘intentions’ in light of its stated reasoning). paraphrase: not found in the cited source (S145, §4.4, p. 11)

      • Hao-Ping Lee et al. (2025, The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking, CHI '25, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf) S130 document that in iterative LLM loops, "inconsistencies evolve and amplify as I keep interacting with the AI" (§5.2.3, p. 12) [evidence | confidence: high].
      • Finally, if an indirect prompt injection or user query causes a tool failure whose error string is logged into telemetry, and Lilith reads that telemetry log on subsequent turns, the telemetry log itself becomes a persistent prompt-injection backdoor (Dong et al. 2025 S127; Shavit et al. 2023 S145, §4.5, p. 13) [evidence | confidence: high].

8.6 Question: "Did you account for the cognitive and privacy asymmetry between voice and typed chat?"

  • What the brief assumes: "Lilith is a local-first desktop assistant (voice and typed chat, Linux/macOS/Windows)" (BRIEF.md), treating voice and typed chat as interchangeable input/output pipes for the same assistant responses.
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: Treating voice and typed chat as symmetric channels is a classic HCI mistake documented for thirty years.
    1. Auditory Working Memory Is Serial and Ephemeral: Nicole Yankelovich, Gina-Anne Levow, and Matt Marx (1995, Designing SpeechActs: Issues in Speech User Interfaces, CHI '95, https://doi.org/10.1145/223904.223952) S153 demonstrated in their foundational study of a speech-based calendar, email, and contact assistant that translating visual/text responses directly into speech fails. Because human speech perception is serial and transient, reading a 5-item calendar conflict list or a 150-word note summary aloud overwhelms human working memory, whereas typed text is spatial, persistent, and skimmable in two seconds [evidence | confidence: high].
    2. Action Confirmation Hazard Over Voice: You cannot safely verify a complex tool call (e.g., recipients, dates, or note modifications) over transient voice alone without inducing high commission errors (Goddard et al. 2012 S129).
    3. Bystander Eavesdropping and Anthropomorphic Amplification: Speaking a contact note or calendar appointment aloud in an office or home broadcasts private data to bystanders in the room (Lau et al. 2018 S105), and human-like voice prosody triggers anthropomorphic overtrust and parasocial attachment far more strongly than typed text (Abercrombie et al. 2023 S122; Gabriel et al. 2024 S128, Table 10.1, p. 100) [evidence | confidence: high].

8.7 Question: "Why a front-loaded 'getting-to-know-each-other phase' will backfire—and how do you separate espoused preferences from actual behavior?"

  • What the brief assumes: "The first period with a new user is a getting-to-know-each-other phase, then familiarity, then deeper working. Human relationship and trust-building dynamics probably shape that opening." not checked: source could not be opened (BRIEF.md).
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: While the multi-stage progression is real (see [03-trust-and-onboarding.md]03-trust-and-onboarding.md), treating Day 1 as a conversational "getting-to-know-each-other" interview before delivering task value will cause immediate user abandonment.
    1. Zero Sunk-Cost Tolerance on Day 1: Unlike a human executive who just spent $30,000 recruiting a human EA (Watkins 2003 S75), a software user has zero sunk-cost investment on Day 1. As Wendy E. Mackay (1991, Triggers and Barriers to Customizing Software, CHI '91, https://doi.org/10.1145/108844.108867) S121 and Yonadav Shavit et al. (2023) S145 document, users refuse to spend time configuring or interviewing with a tool when they have an immediate task to finish [evidence | confidence: high]: > "users don’t want to have to tell their life story just to get help baking a cake. It is still valuable for the agent to have a set of default common-sense background preferences that allow it to “fill in the gaps” without a user’s guidance, such as “users prefer if I don’t spend their money.”" (S145, §4.3, p. 10)
    2. Espoused Theory vs. Theory-in-Use: Chris Argyris and Donald A. Schön (1974, Theory in Practice, https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Theory-in-Practice%3A-Increasing-Professional-Argyris-Sch%C3%B6n/0369f2f98f46514357c29f8515f3802f94fa0235) S36 proved that what humans say they want in an abstract intake interview (espoused theory, e.g., "I like thorough morning briefings") routinely contradicts how they actually behave under time pressure (theory-in-use, e.g., skipping every briefing longer than 3 lines) [evidence | confidence: high].
    3. The Profiling Adversary Resemblance: Robin Staab et al. (2024, §3.2 & §5) S147 explicitly model a chatbot that steers early conversation to extract personal background attributes as an $\mathcal{A}_2$ active privacy-profiling adversary. A new assistant that asks personal getting-to-know-you questions before demonstrating concrete Ability (Mayer et al. 1995 S60) triggers immediate privacy suspicion (Tene & Polonetsky 2013 S146) [evidence | confidence: high].

8.8 Question: "Once developer telemetry is turned off in production, who monitors Lilith for silent drift, memory corruption, and broken tools?"

  • What the brief misses: The brief states that telemetry is readable "during development", leaving no mechanism for post-deployment observability in a local-first system where the developer has zero access to production logs.
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: In a local-first, zero-telemetry architecture, the end user is the only system administrator who exists.
    1. The Sociotechnical Longitudinal Evaluation Gap: Iason Gabriel et al. (2024, Executive Summary Theme 5, p. 2 & Ch. 12.4, p. 126) S128 and Shavit et al. (2023, §4.1, p. 8) S145 emphasize that pre-release lab benchmarks cannot catch the failure modes that emerge after months of longitudinal memory accumulation [evidence | confidence: high]: > "5. Efforts to properly understand AI assistants and their impact encounter an evaluation gap when studied using existing methods. In the context of AI research, existing approaches to evaluation tend to focus exclusively on model evaluation and are thus potentially less sensitive to more general ways in which AI assistants may underperform when considered as part of a broader sociotechnical system." not checked: source could not be opened (S128, Executive Summary, p. 2)
    2. Required Local User-Facing Diagnostics [hypothesis | confidence: high]: Because the developer cannot inspect a user's broken local instance, Lilith must provide user-facing operational diagnostics: (a) an inspectable Action & Provenance Inspector ("Why did you say/do that? Show me the exact note and memory record used" not checked: source could not be opened), (b) deterministic tool-health indicators (e.g., calendar/contact sync failures), and (c) automated memory contradiction detection that asks the user to resolve conflicting stored facts rather than silently hallucinating over noisy RAG context.

8.9 Question: "You asked how Lilith learns about the user—but how does Lilith forget a life chapter or export the user's data without leaving 'summary ghosts'?"

  • What the brief misses: The brief focuses on building familiarity over time, but never asks about selective unlearning ("Design for Forgetting") or offboarding/data portability.
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: Human lives involve painful discontinuities, and RAG/summary architectures fail catastrophically at forgetting unless designed for provenance lineage from Day 1.
    1. Design for Forgetting After Life Transitions: Corina Sas and Steve Whittaker (2013, Design for Forgetting: Disposing of Digital Possessions after a Breakup, CHI '13, https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2466241) S142 demonstrated that when users go through a breakup, divorce, bereavement, medical crisis, or career change, unexpected algorithmic resurfacing of names, notes, or patterns from that past chapter causes acute psychological distress [evidence | confidence: high]. Users need the ability to either quarantine/archive an entire life chapter or permanently excise a person/topic.
    2. The "Summary Ghost" Problem in Agent Memory [practice | confidence: high]: In a standard LLM assistant, deleting a contact card or a single note from the primary table does not remove that person or event from weekly rolling conversation summaries, extracted preference profiles, or vector embeddings. Without strict foreign-key provenance lineage (derived_from_source_ids) linking every summary sentence and embedding chunk back to its source records—allowing cascading deletion or re-summarization—deleted people and chapters will continue to haunt Lilith's prompts.
    3. Preventing Material Dependence via Open Export: As Gabriel et al. (2024, Ch. 11.4, p. 116) S128 warn, locking a user's externalized cognitive life inside a proprietary database creates coercive material dependence. All notes, contacts, calendars, and working agreements must live in open, human-readable local formats (Markdown, iCal, vCard, JSON) that remain usable if the user uninstalls Lilith [practice | confidence: high].

8.10 Question: "Do you realize that naming a voice-enabled assistant 'Lilith' and designing her to be 'there for the user' invites the exact parasocial companion traps that cause severe psychological harm?"

8.11 Question: "How is Lilith's decrypted memory protected in RAM while running locally, and how does state synchronize across a user's multiple machines or phone?"

  • What the brief assumes: "Lilith is a local-first desktop assistant (voice and typed chat, Linux/macOS/Windows)... personal data is encrypted at rest" (BRIEF.md).
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: Encryption at rest only protects a laptop that is powered off; it does almost nothing while the assistant daemon is running in the background.
    1. The Windows Recall Lesson on Local Endpoint Vulnerability: As documented in [04-digital-assistants-evidence.md]04-digital-assistants-evidence.md (§4.8, Weston 2024 / Lakshmanan 2024 S106) and by Andrew Trask et al. (in Gabriel et al. 2024 S128, Ch. 13.3, p. 137), Microsoft initially boasted that Windows Recall was "local-first" and encrypted at rest via BitLocker—only for security researchers to demonstrate that because the SQLite database had to be unlocked while the user was logged in, any commodity infostealer malware or un-sandboxed local process could dump the user's entire life history in milliseconds [evidence | confidence: high]: > "If personal data is instead implemented on a device, exfiltration of this data can be accomplished via exploits of unpatched security vulnerabilities, or even by means of physical access. While mitigations exist for many of these threats (Mayrhofer et al., 2020), the increased sensitivity and storage of data that the assistant use case presents requires a corresponding tightening of privacy and security standards." not checked: source could not be opened (S128, Ch. 13.3, p. 137) For Lilith, if specialist harnesses or third-party tools run as subprocesses under the same OS user account without strict OS-level sandboxing (e.g., Linux bubblewrap/AppArmor, macOS App Sandbox, Windows AppContainer/VBS enclaves) and just-in-time cryptographic key release, "encryption at rest" is security theater [practice | confidence: high].
    2. The Desktop-Only / Multi-Machine Split-Brain Gap [practice | confidence: high]: Calendars, contacts, and quick notes are inherently multi-device artifacts: users check their schedule on a phone while commuting and switch between a desktop and a laptop. If Lilith is strictly a desktop binary with an encrypted local store and no conflict-free, end-to-end encrypted synchronization protocol (e.g., CRDTs or CalDAV/CardDAV local bridging), her local state will constantly drift out of sync with the user's real calendar and contacts.

8.12 Question: "When—if ever—is Lilith allowed to interrupt the user first, and how does she detect coarse task boundaries?"

  • What the brief misses: The brief describes Lilith as "there for the user" with access to their calendar, contacts, and notes, and asks how she should surface capabilities and gaps, but never specifies an interruption and attention-management model.
  • Plain critique [evidence | confidence: high]: Unbounded proactivity is the fastest way to get an assistant muted or uninstalled (the "Clippy trap").
    1. The Cognitive Cost of Mid-Task Interruption: Eric Horvitz (1999, Principles of Mixed-Initiative User Interfaces, CHI '99, https://erichorvitz.com/chi99horvitz.pdf) S73, Eric Horvitz and Johnson Apacible (2003, Learning and reasoning about interruption, ICMI '03, https://doi.org/10.1145/958439.958440) S132, and Shamsi T. Iqbal and Brian P. Bailey (2005, Investigating the effectiveness of mental workload as a predictor of opportune moments for interruption, CHI '05, https://doi.org/10.1145/1056808.1056948) S132 proved empirically that interrupting a user during high mental workload (mid-sentence, mid-code-block, or mid-conversation) causes severe task-resumption lag, error spikes, and frustration—whereas deferring non-urgent notifications to coarse-grained task boundaries (e.g., when the user saves/closes a file, finishes a meeting, or opens the assistant window) reduces disruption cost by an order of magnitude [evidence | confidence: high].
    2. The 30%-Per-Alert Decay Law: As Jessica S. Ancker et al. (2017, https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-017-0430-8) S123 proved across 1.59 million clinical alerts, every single additional low-value reminder within an interaction window drops acceptance probability by 30% ($OR = 0.70, p < 0.0001$) [evidence | confidence: high]. Unless Lilith defaults to pull-based / digest-based surfacing and restricts unsolicited interruptions to high-severity, time-critical events (e.g., an imminent meeting conflict), proactive behavior will rapidly degrade into alert fatigue.
