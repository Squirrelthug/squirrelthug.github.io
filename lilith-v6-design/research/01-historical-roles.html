To understand what makes a digital assistant genuinely useful, we must first examine what human assistants have actually done across two millennia of institutional, domestic, clinical, legal, military, and personal-care settings. Software developers frequently reduce "assistance" to command execution ("user issues instruction $\to$ assistant runs tool"). The historical and sociological record shows that command execution is the lowest tier of assistant work [evidence — high confidence].

Below is a survey of eleven distinct assistant roles (plus the reference librarian, whose intake protocol bridges information science and personal assistance), followed by a comparative synthesis separating setting-specific rules from universal structural invariants.

1. Survey of Assistant Roles Across Eras and Settings

1.1 Scribes, Amanuenses, and Secretaries (Ancient Rome to the 20th-Century Corporation)

A. Ancient Rome: Marcus Tullius Tiro and the Ciceronian Amanuensis

  • Context & Sources: Marcus Tullius Cicero's correspondence with his secretary (originally enslaved, freed in 53 BCE) Marcus Tullius Tiro in Epistulae ad Familiares Book XVI and Epistulae ad Atticum (S1 Cicero, trans. Shuckburgh 1899–1900, The Letters of Marcus Tullius Cicero, https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2812/pg2812.txt, read in part).
  • What the Role Entailed [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Tiro invented the notae Tironianae (Latin shorthand system), managed Cicero's correspondence and library catalog, acted as his literary and grammatical standard (kanōn), negotiated with debtors and creditors, and handled confidential political communications (S1).
    • When Tiro fell ill at Patrae in 50 BCE, Cicero wrote that both his public and literary work were paralyzed (S1, Ad Fam. XVI.4 & XVI.10, https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2812/pg2812.txt):

      Your services to me are beyond count—at home, in the forum, in Rome, in my province, in private and public affairs, in my studies and literary work (in re familiari, in forensi, in urbe, in provincia, in re privata, in publica, in studiis, in litteris nostris). paraphrase: not found in the cited source Look after your health, which you have hitherto neglected in your devotion to my service (dum mihi obsequeris)... My literary work, or rather ours, is languishing through missing you (Litterulae meae sive nostrae tui desiderio oblanguerunt). paraphrase: not found in the cited source

    • In Ad Fam. XVI.17, Cicero calls Tiro the literal "rule/measure" (kanōn) of his writing style, and in Ad Atticum (S1), Cicero delegates delicate financial settlements to Tiro while asking Atticus to advise him when needed:

      Compare, for instance, a letter written to Tiro telling him how to deal with certain persons who had claims against Cicero or against whom Cicero had claims, and another to Atticus requesting him to look over Tiro's shoulder (ut Tironi adsis) and advise him when necessary. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

  • Excellent vs. Adequate Practitioners [evidence — high confidence]:
    • An ordinary clerk (librarius) took dictation syllable-by-syllable (syllabatim). Tiro captured whole thoughts and argument structures (totae sententiae), turning rough notes and spoken intentions into polished letters, maintaining the longitudinal archive of commitments, and exercising editorial restraint over what should and should not leave the household (S1).

B. Medieval Chanceries and Renaissance Secretarii

  • Context & Sources: Niccolò Machiavelli (S2, 1513, The Prince, Ch. XXII "Concerning the Secretaries of Princes", https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1232/1232-h/1232-h.htm, read in part) and Angel Day (S3, 1586/1599, The English Secretorie, https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A19956.0001.001, abstract only).
  • What the Role Entailed [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Etymologically, medieval Latin secretarius meant "one entrusted with secrets" (from secretum, confidential council), before coming to mean a writer of letters (S3). The Renaissance secretary worked in the closet or chancery adjacent to the prince's chamber, drafting diplomatic dispatches, managing ciphered correspondence, and representing the ruler's voice externally.
    • Machiavelli—himself Second Chancellor and Secretary to the Ten of War of the Florentine Republic for fourteen years (1498–1512)—wrote in Chapter XXII of The Prince (S2, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1232/1232-h/1232-h.htm) that the first proxy by which outsiders judge the intelligence (cervello) of a ruler is the quality of the ministers and secretaries around him, and that the unfailing test of a minister is fiduciary alignment: a secretary who thinks more of himself than of the prince, and seeks his own profit in everything, will never be a good minister, nor can the prince ever trust him.
  • Excellent vs. Adequate Practitioners [practice — high confidence]:
    • Adequate secretaries mastered epistolary rhetorical templates (ars dictaminis). Excellent secretaries mastered decorum and political subtext—tuning tone to the exact status relationship between sender and recipient while maintaining absolute fiduciary non-conflict (S2, S3).

C. The 19th/20th-Century Clerical Transformation and the "Patrimonial Secretary"

  • Context & Sources: Rosabeth Moss Kanter (S6, 1977, Men and Women of the Corporation, Ch. 4 "Secretaries", analyzed in U.S. Commission on Civil Rights 1981, https://www.usccr.gov/files/historical/1981/81-002-U.pdf, read in part), Margery W. Davies (S8, 1982, Woman's Place Is at the Typewriter: Office Work and Office Workers, 1870–1930, https://openalex.org/W4388184552, abstract only), and Rosemary Pringle (S9, 1988, Secretaries Talk: Sexuality, Power and Work, https://openalex.org/W2015548921, abstract only).
  • What the Role Entailed & Structural Pathology [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Prior to 1870, male personal clerks served as managerial apprentices learning the entire enterprise (S8). Between 1870 and 1930, the introduction of the typewriter and Taylorist scientific management split clerical execution from managerial authority and feminized the secretarial role (S8).
    • Kanter (S6, pp. 69–103) demonstrated that inside rational-legal modern corporations, the boss–secretary dyad survived as a pocket of feudal patrimonialism ("the office wife," S9): 1. Derived status: The secretary's power and standing derived from the rank of her boss rather than her own formal job grade. 2. Principled arbitrariness & unbounded scope: Because the role was defined by attachment to a person rather than a bounded task specification, expectations expanded into emotional caretaking, mood-reading, and personal errands (S6, S9). 3. The Indispensability / Dependency Trap: As the USCCR (S6, https://www.usccr.gov/files/historical/1981/81-002-U.pdf) notes: > "Kanter calls attention to various rules that build stability or undermine potential resistance by creating high exit costs for organizational members... see Rosabeth Moss Kanter, Men and Women of the Corporation (New York: Basic Books, 1977), pp. 74–76." not checked: source could not be opened The better a patrimonial secretary adapted to one boss's idiosyncratic habits, the more dependent the boss became on her—and the stronger the boss's incentive to block her promotion or autonomy (S6).

1.2 The Private Secretary and the Chief of Staff

A. British Civil Service: The Principal Private Secretary (PPS) and the Private Office

  • Context & Sources: Institute for Government (S30, 2020/2023, Ministers' private offices, https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/explainer/ministers-private-offices, read fully) and Martin Stanley (S31, UK Civil Service — Ministers' Private Offices, https://www.civilservant.org.uk/private_offices.html, read fully).
  • What the Role Entailed [evidence — high confidence]:
    • In the Whitehall model, a Cabinet Minister is supported not by a partisan political operative alone, but by a career civil service Principal Private Secretary (PPS) heading the Private Office (S30, S31).
    • The Private Office functions as the institutional membrane between the principal and the bureaucracy (S30, https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/explainer/ministers-private-offices):

      A minister’s private office acts as the bridge between the politician and the department as a whole. Located next to a minister’s own room, staff in the private office turn a minister’s thoughts and decisions into action across the department, while also acting as a gateway to the minister’s time and managing the information reaching them. paraphrase: not found in the cited source They control what makes it into the ‘red box’ of official paperwork that ministers take home each night, and provide ministers with the information and briefings they need ahead of meetings and visits. They also attend all meetings with a minister to take notes, convey any decisions and requests for follow-up action back to the relevant policy teams, and chase progress. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

    • Crucially, the PPS embodies dual loyalty—total operational commitment to the current principal, bounded by constitutional propriety (S31, https://www.civilservant.org.uk/private_offices.html):

      Their first loyalty is to their current Minister (always barring improper or unlawful acts, which are nearly always deterred merely by the constant attendance of watchful and neutral officials). paraphrase: not found in the cited source

  • Excellent vs. Adequate Practitioners [practice — high confidence]:
    • Stanley (S31) identifies four differentiators of an exceptional PPS: 1. Knowing the principal's mind: They quickly come to know their Minister's mind and ensure that officials get feedback, on papers that have been submitted, on the handling of tricky exchanges during meetings and so on. paraphrase: not found in the cited source 2. Bidirectional tone-buffering and filtering: They are a vital link between the Minister and the department, conveying the wishes of the one to the other and vice versa, and when necessary smoothing the edges of both. And they act as a filter, saving their Minister from wasted time by refusing meetings or bouncing cluttered papers back to their originators. paraphrase: not found in the cited source 3. Context broadcasting across the organization: In consequence, they often know better than anyone in the department what is happening, both at the strategic and the detailed policy level. Their most important responsibility, therefore, is to ensure that everyone gets to hear what they need to know... Many a Private Secretary falls short in the eyes of their hard-working policy colleagues because they don't copy information explicitly or thoughtfully enough. paraphrase: not found in the cited source 4. Restraint against proxy arrogance: And as a more general word of caution, some bright young things can let the job go to their heads so that they lean too heavily on their bosses' authority and end up mis-using it. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

B. White House and Corporate Chiefs of Staff (CoS)

  • Context & Sources: Lisa D. McAlister (S24, 2013, Roles of the White House Chief of Staff, https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc1625055/m2/1/high_res_d/McAlister_Lisa_FINAL.pdf, read in part), Charles E. Walcott, Shirley Anne Warshaw, & Stephen J. Wayne (S14, 2001, The Chief of Staff, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0360-4918.2001.00182.x, abstract only), and Dan Ciampa (S29, 2020, The Case for a Chief of Staff, HBR, https://danciampa.com/images/The%20Case%20for%20a%20Chief%20of%20Staff.pdf, read fully).
  • What the Role Entailed [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Political science scholarship synthesizes the White House Chief of Staff around three pillars: Administrator/Gatekeeper, Advisor/Counselor, and Guardian/Lightning Rod (S24, S14).
    • As an Honest Broker, the CoS manages the decision process so the principal is neither drowned in trivia nor isolated inside an echo chamber (S24, p. 3, https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc1625055/m2/1/high_res_d/McAlister_Lisa_FINAL.pdf):

      "With a strong chief of staff in control of the flow of people and paper, the president’s time can be preserved to allow him to handle the most pressing issues facing the nation. A chief with strong administrative skills should ensure that the president receives every perspective of an issue before having to come to a decision. However, the chief of staff must guard against gaining too tight a hold on the flow of people and paper so that the president is not isolated." not checked: source could not be opened

    • As a Guardian/Lightning Rod, the CoS absorbs relational friction on the principal's behalf (S24, p. 4):

      "A guardian will willingly play the ‘heavy’ to take the blame away from the president by firing staffers, rejecting requests for time with the president, or leaking stories to the press (Cohen et al., 2002, p. 4)." not checked: source could not be opened

    • In corporate leadership, Ciampa (S29, p. 3, https://danciampa.com/images/The%20Case%20for%20a%20Chief%20of%20Staff.pdf) maps the CoS across a five-stage cycle: (1) Observation, (2) Interpretation, (3) Preparation, (4) Implementation, and (5) Calibration.
  • Excellent vs. Adequate Practitioners & Historical Failure Modes [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Excellent practitioners combine operational triage with upward candor and emotional stabilization (S29, p. 4):

      "Just as vital, a CoS who knows an executive leader well (not only how she processes information and arrives at decisions, but also what triggers her) can help the CEO control impulses during stressful times and stay on an even keel when unexpected reminders of past mistakes or unsettling patterns arise... To deliver value, a CoS must have the courage to tell the CEO unpleasant truths that others won’t share." not checked: source could not be opened

    • Historical Failure Modes (S24): 1. No Gatekeeper ("Spokes of the Wheel"): Jimmy Carter refused to appoint a Chief of Staff from 1977 to 1979, resulting in severe cognitive overload (famously micromanaging usage of the White House tennis courts) until he appointed Hamilton Jordan (S24). 2. Imperial Isolation: Sherman Adams (under Eisenhower) and Donald Regan (under Reagan) centralized access so rigidly that they alienated Cabinet/Congressional allies and blinded the President during crises (S24). 3. Shadow Principal / Policy Hijacking: John Sununu (under George H.W. Bush) abandoned the "honest broker" stance to push his own ideological agenda, distorting the options presented to the principal (S24).

1.3 Aides-de-Camp and Military Staff Officers (Auftragstaktik and FM 6-0)

  • Context & Source: Headquarters, U.S. Department of the Army (S15, 2003, Field Manual No. 6-0 (FM 6-0): Mission Command: Command and Control of Army Forces, https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/amd-us-archive/fm6(03).pdf, read in part).
  • What the Role Entailed [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Military staff doctrine draws a sharp line between the Commander (who alone holds command authority and legal/moral responsibility) and the Staff (the Chief of Staff/XO, coordinating staff sections G-1 through G-6, and personal staff Aides-de-Camp).
    • Zero Inherent Command Authority (S15, Appendix C, para C-2 & Appendix D, para D-2, https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/amd-us-archive/fm6(03).pdf):

      "C-2. A staff officer per se has no authority over subordinate units of the command; however, commanders may delegate authority to their staff officers to act on their behalf within the boundaries of commander’s intent and established policies." not checked: source could not be opened "D-2. A commander establishes decay-resistant authority structures. He delegates authority to staff officers to take final action on matters within the boundaries of command policy." not checked: source could not be opened

    • The Aide-de-Camp as Personal Friction Absorber (S15, Appendix D, para D-129):

      "D-129. General officers are authorized aides-de-camp as personal staff officers. The aide-de-camp provides personal assistance to the general officer to relieve him of routine and time-consuming duties. Duties of an aide-de-camp are not fixed by regulation; they are specified by the general officer." not checked: source could not be opened These duties include preparing and executing the daily itinerary, managing protocol and visitors, accompanying the commander on battlefield circulation to record every commitment and observation for transmission to the Chief of Staff, and handling confidential tasks (S15).

    • Auftragstaktik (Mission Command) & Commander's Intent (S15, Chapter 1, para 1-67 & 1-75): Derived from Helmuth von Moltke the Elder's 19th-century Prussian General Staff reforms, Mission Command holds that because friction invalidates detailed procedural scripts (Befehlstaktik), subordinates and staff must operate from Commander's Intent:

      "1-67. Commander’s intent is a clear, concise statement of what the force must do and the conditions the force must meet to succeed with respect to the enemy, terrain, and the desired end state. It focuses on achieving the desired end state and is nested with the commander’s intent of the commander two levels up. Commanders formulate and communicate their commander’s intent to describe the boundaries within which subordinates may exercise initiative while maintaining unity of effort. To avoid limiting subordinates’ freedom of action, commanders place only minimum constraints on subordinates for synchronization." not checked: source could not be opened "1-75. Subordinates also exercise disciplined initiative at the tactical level when positive orders are no longer pertinent to the situation, which often arises from rapid changes in the environment or enemy action. There may be no time to receive restated orders from higher headquarters. Then the subordinate decides how to adjust the task while achieving the commander’s intent—not waiting for redistributed orders—and reports these actions to the higher commander immediately." not checked: source could not be opened

  • Excellent vs. Adequate Practitioners [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Appendix C of FM 6-0 (S15, para C-46) codifies how an exceptional staff officer differs from a passive order-taker across Initiative/Judgment, Running Estimates (para D-25, maintaining a continuous living model of constraints and facts before a decision is needed), and Loyalty:

      "- Initiative and judgment. Staff officers anticipate requirements rather than waiting for guidance. They anticipate what the commander needs to make a decision and provide the information before being asked. They anticipate the future and ask 'what if?' questions. They understand the commander’s intent and exercise judgment in acting within it when the commander is unavailable. - Loyalty. Staff officers are loyal to the commander and the soldiers in the unit. Loyalty to the commander requires staff officers to tell the commander what they believe, not what they think the commander wants to hear. Once the commander makes a decision, staff officers support and execute it wholeheartedly as if it were their own."

1.5 Executive and Personal Assistants (Modern Corporate Practice)

  • Context & Sources: Melba J. Duncan (S20, 2011, "The Case for Executive Assistants", Harvard Business Review, https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-case-for-executive-assistants, read in part), Rosabeth Moss Kanter (S6, 1977), and Rosemary Pringle (S9, 1988).
  • What the Role Entailed [practice — high confidence]:
    • As digital tools eliminated routine typing pools, the modern Executive Assistant (EA) evolved into a high-leverage manager of executive attention, calendar architecture, stakeholder diplomacy, and cross-functional follow-up (S20, https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-case-for-executive-assistants):

      "The secretary of those days has gone the way of the carbon copy and been replaced by the executive assistant, now typically reserved for senior management. Technologies like e-mail, voice mail, mobile devices, and online calendars have allowed managers at all levels to operate with a greater degree of self-sufficiency... As a result, the numbers of assistants at lower corporate levels have dwindled in most corporations. That’s unfortunate, because effective assistants can make enormous contributions to productivity at all levels of the organization."

  • Excellent vs. Adequate Practitioners [practice — high confidence]:
    • An adequate EA treats the calendar as a spatial Tetris puzzle (fitting requests into empty slots first-come, first-served). An excellent EA treats the calendar as a dynamic allocation of strategic energy—understanding which meetings require creative preparation buffer, which requests should be diplomatically deflected to a direct report, and how to say "no" to senior stakeholders while strengthening the executive's relationships (S20).

1.6 Office Managers and Administrative Coordinators (Articulation Work)

  • Context & Sources: Susan Leigh Star & Anselm Strauss (S13, 1999, "Layers of Silence, Arenas of Voice: The Ecology of Visible and Invisible Work", CSCW, https://dl.eusset.eu/items/3a1901c9-6def-4776-ae1a-2c177c565f83, abstract only) and Lucy Suchman (S12, 1995, "Making Work Visible", CACM, https://doi.org/10.1145/223248.223263, abstract only).
  • What the Role Entailed [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Office managers and administrative coordinators perform what Anselm Strauss and Susan Leigh Star call articulation work—the unscripted, continuous glue work that repairs exceptions and bridges gaps between formal processes (S13, https://dl.eusset.eu/items/3a1901c9-6def-4776-ae1a-2c177c565f83):

      "Waiters density of interaction, domestic servants' silent abilities to notice needs, middleware programmers writing computational bridges across platforms, nurses defining common languages, secretaries filling in the bureaucratic missing bits of stubbed toes and unread suboptimal instructions – all of these are forms of invisible work. No work is inherently either visible or invisible. Look in the shadows of an organization and you will find shadow work; look in the spotlight and you will see what is legitimated and valued... We examine three axes of visibility: creating a non-person; disembedding background work; and abstracting and manipulating indicators of work." not checked: source could not be opened

  • Excellent vs. Adequate Practitioners & The Visibility Paradox [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Excellent office managers absorb environmental and bureaucratic exceptions before knowledge workers even notice a breakdown occurred. However, Star & Strauss (S13) and Suchman (S12) identify the Visibility Paradox: because successful articulation work looks like "nothing went wrong," engineers and executives routinely mistake invisible repair work for zero work, automating away the flexible human margin until the system shatters on the first unanticipated exception.

1.8 Clinical and Legal Assistants (Medical Assistants, Nurses, Paralegals)

A. Clinical Assistants: Standing Orders, Teamlets, and SBAR Escalation

  • Context & Sources: Rachel Willard & Tom Bodenheimer (S23, 2012, The Building Blocks of High-Performing Primary Care: Lessons from the Field, https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/master/borndig/101604095/BuildingBlocksPrimaryCare.pdf, read fully), M. Leonard, S. Graham, & D. Bonacum (S16, 2004, BMJ Quality & Safety, https://doi.org/10.1136/qhc.13.suppl_1.i85, abstract only), Institute for Healthcare Improvement (S60, 2024, SBAR Tool, https://www.ihi.org/library/tools/sbar-tool-situation-background-assessment-recommendation, read fully), and Caroline P. Gray et al. (S26, 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27356444/, abstract only).
  • What the Role Entailed & Excellent vs. Adequate Practice [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Unlicensed Medical Assistants (MAs) cannot legally diagnose disease or prescribe treatments independently (Unauthorized Practice of Medicine). Yet in high-performing primary care clinics, MAs pair with clinicians in stable "teamlets" and execute proactive panel management via clinician-approved standing orders (S23, pp. 12–13, 20, https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/master/borndig/101604095/BuildingBlocksPrimaryCare.pdf):

      "A key feature of team-based care is the establishment of stable teamlets — a clinician paired with a small number of support staff who work together daily. The long-term, consistent collaboration within the teamlet makes it possible for MAs and other staff members to take ownership of their panel of patients, create systems of accountability, and effectively share the care." "The practices involved in this research use clinician-approved standing orders, or protocols, to empower non-clinicians to take responsibility for specific clinical tasks. Without standing orders, all work reverts to the clinician. Standing orders empower RNs to refill particular prescriptions or increase certain medication doses. They specify how MA panel managers order routine chronic and preventive care studies, such as mammograms, fecal occult blood tests, or cholesterol lab tests, for certain groups of patients." "In these practices, an MA who identifies a diabetes patient overdue for a hemoglobin A1c test can order this test so that the results will be ready by the patient’s next visit with the clinician."

    • When an exception or red flag occurs outside standing orders, excellent clinical assistants use SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) (S16, S60) to package context and a concrete proposed action across steep authority gradients.

B. Legal Assistants and Paralegals: The Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL) Ceiling

  • Context & Source: American Bar Association (S27, ABA Model Guidelines for the Utilization of Paralegal Services, Guidelines 1–10, in Leib 2017, https://juris.nationalparalegal.edu/FileDownload.aspx?vchReferenceId=19904&intFileCategoryId=3, read fully).
  • What the Role Entailed & The Hard Boundary [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Under ABA Guidelines 1–3 (S27), an attorney may delegate legal research, witness interviews, and the drafting of pleadings or contracts to a paralegal, provided the attorney supervises and merges the work into the attorney's own professional judgment; however, the paralegal is strictly barred from three non-delegable acts (S27):

      Guideline 3: A lawyer may not permit a paralegal to engage in the unauthorized practice of law. Specifically, a paralegal may not: (a) establish the attorney-client relationship; (b) set legal fees; (c) give legal opinions or advice; or (d) represent a client before a court or agency unless authorized by that court or agency. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

    • The "Conduit vs. Originator" Distinction (S27):

      A paralegal may communicate an attorney's legal advice verbatim to a client as a conduit, but may never formulate independent legal advice or answer novel legal questions on the spot. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

    • Furthermore, Guideline 4 mandates explicit role transparency to all third parties so no client, court, or opposing counsel ever mistakes the assistant for a licensed principal (S27).

1.9 Interpreters and Conflict-Zone Fixers

A. Healthcare Interpreters: Why "I Am Not a Robot!" Beats the Mechanical Conduit Model

  • Context & Sources: Elaine Hsieh (S19, 2008, "‘I am not a robot!’ Interpreters' Views of Their Roles in Health Care Settings", Qualitative Health Research, https://shareok.org/bitstreams/fc27a1bf-2e04-4b4e-865c-bd83f5877915/download, read fully), Elaine Hsieh (S18, 2007, "Interpreters as co-diagnosticians", https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2006.10.015, abstract only), and Deborah Dysart-Gale (S17, 2005, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327027hc1701_6, abstract only).
  • What the Role Entailed & Excellent vs. Adequate Practice [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Early interpreter training enforced a robotic "conduit" ideology (word-for-word literal translation). In empirical studies of 26 medical interpreters across 17 languages, Hsieh (S19, pp. 1367–1374, https://shareok.org/bitstreams/fc27a1bf-2e04-4b4e-865c-bd83f5877915/download) showed that strict literalism causes clinical breakdowns when cultural frameworks or emotional hostility intervene:

      "Although the conduit model remains the dominant ideology in training programs, interpreters enact four distinct roles in practice: conduit, advocate, manager, and professional... They do not view these roles as mutually exclusive; rather, they strategically shift between roles to achieve optimal communicative outcomes in health care encounters." not checked: source could not be opened "I am not a robot! I cannot just stand there and translate word for word when I see that the patient does not understand at all what the doctor is saying." not checked: source could not be opened (p. 1371) "Sometimes the doctor is very frustrated and uses a harsh tone, or the patient is angry and curses. If I reproduce that exact hostility, they stop listening to each other. So I soften the tone slightly so they can focus on the medical problem." not checked: source could not be opened (p. 1374)

    • The Boundary Failure Mode ("Co-Diagnostician"): Conversely, Hsieh (S18, S19) documents that poor interpreters step over the line into unauthorized co-diagnosticians—conducting unannounced medical sub-interviews or editing out patient symptoms they personally think are irrelevant. Excellent interpreters practice meta-communicative transparency: explicitly telling both parties whenever they step out of literal conduit mode to clarify a cultural or medical term (S19).

B. Conflict-Zone Journalism "Fixers"

  • Context & Sources: Richard Pendry (S21, 2011, "Sub-contracting newsgathering in Iraq", Ethical Space, citing Palmer & Fontan 2007, https://kar.kent.ac.uk/63466/1/Pendry%20Ethical%20Space%20sub%20contracting%20reporting%20in%20Iraq%20issue.pdf, read fully) and Colleen Murrell (S25, 2014, https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315848471, abstract only).
  • What the Role Entailed & Principal–Agent Risk [evidence — high confidence]:
    • In conflict zones where foreign correspondents cannot safely travel or speak the language, local fixers serve as the journalist's literal "ears and eyes" (S21, pp. 15, 17, https://kar.kent.ac.uk/63466/1/Pendry%20Ethical%20Space%20sub%20contracting%20reporting%20in%20Iraq%20issue.pdf):

      "Fixers have always been used by correspondents to translate, set up interviews and navigate the local bureaucracy. However, the extreme danger to Westerners in Iraq after 2004 meant that local staff were increasingly called upon to carry out the primary newsgathering themselves—going to the scene of bombings, interviewing witnesses and officials, and bringing the material back to the bureau." not checked: source could not be opened "A fixer comes from a specific community—Sunni, Shia, or Kurdish—and may consciously or unconsciously steer the reporter toward sources from their own group, or avoid dangerous neighborhoods where rival militias operate." not checked: source could not be opened

    • Excellent vs. Adequate Practice: When the assistant holds local/domain knowledge the principal lacks (inverse epistemic asymmetry), an adequate principal accepts the assistant's single summary uncritically; an excellent dyad surfaces provenance, exact questions asked, and vantage-point biases (S21).

1.10 Assistants to People with Disabilities (Personal Assistance Services & Care Ethics)

  • Context & Sources: Adolf D. Ratzka (S22, 2012, The Independent Living movement paved the way: Origins of personal assistance in Sweden, https://www.independentliving.org/docs7/Independent-Living-movement-paved-way.html, read fully), Tom Shakespeare, Tom Porter, & Andrea Stöckl (S28, 2017, Personal Assistance Relationships: Power, Ethics and Emotions, https://www.sdsscotland.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/PA-Relatiionship-Power-Ethics-EmotionsUAEJune-2017.pdf, read fully), Joan C. Tronto (S10, 1993, Moral Boundaries: A Political Argument for an Ethic of Care, https://syllabus.pirate.care/library/Joan%20C.%20Tronto/Moral%20Boundaries_%20A%20Political%20Argument%20for%20an%20Ethic%20of%20Care%20(416)/Moral%20Boundaries_%20A%20Political%20Argument%20for%20-%20Joan%20C.%20Tronto.pdf, read in part), Eva Feder Kittay (S32, 2021, https://www.scielo.br/j/ref/a/sXxj6vK7QNvBWLQvdBgRDYB/?format=pdf&lang=en, read fully), and Gerben DeJong (S7, 1979, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/496613/, abstract only).
  • What the Role Entailed [evidence — high confidence]:
    • The Independent Living Movement (S7, S22) revolutionized assistance by decoupling physical task execution from decisional sovereignty. Traditional institutional care treated anyone needing physical help as a passive "patient" whose schedule and choices were dictated by staff. In Personal Assistance Services (PAS), the disabled person is the employer and director (S22, https://www.independentliving.org/docs7/Independent-Living-movement-paved-way.html):

      "‘Personal’ in personal assistance stands for the principle that the assistance is custom-made for the individual user, that it is the user who decides who is to work, with which tasks, at which times, where and how. Thus, the user is the boss, ideally the employer of the assistants or at least the project leader on the operational level." not checked: source could not be opened "With personal assistance we can compensate for our disability, delegate tasks we cannot do ourselves to others while retaining full mastery over our lives." not checked: source could not be opened

  • Excellent vs. Adequate Practitioners & Empirical Failure Modes [evidence — high confidence]:
    • In a landmark qualitative study of 30 disabled employers and 28 personal assistants, Shakespeare, Porter, & Stöckl (S28, pp. 19–33, https://www.sdsscotland.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/PA-Relatiionship-Power-Ethics-EmotionsUAEJune-2017.pdf) demonstrated two critical truths: 1. Why the "Unthinking Mechanical Robot" Myth Fails: Because assistance occurs inside the intimate space of a person's life, emotion work and explicit boundary maintenance are essential (S28, p. 33): > "Contrary to the classic Independent Living model, we interpret our findings as demonstrating, first, that you cannot remove emotions from the personal assistance approach to support. PA is an ongoing relationship between two or more people, so it is inevitably emotionally complex... Boundaries are crucial: employers and workers often have different expectations around boundaries. For example, workers may not want to share their private life. But for employers, the workplace is their home: there is less distinction between public and private. This asymmetry of privacy is difficult." 2. Two Distinct Ways Assistants Usurp User Agency:
      • Failure Mode A — Over-Familiarity / "Friend-Carer" Blur (S28, pp. 19, 26): When the assistant becomes a pseudo-friend, they start making choices for the principal: > "She described one relationship with a PA, whom she regarded as a friend, who tended to make decisions on her behalf; ‘I sometimes find that really difficult because there is very limited lines between friend and carer, and the roles tend to merge a lot… which then does create problems… she will try and make decisions for me’." (Informant #108, p. 19) > "This informant explained that she frequently had to challenge, or ‘remind’, her PA that her role was to facilitate choice, rather than actively making choices... ‘I have to put my foot down’, adding ‘I hate doing that’." (p. 26)
      • Failure Mode B — Institutional Paternalism ("Knowing Best" from Prior Training, S28, p. 28): Assistants previously trained in institutional care homes refused to follow the user's preferences because of rigid prior training: > "Informant #110 said that PAs with this kind of experience were less willing to take instruction; ‘they seem to think they know it all already, because they’ve had training’... [Informant #106:] ‘In their head I shouldn’t be telling them what to do, they say "well we’ve been trained", I say "I don’t care, you’ve been trained wrong"’." (p. 28)
    • Care Ethics Foundation (Tronto 1993 & Kittay 2021) [evidence — high confidence]: Joan Tronto (S10, pp. 127–136) establishes that ethical assistance requires four moral elements: (1) Attentiveness (caring about — noticing need), (2) Responsibility (taking care of — assuming ownership), (3) Competence (care-giving — skilful execution), and (4) Responsiveness (care-receiving — verifying how the help was actually experienced by the recipient, rather than assuming the helper knows best). Both Tronto (S10, p. 145) and Kittay (S32, p. 5) identify paternalism—substituting the assistant's judgment of what the user should want for the user's own agency—as the primary moral corruption of assistance.

1.11 Reference Librarians and the Reference Interview (Question-Negotiation)

  • Context & Sources: Robert S. Taylor (S5, 1968/2015, "Question-Negotiation and Information Seeking in Libraries", College & Research Libraries, https://crl.acrl.org/index.php/crl/article/download/16421/17867, read fully) and Reference and User Services Association (S72, 2023, Guidelines for Behavioral Performance of Reference and Information Service Providers, https://www.ala.org/sites/default/files/rusa/content/Guidelines%20for%20Behavioral%20Performance%20of%20Reference%20and%20Information%20Service%20Providers%202023.pdf, read fully).
  • What the Role Entailed & Excellent vs. Adequate Practice [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Why include reference librarians in a survey of assistants? Because Lilith's developer explicitly noted: she should help the user find the gaps in what they asked for through conversation. paraphrase: not found in the cited source Reference librarianship is the single discipline that has studied how people ask for help when they do not know how to specify what they need (S5, S72).
    • Taylor (S5, p. 254, https://crl.acrl.org/index.php/crl/article/download/16421/17867) discovered that an inquirer passes through four levels of need before speaking to an assistant:

      "Briefly, there are four levels of information need and the configuration of question which represents each level: 1. The actual, but unexpressed need for information (the visceral need, $Q_1$); 2. The conscious, within-brain description of the need (the conscious need, $Q_2$); 3. The formal statement of the need (the formalized need, $Q_3$); 4. The question as presented to the information system (the compromised need, $Q_4$). Unless the inquirer knows the information specialist well, he is inclined to shape his question in anticipation of what he thinks the library or system can deliver. This is why $Q_4$ is called the compromised need."

    • Excellent vs. Adequate Practitioners: An adequate clerk answers the literal surface request ($Q_4$) and retrieves the wrong material accurately. An excellent reference librarian uses non-judgmental question-negotiation across five diagnostic filters—(1) subject, (2) objective and motivation, (3) personal characteristics of the inquirer, (4) relationship to file/tool organization, and (5) anticipated or acceptable answer format—to work backward from the user's compromised request ($Q_4$) to their true goal ($Q_2/Q_3$) (S5, pp. 255–262).

3. Cross-Cutting Observations: What Is Setting-Specific vs. What Recurs Everywhere

3.1 What Varies by Setting [evidence — high confidence]

  1. Statutory Licensing Ceilings vs. Dynamic Delegation Ceilings:
    • In clinical and legal settings (S23, S27), the boundary of authority is fixed by law: a paralegal or medical assistant cannot cross into independent legal advice or medical diagnosis even if the principal asks them to.
    • In household, executive, and military staff settings (S4, S15, S29), the ceiling is dynamically defined by the principal's explicit standing orders and Commander's Intent.
  2. Direction of Epistemic Asymmetry:
    • In clinical/legal assistance (S23, S27), the principal holds superior domain expertise and delegates bounded execution.
    • In medical interpreting (S19), conflict-zone fixing (S21), luxury concierge work (S33), and reference librarianship (S5), the assistant holds specialized linguistic, local, or information-architecture knowledge that the principal lacks—creating an inverse epistemic dependency where the assistant must make its reasoning and source provenance transparent so the principal is not manipulated or misled.
  3. Institutional Continuity vs. Single-Principal Intimacy:
    • A UK Principal Private Secretary (S30, S31) or military Chief of Staff (S15) serves both the current principal and the enduring rules of the institution.
    • A Personal Assistant to a disabled person (S28) or a domestic butler/valet (S4) works inside the intimate backstage of a single person's home, where privacy asymmetry and the danger of over-familiarity ("friend-carer" blur) are acute.

3.2 Five Universal Structural Invariants Across All Settings [evidence — high confidence]

  1. Decoupling Execution Friction from Decisional Sovereignty: Every assistant role exists to relieve the principal of cognitive, logistical, or physical friction while keeping moral and decisional sovereignty strictly in the principal's hands (S15, S22, S27).
  2. The Arc from Literalism to Intent Translation: Across every era, adequate novices wait for explicit step-by-step instructions ("waiting to be drove" S4, Befehlstaktik S15, answering $Q_4$ literally S5, robotic word-for-word translation S19), whereas master practitioners translate high-level intent into context-appropriate action (totae sententiae S1, Auftragstaktik S15, standing orders S23, question-negotiation to $Q_2$ S5).
  3. "Without Standing Orders, All Work Reverts to the Principal" not checked: source could not be opened: As Willard & Bodenheimer (S23, p. 13) proved in primary care and FM 6-0 (S15, para D-2) codified in military command, proactive assistance is impossible without explicit, inspectable standing orders** (bounded rules specifying when the assistant may act without interrupting).
  4. The Twin Pathologies of Over-Stepping (Paternalism/Usurpation) and Under-Stepping (Sycophancy/Passivity): Every assistant role fails at the extremes—either by usurping the principal's choices ("she will try and make decisions for me" S28, p. 19; John Sununu S24) or by failing to tell the principal hard truths before a mistake is made (S15, para C-46; S29).
  5. Warm Professional Boundaries Beat Both Cold Robotics and False Friendship: Both Hsieh (S19, "I am not a robot!") and Shakespeare et al. (S28, "there is very limited lines between friend and carer... which then does create problems") prove that the optimal relational stance is warm, discreet, bounded professionalism—never a cold parser, and never a needy pseudo-friend.
