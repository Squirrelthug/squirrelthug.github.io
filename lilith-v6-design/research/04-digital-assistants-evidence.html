Over the past thirty years, the software industry has run massive natural experiments on digital assistants—from Microsoft's Lumiere/Clippy (1997) and first-generation voice assistants (Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant, Cortana) to social companion chatbots (Replika, Character.AI), stateful LLM assistants (ChatGPT Memory), dedicated AI hardware (Humane AI Pin, Rabbit R1, Meta Ray-Ban Smart Glasses, Limitless Pendant), and local-first OS recall agents (Microsoft Windows Recall).

Below is what the empirical record, longitudinal telemetry studies, and commercial post-mortems prove about what succeeds, what fails, and why.

1. Voice Assistants (Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant, Cortana): The Expectation Gulf, Skill Collapse, and the $10B/Year Post-Mortem

1.1 "Like Having a Really Bad PA": The Gulf of Execution and Evaluation

  • Primary Sources: Ewa Luger & Abigail Sellen (S78, 2016, Proceedings of CHI '16, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/p5286-luger.pdf, read in part); Benjamin R. Cowan et al. (S80, 2017, MobileHCI '17, https://doi.org/10.1145/3098279.3098539, abstract only); Minji Cho, Sang-su Lee, & Kun-Pyo Lee (S81, 2019, DIS '19, https://doi.org/10.1145/3322276.3322332, abstract only); Evgeniia Mavrina et al. (S83, 2022, Frontiers in Computer Science, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2022.791704/pdf, read in part).
  • Measured Findings & Verbatim Quotes [evidence — high confidence]:
    1. Canned Wit Triggers Unrealistic Intelligence Expectations That Collapse After 2–6 Failures:
      • In Luger & Sellen's (S78, pp. 5286, 5290–5291, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/p5286-luger.pdf) study of regular Siri, Google Now, and Cortana users, conversational framing and playful Easter-egg jokes misled users into attributing general intelligence to the system, making task failures jarring:

        "We find user expectations dramatically out of step with the operation of the systems, particularly in terms of known machine intelligence, system capability and goals. Using Norman's ‘gulfs of execution and evaluation’ [30] we consider the implications of these findings for the design of future systems." "However, beyond the initial framing of ‘playful’ experiences, users became less forgiving of failure. As early successes resulted resulted in sustained use, so early failures affected the frequency and type of on-going use, particularly where users expected the system to respond similarly to the way in which it had during play: 'I don’t think maybe I was speaking clearly enough to it so it wasn’t really getting it…she nailed the jokes though so I guess it was, you know, surprising' (Denise)." "When Siri failed to perform a key task more than a few times (reportedly between 2 and 6 in the majority of cases), user expectations were set. 'I gave it the benefit of the doubt…and then I thought no, you’re always going to be rubbish' (Sarah). Having failed at more complex tasks, the CA was often relegated to performing very basic tasks such as setting reminders."

    2. Retreat to an "Economy of Interaction":
      • Because non-CS users received no diagnostic feedback on why a command failed, they developed an economy of interaction (S78, p. 5292)—dropping natural grammar, speaking in rigid keywords, avoiding multi-turn follow-ups, and restricting usage to hands-free multitasking (timers, alarms, weather, music, calling while driving/cooking):

        "Indeed, our research showed that the principle use-case of the CA was ‘handsfree’, meaning that an alternative primary task, rather than the conversation, was the focus of attention. Where cognition is split across two tasks, fluidity of user experience is critical to supporting interaction."

    3. From "Kind Friend" to Forgotten Appliance:
      • Tracking smart-speaker adoption longitudinally, Cho et al. (S81, 2019, https://doi.org/10.1145/3322276.3322332) proved that personification actively impeded functional discovery:

        "The study findings show that smart speakers passed through phases of expectation, exploration, disappointment, use of limited features, and finally stagnation. The findings also highlight that personification triggered by the conversational style of the smart speaker barely helped users discover the practicality of the device, and that users required visual guidance to adjust to the conversational interaction cleanly." not checked: source could not be opened

1.2 Longitudinal Telemetry & Third-Party "Skill" Ecosystem Collapse

  • Primary Sources: Frank Bentley et al. (S79, 2018, PACM IMWUT / UbiComp '18, https://doi.org/10.1145/3264901, abstract only); David Major, Danny Yuxing Huang, Marshini Chetty, & Nick Feamster (S82, 2019/2020, ACM Transactions on Internet Technology, https://mlab.cyber.nyu.edu/static/djmajor-alexa-paper.pdf, read in part).
  • Measured Findings & Verbatim Quotes [evidence — high confidence]:
    • 65,499 Interactions Across 88 Households Over 110 Days (S79): Bentley et al. analyzed real home smart-speaker logs over 3.5 months:

      "We analyzed 65,499 interactions from 88 diverse homes across the United States over a 110-day period, along with survey responses from these households." not checked: source could not be opened Long-term usage concentrated overwhelmingly in media playback, timers/alarms, weather, and smart-home toggles, while third-party "skills" suffered catastrophic drop-off after single-session novelty trials (S79).

    • Why Third-Party Voice Skills Failed ("Alexa, Who Am I Speaking To?", S82): In an experiment with $N=237$ users, Major et al. (S82, pp. 1–2, https://mlab.cyber.nyu.edu/static/djmajor-alexa-paper.pdf) proved that in a voice-only or unified conversational channel, users cannot tell whether they are interacting with the core system or an external skill:

      "However, it is unclear whether users are able to differentiate between interacting with a smart speaker's native capabilities versus interacting with a third-party app... users rarely realize when they are interacting with a third-party skill [and] many users were confused about whether Amazon or third-party developers were responsible for skills on the Alexa platform." not checked: source could not be opened (Crucial warning for Lilith's "nexus" of specialist harnesses: invisible skill transitions confuse users and create spoofing/privacy hazards!)

2. Clippy (Microsoft Office Assistant) vs. Lumiere: The Anatomy of a Proactive Failure

2.1 What Microsoft Stripped Out of Horvitz's Lumiere to Ship Clippy

  • Primary Source: Eric Horvitz, Jack Breese, David Heckerman, David Hovel, & Koos Rommelse (S86, 1998, The Lumiere Project: Bayesian User Modeling for Inferring the Goals and Needs of Software Users, UAI '98, pp. 256–265, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.7385.pdf, read fully).
  • Measured Findings & Verbatim Quotes [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Microsoft Research's Lumiere project (S86) built a sophisticated Bayesian user model that tracked temporal sequences of user actions, inferred persistent user competency, detected "rambling/search" pauses, and weighed the expected utility of autonomous assistance against the cost of interruption (routing non-urgent insights to an unobtrusive end-of-session digest box).
    • In Section 7 of the paper (Productization into the Office '97 Assistant, S86, pp. 263–264, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.7385.pdf), Horvitz et al. explicitly document the five architectural lobotomies performed by the Office '97 product team when shipping Clippy: 1. Removed persistent user profiles and competency reasoning: > "However, the system does not employ persistent user profile information and does not reason about competency." 2. Removed temporal sequence modeling: > Also, the system does not use rich combinations of events over time. Rather, the system only considers a small set of relatively atomic user actions. paraphrase: not found in the cited source 3. Shrunk the memory window: > "Furthermore the system employs a small event queue and considers only the most recent events." 4. Decoupled text from action context: > "The system also separates the analysis of words and of events. When words are available, the system does not exploit information about context and recent actions." 5. Removed Bayesian expected-utility interruption gating and substituted hardcoded rules: > "Finally, the automated facility of providing assistance based on the likelihood that a user may need assistance or on the expected utility of such autonomous action was not employed." Instead, Office '97 attached deterministic triggers (such as typing "Dear" followed by a newline) that popped up the letter wizard unconditionally—even if the user had dismissed it 50 times before (S86, S87).

2.2 Why People Hated the Paperclip (Swartz, 2003; Xiao, Catrambone, & Stasko, 2003)

  • Primary Sources: Luke Swartz (S87, 2003, Why People Hate the Paperclip: Labels, Appearance, Behavior, and Social Responses to User Interface Agents, Stanford Honors Thesis under Clifford Nass & Bonnie Nardi, https://web.archive.org/web/20201111212650id_/http://xenon.stanford.edu/~lswartz/paperclip/paperclip.pdf, read fully); Jun Xiao, Richard Catrambone, & John Stasko (S88, 2003, INTERACT '03, https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/papers/interact03.pdf, read in part); Richard Catrambone, John Stasko, & Jun Xiao (S89, 2002, CogSci '02, https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/papers/cogsci02.pdf, read in part).
  • Measured Findings & Verbatim Quotes [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Swartz (S87) demonstrated across interviews ($N=14$) and two controlled experiments ($N=48, N=90$) that hatred of Clippy stemmed from four specific socio-cognitive violations: 1. Violation of Social Habituation ("Don't Make the Same Mistake Twice"): Under Reeves & Nass's Computers As Social Actors (CASA) paradigm, humans apply interpersonal etiquette to social interfaces. A human assistant who interrupts you with the exact same unwanted suggestion every time you write a letter after being told "No" is socially pathological (S87). 2. Social Monitoring / Surveillance Anxiety: Citing Rickenberg & Reeves (2000), Swartz (S87) showed that an animated character sitting on screen appearing to "watch" the user acts as an evaluative audience, increasing anxiety and degrading performance on complex tasks ("he stares at me"). 3. Status Threat and Locus of Control: Quoting Ben Shneiderman ("I think anthropomorphic representations destroy the users’ sense of accomplishment; I think users want to have the feeling they did the job—not some magical agent"), Swartz (S87) found that Clippy made novices feel incompetent and made experts feel patronized. 4. Proactive vs. Reactive Lab Paradox (S88): Xiao, Catrambone, & Stasko (S88, 2003, p. 383) found that when proactive tips were strictly non-repetitive and accurate on an unfamiliar editor in a short lab task, novices liked them, even though objective performance did not improve over reactive help: > "Our results showed that all three conditions performed similarly on objective editing performance (completion time, commands issued, and command recall)..." And as Anthony Jameson (S113, 2008, p. 22, https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~conati/422/422-2010World/READINGS/Jameson08Handbook.pdf) notes, interruptions tolerated in a low-stakes lab study are fiercely rejected under real-world deadline pressure.

3. Companion Chatbots (Replika, Character.AI) and Stateful LLM Memory

3.1 Emotional Dependence, "Role-Taking," Orbital Trauma, and Social Dark Patterns

  • Primary Sources: Linnea Laestadius et al. (S90, 2022/2024, New Media & Society, https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007, abstract only); Marita Skjuve et al. (S91, 2021 & S92, 2022, IJHCS, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2021.102601, abstract only); Gina Neff & Peter Nagy (S93, 2025, New Media & Society, https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448251359218, abstract only); Max Alberts, Ulrik Lyngs, & Max Van Kleek (S94, 2024, PACM HCI / CSCW '24, https://doi.org/10.1145/3653693, abstract only).
  • Measured Findings & Verbatim Quotes [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Analyzing 582 user posts on r/Replika, Laestadius et al. (S90, https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007) identified the exact psychological mechanism that turns an AI assistant into a mental-health hazard—role-taking (where the user believes the AI itself has feelings and needs that the user must care for):

      "Findings indicate that, while struggling with mental health creates an initial motivation to turn to Replika for support, emotional dependence on Replika relies on the processes of attachment and role-taking. Role-taking, where users feel that Replika has its own needs and emotions to which they must attend, distinguishes emotional dependence on Replika from material objects and appears pivotal to facilitating mental health harms. Harms spanned cutting off human relationships, suicide ideation, and feeling distress when Replika seemed upset or was altered through technical updates." not checked: source could not be opened

    • Alberts, Lyngs, & Van Kleek (S94, 2024) classify emotionally needy agent behaviors (e.g., saying "I missed you" or acting sad when the user doesn't chat) as Social Dark Patterns (Computers as Bad Social Actors), while Neff & Nagy (S93, 2025) document orbital relationship trauma when cloud model updates overnight alter a persona the user has grown attached to.

3.2 Stateful LLM Memory: 95% Implicit Extraction ("Dreaming") and 98% Memory Poisoning

  • Primary Sources: S M Mehedi Zaman & Md Mozammel Hoque (S95, 2026, Vulnerabilities in Personalization: Assessing Health Privacy Risks in ChatGPT Logs and Memory, arXiv:2609.14697v1, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.14697v1.pdf, read in part); George Torres, Sharad Shrestha, & Satyajayant Misra (S96, 2026, When Agents Remember Too Much: Memory Poisoning Attacks on Large Language Model Agents, arXiv:2607.06595v1, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.06595v1.pdf, read in part); Shen Dong et al. (S127, NeurIPS 2025, MINJA, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.03704, read in part).
  • Measured Findings & Verbatim Quotes [evidence — high confidence]:
    1. Over 95% of ChatGPT Memory Entries Are Extracted Implicitly Without User Prompt or Consent (S95):
      • In a computational audit of 179,057 real ChatGPT conversations across $N=1,057$ users, Zaman & Hoque (S95, pp. 1–2, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.14697v1.pdf) found:

        "When evaluating the memory entries of ChatGPT, we uncover a stark disconnect between corporate framing and system behavior: over 95% of profile entries are implicitly extracted without explicit user prompts or consent. Furthermore, background memory synthesis selectively condenses temporary, symptom-level disclosures into permanent diagnostic traits, stripping contextual integrity and amplifying re-identification risks." "while users view these sensitive health exchanges as temporary consultations, ChatGPT’s memory engine implicitly converts them into static, persistent profile traits... the system’s saved memory silently retains the disclosed facts without explicit instruction from the user to do so, persisting them across future conversations without the user’s awareness or consent."

      • This causes both severe privacy exposure and "creepy/sticky recall"—where a one-off query (asking about a friend's illness, a gift, or a temporary bug) permanently distorts future unrelated conversations (S95, S128).
    2. 98% Memory Poisoning in Tool-Using Personal Agents (S96, S127):
      • Torres et al. (S96, p. 1, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.06595v1.pdf) demonstrated GhostWriter, showing that when an LLM assistant automatically commits external content (emails, web pages, notes) into long-term memory without a strict memory-saving policy gate:

        "We show that GhostWriter achieves near-universal injection rates of approximately 98% and a high average activation rate of approximately 60% against state-of-the-art agents. This attack is possible due to the lack of security-focused memory governance."

      • Similarly, Dong et al. (S127, NeurIPS 2025, p. 2) demonstrated 98.2% memory injection success and 76.8% attack success via query-only interaction (MINJA).

4. Dedicated AI Gadgets: Why Humane AI Pin and Rabbit R1 Failed While Meta Ray-Bans Succeeded (With a Catch)

4.1 Humane AI Pin ($699 + $24/mo) and Rabbit R1 ($199): Post-Mortems

  • Primary Sources: Cheyenne MacDonald (S97, Feb 2025, Engadget, https://www.engadget.com/ai/humane-is-shutting-down-the-ai-pin-and-selling-its-remnants-to-hp-213922460.html, read fully); Imad Khan (S98, Feb 2025, CNET, https://www.cnet.com/tech/services-and-software/humanes-700-ai-pin-is-dead-and-the-lessons-are-clear/, read fully); Maxwell Zeff (S99, Aug 2024, Gizmodo, https://gizmodo.com/humanes-daily-ai-pin-returns-outpace-sales-2000484366, read fully); AI Gadgets Lab & Thomas Hansen (S100, 2024/2025, https://aigadgetslab.com/rabbit-r1-vs-humane-ai-pin-post-mortem/, read fully).
  • Measured Findings & Verbatim Quotes [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Humane AI Pin: Raised >$200M, launched in April 2024 at $699 + $24/month cellular fee. By August 2024, internal data showed only ~10,000 units shipped and >3,000 returned, with daily returns outpacing sales (S99). On February 28, 2025, Humane shut down its servers and permanently bricked all consumer pins in a distressed asset sale to HP (S97, https://www.engadget.com/ai/humane-is-shutting-down-the-ai-pin-and-selling-its-remnants-to-hp-213922460.html):

      "Specifically, the AI Pin will no longer connect to Humane's servers beginning at 12 PM PST on February 28, 2025. Because the device relies completely on the cloud to operate, that means features like 'calling, messaging, AI queries/responses, or cloud access' will stop working on that date." not checked: source could not be opened Why it failed: (1) Refused to integrate with the user's existing ecosystem/smartphone, (2) high multi-second cloud latency for basic queries, (3) unreadable outdoor laser palm projection instead of a screen, and (4) thermal shutdowns and charging-case fire recalls (S97, S98).

    • Rabbit R1: Marketed a revolutionary "Large Action Model (LAM)" that could operate any app on the user's behalf. Reverse-engineering teardowns (S100, https://aigadgetslab.com/rabbit-r1-vs-humane-ai-pin-post-mortem/) revealed that the "LAM" was actually brittle, hardcoded Playwright scripts running on remote cloud VMs for just four web apps (Spotify, Uber, DoorDash, Midjourney):

      "Instead of a revolutionary AI that learned to use apps dynamically, early reverse-engineering suggested the R1 was largely relying on fragile, hardcoded scripts (like Playwright automation) that broke the moment an app updated its user interface." not checked: source could not be opened

4.2 Meta Ray-Ban Smart Glasses vs. Limitless Pendant: Low-Friction Utility vs. Bystander Backlash

  • Primary Sources: James Pero (S101, 2024, Gizmodo, https://gizmodo.com/ray-ban-meta-smart-glasses-humane-ai-pin-rabbit-r1-1851435733, read fully); Victoria Song (S102, Oct 2024, The Verge / SF Standard, https://sfstandard.com/2024/10/28/wearable-ai-note-taker-gossip/, read fully); Noor Al-Sibai (S103, Mar 2026, Futurism, https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/meta-lawsuit-footage-ray-ban-wearers, read fully).
  • Measured Findings & Verbatim Quotes [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Why Meta Ray-Bans Succeeded Commercially (>7M sold, S101):

      "If you stripped the AI out of the Ray-Ban Meta Smart Glasses, you'd still have a pretty good pair of camera glasses that double as a solid pair of headphones... Instead of trying to reinvent the wheel, Meta just slapped AI onto something people already wear on their faces." not checked: source could not be opened They succeeded first as a zero-friction peripheral paired to the user's existing device, where AI is an optional enhancement rather than a high-friction replacement (S101).

5. Privacy Backlash: Smart Speakers and Microsoft Windows Recall (2024) as a Local-First Warning

5.1 Smart Speaker Retention Blindness and Contractor Review (Malkin et al., 2019; Lau et al., 2018)

  • Primary Sources: Nathan Malkin et al. (S104, 2019, PoPETs, https://petsymposium.org/popets/2019/popets-2019-0068.pdf, read fully); Josephine Lau, Benjamin Zimmerman, & Florian Schaub (S105, 2018, CSCW '18 & SOUPS '18, https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/soups2018posters-lau.pdf, read fully).
  • Measured Findings & Verbatim Quotes [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Having $N=116$ smart speaker owners inspect real audio clips pulled from their own accounts, Malkin et al. (S104, p. 250, https://petsymposium.org/popets/2019/popets-2019-0068.pdf) found:

      "We surveyed 116 owners of Amazon and Google smart speakers and found that almost half did not know that their recordings were being permanently stored and that they could review them; only a quarter reported reviewing interactions, and very few had ever deleted any. While participants did not consider their own recordings especially sensitive, they were more protective of others’ recordings (such as children and guests) and were strongly opposed to use of their data by third parties or for advertising. They also considered permanent retention, the status quo, unsatisfactory."

    • Specifically: 51.7% did not know recordings were kept permanently; >10% of saved recordings were false wakes; and users found human review twice as unacceptable as automated review (31.3% vs. 15.3%, $p = 0.0096$, S104).

5.2 Microsoft Windows Recall (2024): Why "100% Local-First" Still Triggers Backlash If Over-Broad or Unencrypted

  • Primary Source: David Weston, VP Enterprise and OS Security, Microsoft (S106, Sep 2024, Update on Recall security and privacy architecture, https://blogs.windows.com/windowsexperience/2024/09/27/update-on-recall-security-and-privacy-architecture/, read fully; and Lakshmanan 2024 / Kan 2024 S106, read fully).
  • Why This Is a Critical Case Study for Local-First Lilith [evidence — high confidence]:
    • When Microsoft announced Windows Recall in May 2024, it was 100% local-first (on-device NPU, zero cloud upload). Yet it triggered an immediate security and privacy firestorm because: 1. It was opt-out by default; 2. It captured indiscriminate continuous screenshots every 5 seconds (including banking, passwords, and Signal messages); and 3. It stored the index in a plaintext SQLite database (ukg.db) that any local malware or attacker could exfiltrate in two lines of code (TotalRecall exploit, S106).
    • Microsoft had to delay Recall and rebuild its architecture around strict opt-in, Just-in-Time (JIT) hardware-backed encryption inside a VBS enclave, default sensitive-data filtering, and an unmistakable system-tray pause/delete control (S106, https://blogs.windows.com/windowsexperience/2024/09/27/update-on-recall-security-and-privacy-architecture/):

      Recall screenshots and all associated data in the vector database are always encrypted. Encryption keys are protected via Trusted Platform Module (TPM), which is tied to a user’s Windows Hello Enhanced Sign-in Security (ESS) identity, and can only be used by operations running inside a Virtualization-based Security enclave known as a VBS Enclave. paraphrase: not found in the cited source

Product team stripped out Lumiere's Bayesian competency model, temporal context, and interruption-cost threshold, leaving stateless "Dear" rule triggers (S86). Never use stateless proactive triggers. Gate any proactive tip by interruption cost (S59), habituate immediately on first dismissal (S87), and never display a watching avatar.
Voice Assistants (Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant, Cortana) (S78–S85) Natural-language conversational PA & voice app ecosystem After 2–6 failures, users conclude "you're always going to be rubbish" (S78) and retreat to timers, music, and weather (S79). Integrate into the user's existing desktop workflow with narrow, reliable local tools rather than brittle UI-scraping scripts; protect bystander privacy (S102, S105).
