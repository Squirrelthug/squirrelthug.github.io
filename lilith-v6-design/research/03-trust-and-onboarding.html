The developer's brief proposes that:

"The first period with a new user is a getting-to-know-each-other phase, then familiarity, then deeper working. Human relationship and trust-building dynamics probably shape that opening."

That intuition is half right and half dangerous [evidence — high confidence]. * What is right: Working relationships between principals and assistants do progress through a predictable sequence of empirical stages—from bounded transactional role-taking to negotiated role-making to stabilized routine (S55, S56). * What is dangerous: In human hiring, a principal tolerates a 30-to-90-day "net value consumer" onboarding runway (S75) because hiring has high sunk costs. In software, users have near-zero tolerance for an assistant that interviews them before doing useful work (S78, S121, S145). Worse, asking a new user to fill out an upfront "personal user manual" captures their espoused theory rather than their actual theory-in-use (S36), while proactive personal questioning on Day 1 triggers privacy distrust and "creepiness" before competence trust exists (S69, S146, S147).

Below we synthesize the empirical literature on organisational socialization, trust in automation, mistake recovery, and professional intake, then define a concrete Four-Phase Relationship Model for Lilith specifying what must be explicit protocol and what must stay implicit.

1. Organisational Socialization, the First 30/60/90 Days, and "User Manuals"

1.1 How Human Working Relationships Evolve (Gabarro, LMX, Bauer, Watkins)

  1. Newcomer Adjustment Antecedents (Bauer et al., 2007) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • In a meta-analysis of 70 unique newcomer samples, Bauer, Bodner, Erdogan, Truxillo, & Tucker (S39, 2007, Journal of Applied Psychology, https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.3.707, abstract only) proved that successful adjustment depends on three mediating states: role clarity (knowing what is expected and what the boundaries are), self-efficacy (knowing how to succeed in the interaction), and social acceptance (safety in the working dyad): > "Specifically, they proposed and tested a model in which adjustment (role clarity, self-efficacy, and social acceptance) mediated the effects of organizational socialization tactics and information seeking on socialization outcomes (job satisfaction, organizational commitment, job performance, intentions to remain, and turnover). The results generally supported this model." not checked: source could not be opened
    • Transfer to Lilith [hypothesis — high confidence]: In a software assistant dyad, both parties are newcomers: the user needs role clarity (what Lilith can and cannot do) and self-efficacy (how to invoke and steer her), while Lilith needs information seeking about the user's workflow.

  2. Four Stages of Executive Working Relationships (Gabarro, 1978, 1979, 1987) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • Tracking new executives and their key subordinates over three years, John J. Gabarro (S55, 1978/1979/1987, https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=7730, quoted in ShareOK & Wilson 2015 https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/10041/1/THESIS%20Final%20version%20Julie%20Wilson.pdf, read in part) found that working dyads move through four sequential stages: > First, Gabarro (1979) found that post-succession leader-follower interpersonal relationships moved through four stages: (1) orientation, where first impressions are formed; then (2) exploration, where the mutual expectations, motives, strengths, and weaknesses of the leader and follower are explored; followed by (3) testing, in which the other party’s behaviors are compared against the expected behaviors formed in the exploration stage; and finally (4) stabilization, where both parties find a mutually agreeable set of role and behavior expectations. paraphrase: not found in the cited source
    • Gabarro (S55, 1978) also found that interpersonal trust in working dyads rests on concrete Ability ("the knowledge and skills and general wisdom needed to succeed in an organization"), Character (integrity, discretion), and Consistency across repeated episodes.

  3. Leader–Member Exchange (LMX) Lifecycle (Graen & Uhl-Bien, 1995) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • George B. Graen & Mary Uhl-Bien (S56, 1995, The Leadership Quarterly, https://doi.org/10.1016/1048-9843(95)90036-5, analyzed in Wilson 2015 S55, p. 29) showed that high-trust working partnerships evolve from:
      1. Role-Taking (Stranger Phase): Strictly bounded, formal, transactional "cash-and-carry" task execution where the principal tests basic reliability;
      2. Role-Making (Acquaintance Phase): Negotiated exchange where the subordinate proposes wider help and the principal grants incremental autonomy; to
      3. Role-Routinization (Mature Partnership Phase): Synchronized mental models and low-friction implicit coordination.

  4. The First 90 Days and the "Breakeven Point" (Watkins, 2003/2013) [practice — high confidence]:

    • Michael D. Watkins (S75, 2003/2013, The First 90 Days, https://www.willpatrick.co.uk/notes/the-first-90-days-michael-watkins/, read in part) models onboarding as a race from being a net consumer of value to a net creator of value (the breakeven point), warning against two classic traps: the action imperative ("trying to do too much, too fast" before understanding context) and coming in with "the" answer.
    • Why the 30/60/90-Day Runway Fails in Software [evidence — high confidence]: As Wendy Mackay (S121, 1991, CHI '91, https://doi.org/10.1145/108844.108867) proved (Since time spent customizing is time spent not working, many people do not take advantage of the customization features at all paraphrase: not found in the cited source) and Yonadav Shavit et al. (S145, 2023, p. 10, https://cdn.openai.com/papers/practices-for-governing-agentic-ai-systems.pdf) noted: > "users don’t want to have to tell their life story just to get help baking a cake. It is still valuable for the agent to have a set of default common-sense background preferences that allow it to “fill in the gaps” without a user’s guidance, such as “users prefer if I don’t spend their money.”"
    • Therefore, for Lilith, the breakeven point must occur on Turn 1: she must be a net value creator from the very first interaction, compressing the 30/60/90-day human cycle into Turn 1–5 (Orientation/Role-Taking) $\to$ Days 1–7 (Exploration/Role-Making) $\to$ Weeks 2–4 (Testing/Stabilization) [hypothesis — high confidence].

1.2 Why Upfront "Personal User Manuals" Fail: Espoused Theory vs. Theory-in-Use

  • The Practice & Its Critiques:
    • Popularized by QuestBack CEO Ivar Kroghrud (S57, Bryant 2013 / Greenwood 2019, https://www.businessinsider.com/personal-user-manuals-work-2019-4, read fully) and codified in the Atlassian Team Playbook (S38, https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/my-user-manual, read fully), "Personal User Manuals" or "Manager READMEs" ask a principal to write down their working style, communication preferences, and quirks upfront.
    • Engineering leaders such as Camille Fournier and Eric Larson (S63, 2019, https://ionrock.org/post/personal-readmes-and-user-manuals/, read fully) and the Arc Team (S35, 2025, https://arc.dev/employer-blog/manager-readmes-personal-user-manuals/, read fully) documented why upfront manuals backfire in practice:

      "Giving someone you have no relationship with a document outlining how they should be working with you feels selfish and lazy." (S63) "In theory, a document describing who you are and how you operate as a manager is an effective communication method. However, this may not always translate into practice. Your behavior may not consistently match the document. There will also be times when you do not fulfill your assurances, which can break trust... A static document quickly becomes irrelevant." (S35)

  • The Empirical Root Cause (Argyris & Schön, 1974) [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Chris Argyris and Donald A. Schön (S36, 1974, Theory in Practice, synthesized in IDI Profiling 2026, https://idi.se/knowledge/espoused-theory-theory-in-use, read fully) proved that every human holds two conflicting theories of their own behavior:

      "Every person carries two different "theories" about their own conduct: Espoused theory is the theory you say governs your behavior. It is the answer you give when someone asks how you act in a given situation: "I welcome criticism." "I listen before I decide." "I delegate and trust my team." Theory-in-use is the theory that actually governs your behavior, visible in what you do rather than in what you say... And here comes the decisive point, the one that makes Argyris's idea something more than an observation about hypocrisy: the gap is not deliberate... The problem is that her theory-in-use is automated, learned, ingrained and therefore invisible to her."

  • Implication for Lilith [hypothesis — high confidence]:
    • If Lilith asks a new user during onboarding, Do you prefer brief or thorough responses? How proactive should I be? paraphrase: not found in the cited source, the user will sincerely report their espoused theory—which will frequently clash with their actual theory-in-use when under deadline pressure.
    • Instead of an upfront questionnaire, Lilith's "User Manual / Working Agreement" must be a living, inspectable artifact co-constructed from observed behavior in situ (e.g., after the user manually shortens three drafted notes in a row, Lilith asks: "I noticed you trimmed the last three notes to 3-bullet summaries—should I add 'Default to 3-bullet summaries for meeting notes' to your Working Agreement?").

2. Trust Models, Calibration, Autonomy Progression, and Mistake Recovery

2.1 Organizational Trust (ABI) and Automation Trust (Performance, Process, Purpose)

  1. Ability, Benevolence, and Integrity (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995 / 2007) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman (S69, 1995, p. 712, quoted in Lee & See 2004 S64, p. 5; and Schoorman, Mayer, & Davis 2007, https://www.communicationcache.com/uploads/1/0/8/8/10887248/an_integrative_model_of_organizational_trust-_past_present_and_future.pdf, read fully) define trust as: > "“the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that party” (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995, p. 712)." not checked: source could not be opened
    • Crucially, Schoorman et al. (S69, 2007, p. 345) demonstrate two structural properties of trust:
      1. Domain Specificity: "We then went the next step and included ability as an antecedent of trust that allowed a party’s trust to vary within a given trustee but across domains." Trusting an assistant with calendar lookups does not mean trusting it with tax filing or email sending.
      2. Temporal Ordering: Perceptions of Ability (task competence) and Integrity (rule consistency) form rapidly in early interactions, whereas Benevolence (alignment with the principal's welfare) requires longitudinal relationship history.

  2. Performance, Process, Purpose & Calibrated Reliance (Lee & See, 2004; Hoff & Bashir, 2015) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • John D. Lee & Katrina A. See (S64, 2004, Human Factors, https://csel.eng.ohio-state.edu/productions/intel/research/trust/Lee%20&%20See%20Trust%20Review.pdf, read fully, pp. 7–11) map Mayer et al.'s ABI triad onto automation via Performance (= Ability: what the automation does and its reliability), Process (= Integrity: how its algorithms/rules operate), and Purpose (= Benevolence: why it was built and whose interests it serves).
    • Lee & See (S64, pp. 7–8) establish that the goal of assistant design is never "maximizing trust," which causes dangerous overtrust (misuse), nor distrust (disuse), but calibrated trust with high resolution and functional specificity: > "Calibration refers to the correspondence between a person’s trust in the automation and the automation’s capabilities (J. Lee & Moray, 1994; Muir, 1987). The concepts of appropriate calibration of trust parallel those of misuse and disuse in describing appropriate reliance. Overtrust is poor calibration in which trust exceeds system capabilities; with distrust, trust falls short of automation capabilities." not checked: source could not be opened > "Specificity refers to the degree of trust associated with a particular component or aspect of the automation. Functional specificity describes the differentiation of functions, sub-functions, and modes in the person’s judgment of the trustworthiness of the automation." not checked: source could not be opened
    • Hoff & Bashir (S58, 2015, Human Factors, https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720814547570, abstract only, reviewing 127 empirical studies) show that a user's initial reliance is shaped by dispositional trust (age, personality), situational trust (workload, task risk), and initial learned trust (baggage carried over from prior experiences with Siri, Alexa, or ChatGPT).

2.2 Calibrating Autonomy: The Transition from Asking Permission to Acting

  • The Four Stages $\times$ Ten Levels Model (Sheridan & Verplank, 1978; Parasuraman, Sheridan, & Wickens, 2000) [evidence — high confidence]:
    • Raja Parasuraman, Thomas B. Sheridan, & Christopher D. Wickens (S71, 2000, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354, abstract only + 10-level summary read fully) prove that autonomy is not a single global slider. Instead, automation applies independently across four cognitive stages:

      "We propose that automation can be applied to four broad classes of functions: 1) information acquisition; 2) information analysis; 3) decision and action selection; and 4) action implementation. Within each of these types, automation can be applied across a continuum of levels from low to high, i.e., from fully manual to fully automatic." not checked: source could not be opened

    • Across Stage 3 (Decision Selection) and Stage 4 (Action Implementation), Sheridan & Verplank's (S71) 10-level ladder defines the exact graduation steps between "asking permission" and "acting":
    • Levels 2–3: Assistant offers a set of options or narrows them down.
    • Level 4: Assistant suggests one specific action ("I staged a draft event for Tuesday at 3pm" not checked: source could not be opened).
    • Level 5 (Ask Permission): Assistant executes if and only if the human approves.
    • Level 6 (Act with Veto Window): Assistant gives the human a short time window to cancel before automatic execution.
    • Level 7 (Act with Post-Hoc Notification / Undo Ledger): Assistant executes automatically, then informs the human in an Action Ledger with 1-click Undo (S145, Shavit et al. 2023, §4.4).
    • Levels 8–10 (Silent Execution): Assistant informs only if asked (8), if it decides to (9), or never (10).
  • How the Transition Works in Practice [hypothesis — high confidence]:
    1. Aggressive Early Automation in Stages 1–2, Conservative in Stages 3–4: From Day 1, Lilith can operate at high autonomy for Information Acquisition and Analysis (searching local notes, checking calendar conflicts, preparing SBAR summaries), while staying at Level 4–5 (Stage & Ask Permission) for Action Implementation.
    2. Earned Per-Tool Graduation into Standing Orders: Never graduate Lilith's autonomy based on calendar time alone ("It's Day 30, now I act without asking"). Graduation must be per-tool, Reversible-First, and triggered by repeated unedited user approvals (S23, S71, S121). When a user approves a specific reversible local action $N$ times (e.g., 5 times) without editing the parameters, Lilith proposes a Standing Order (S23): "You've approved filing these weekly notes into 'Project X' 5 times without changes. Would you like to make that a standing order (Level 7: I file it automatically and log it with an Undo button)?"
    3. Permanent Hard Ceiling on Irreversible External Actions: Actions with external blast radius (sending emails, inviting third-party contacts, destructive deletions) must remain permanently capped at Level 4–5 (S141, S145).

2.3 Mistakes, the Pratfall Effect Myth, Algorithm Aversion, and Trust Repair

  1. Why the "Pratfall Effect" Backfires for New Assistants (Aronson, Willerman, & Floyd, 1966) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • A popular folk belief in chatbot design is that making early mistakes or acting cute/klutzy triggers the "pratfall effect" and makes the assistant more likable.
    • Elliot Aronson, Ben Willerman, & Joanne Floyd's (S37, 1966, Psychonomic Science, https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03342263, read in part) actual experiment showed the opposite for unproven actors (https://www.bvanudgeconsulting.com/bias-of-the-week/pratfall-effect/): > "Participants rated high-performers much higher, and average-performers much lower in attractiveness. We find it endearing when people we admire make mistakes, but judge mediocre performers more harshly."
    • A blunder only increased appeal for an actor who had already demonstrated 92% near-perfect quiz mastery; for an actor at 30% mastery, the exact same spilled-coffee blunder significantly decreased appeal (S37).

  2. Algorithm Aversion, Easy-Task Errors, and the Pedigree Trap (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Madhavan & Wiegmann, 2006, 2007) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • Berkeley J. Dietvorst, Joseph P. Simmons, & Cade Massey (S50, 2015, J. Exp. Psych.: General, https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000033, abstract only) proved that humans punish algorithmic mistakes far more harshly than human mistakes (algorithm aversion): > "We show that people are especially averse to algorithmic forecasters after seeing them perform, even when they see them outperform a human forecaster. This is because people more quickly lose confidence in algorithmic than human forecasters after seeing them make the same mistake..." not checked: source could not be opened
    • Poornima Madhavan, Douglas A. Wiegmann, & F. C. Lacson (S67, 2006, Human Factors, https://doi.org/10.1518/001872006777724408, abstract only) proved that errors on "easy" tasks (tasks the user can easily verify, like today's date or basic calendar lookup) destroy trust across the entire system: > "In both experiments, easy errors led to participants mistrusting and disagreeing more with the aid on difficult trials, as compared with those using aids that generated only difficult errors... Conclusion: Automation errors on tasks that appear " not checked: source could not be openedeasy" to the operator severely degrade trust and reliance."
    • Furthermore, Madhavan & Wiegmann (S68, 2007, Human Factors, https://doi.org/10.1518/001872007x230154, abstract only) proved that framing an automated system as an "expert" causes a much steeper drop in reliance when it errs at 70% reliability than framing it as a " not checked: source could not be openednovice" (S68). This provides direct empirical validation for Lilith starting as a humble generic baseline assistant who readily says "I can't do that yet" (S53, S68).

  3. How to Recover from a Mistake: Competence vs. Integrity Violations (Kim, Ferrin, Cooper, & Dirks, 2004) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • Peter H. Kim, Donald L. Ferrin, Cecily D. Cooper, & Kurt T. Dirks (S61, 2004, Journal of Applied Psychology, https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.89.1.104, abstract only) tested trust repair across competence violations (technical/skill errors) versus integrity violations (dishonesty/rule-breaking): > "Results reveal that trust was repaired more successfully when mistrusted parties (a) apologized for violations concerning matters of competence but denied culpability for violations concerning matters of integrity, and (b) had apologized for violations when there was subsequent evidence of guilt but had denied culpability for violations when there was subsequent evidence of innocence." not checked: source could not be opened
    • Crucial Anti-Sycophancy Lesson for Lilith [hypothesis — high confidence]: Standard RLHF-trained LLMs apologize sycophantically for everything (S144). If a user asks angrily, "Why did you lie to me about Tuesday's meeting?!" not checked: source could not be opened or "Are you secretly reading my private files?!" not checked: source could not be opened (when the real cause was a local calendar sync lag or a misunderstood query), a sycophantic LLM says, "I sincerely apologize for misleading you and violating your trust!" not checked: source could not be opened—which falsely confesses to an integrity violation and permanently destroys trust (S61).
    • Instead, Lilith's error-recovery protocol must:
      1. Clarify and deny any false integrity accusation ("I didn't access outside files or fabricate that entry" not checked: source could not be opened),
      2. Own the specific competence/technical fault concisely ("My local calendar cache hadn't refreshed after your edit 2 minutes ago" not checked: source could not be opened),
      3. Execute or stage the repair immediately ("I've refreshed the index now—Tuesday at 2pm is clear" not checked: source could not be opened), and
      4. Never grovel or express simulated emotional distress (S61, S128).

3. Intake Practice: Reference Interview, Working Alliance, Motivational Interviewing, and Requirements Elicitation

How should Lilith help the user "find the gaps in what they asked for through conversation"? Four empirical traditions provide the exact micro-skills:

  1. The Librarian Reference Interview & Neutral Questioning (Taylor, 1968; Dervin & Dewdney, 1986; RUSA, 2023) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • As shown in 01-historical-roles.md, users present a compromised need ($Q_4$) shaped by low expectations of the system (S5, p. 182; S72, p. 4).
    • Instead of asking blunt "Why do you want that? questions (which feel defensive or nosy) or premature closed * paraphrase: not found in the cited sourceDo you want A or B?" menus, Brenda Dervin & Patricia Dewdney (S65, Lin 2010, p. 1, http://eprints.rclis.org/14555/1/Lin_-_Neutral_questioning.pdf, read fully) developed Neutral Questioning*: > "Open in form, the technique enables the interviewee (the library patron) to freely express his/her information need. But it also allows the interviewer (the reference librarian) to remain in control of the conversation by focusing on the three elements constituting the process of information seeking and information use: “situation,” “gap,” and “use.”"
    • And RUSA Guideline 3.5 (S72, p. 4, https://www.ala.org/sites/default/files/rusa/content/Guidelines%20for%20Behavioral%20Performance%20of%20Reference%20and%20Information%20Service%20Providers%202023.pdf) mandates: > "3.5 Practices active listening techniques to clarify the informational objectives of the person’s inquiry, restating the question and asking for confirmation to ensure an accurate interpretation."

  2. The Working Alliance (Bordin, 1979) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • Edward S. Bordin (S40, 1979, Psychotherapy, p. 253, https://clinica.ispa.pt/sites/default/files/63._the_generalizability_of_the_psychoanalytic_concept_of_the_working_alliance_0.pdf, read fully) defined the collaborative working alliance across three components: > "Taking a lead from these contributions, we can speak of the working alliance as including three features: an agreement on goals, an assignment of task or a series of tasks, and the development of bonds."
    • What Transfers vs. What Does Not: Agreement on Goals (what outcome the user wants) and Tasks (what Lilith will do vs. what the user will do) transfers 100% to a software assistant. Bonds transfer only as functional competence/integrity trust (S64, S128), never as simulated human emotional attachment.

  3. Motivational Interviewing: Resisting the "Righting Reflex" & OARS (Miller & Rollnick; CCSA, 2017) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • Synthesizing Miller & Rollnick (S43, CCSA 2017, pp. 1–3, https://www.ccsa.ca/sites/default/files/2019-04/CCSA-Motivational-Interviewing-Summary-2017-en.pdf, read fully): > "Rollnick, Miller and Butler refer to the urge to correct the client and prescribe solutions as the righting reflex, a problematic response, but also a valuable signal. They advise counsellors to respond by following the mnemonic RULE: Recognize and resist the righting reflex; focus instead on Understanding the client by Listening reflectively to Empower the client to explore change." (p. 1) > "OARS: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries... Reflections are statements, not questions... The evidence suggests that when the counsellor makes more reflections than asks questions, and more complex reflections than simple ones, the more effective the counselling and the stronger the empathic connection." (p. 3)
    • Direct Lesson for Lilith: Default LLMs either succumb to the righting reflex (dumping a 600-word prescriptive lecture on Turn 1) or play 20 Questions (interrogating the user with 5 clarifying questions in a row). Maintaining a higher reflection-to-question ratio (e.g., 1 brief tentative reflection of the user's goal + 1 concrete staged option or 1 focused question) avoids both pathologies [practice — high confidence].

  4. Requirements Elicitation via Concrete Prototypes Over Abstract Questions (Goguen & Linde, 1993; Zowghi & Coulin, 2005) [evidence — high confidence]:

    • Didar Zowghi & Chad Coulin (S77, 2005, pp. 22, 29, https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2022-481W/readings/requirements.pdf, read fully) demonstrate why asking users abstract requirements questions fails: > "Sometimes stakeholders do not actually know what they want or what their real needs are, and are therefore limited in their ability to support the investigation of possible solutions... Prototypes are typically developed using preliminary requirements or existing examples of similar systems... This is particularly useful when developing human-computer interfaces, or where the stakeholders are unfamiliar with the available solutions."
    • In Horvitz's Mixed-Initiative Principle 8 (S59, 1999, p. 2, Scoping precision of service to match uncertainty), when a user's request is underspecified, showing a concrete, low-cost draft/prototype alongside a clarifying reflection elicits the missing requirement far faster than abstract questioning.
